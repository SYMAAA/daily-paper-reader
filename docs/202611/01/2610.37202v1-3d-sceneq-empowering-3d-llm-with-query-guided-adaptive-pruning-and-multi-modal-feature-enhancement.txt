# 3D-SceneQ: Empowering 3D LLM With Query-Guided Adaptive Pruning and Multi-Modal Feature Enhancement 

Yongqi Shan, Lu Zhang, Member, IEEE, Jiazuo Yu, Yunzhi Zhuge, and Huchuan Lu, Fellow, IEEE 

Abstract —Current 3D scene understanding pipelines typically concatenate vast numbers of 3D object tokens with text tokens and feed the resulting sequence to a Large Language Model (LLM). However, existing 3D-LLMs for scene understanding encounter two major limitations: (i) excessive inclusion of task-irrelevant object data, introducing noise that reduces reasoning accuracy and increases hallucinations; and (ii) reliance solely on point cloud data, which inherently lacks the rich semantic information available in complementary 2D modalities, such as color, material properties, texture, and high-level contextual relationships. To address these challenges, we introduce 3D-SceneQ, a 3D LLM that unifies adaptive token pruning with multimodal semantic enrichment, markedly advancing scene un-derstanding, reasoning, and grounding. Specifically, we propose a Query-Guided Adaptive Pruning (QGAP) module that adaptively selects task-relevant objects based on the intended meaning of user instructions, leveraging a learnable latent query to retain critical information while effectively suppressing irrelevant noise. In addition, we introduce a Multi-modal Object-level Feature Enhancement (MOFE) module, which integrates 2D feature embeddings from pre-trained models into 3D representation to enrich object-level semantic information. Evaluations show that 3D-SceneQ outperforms current state-of-the-art methods across multiple benchmarks (e.g., +5.7 CIDEr on Scan2Cap and +17.0 EM@1 on SQA3D), demonstrating its remarkable capabilities in various 3D language reasoning tasks. 

Index Terms —Multi-modal Object-level Feature Enhancement, Query-Guided Adaptive Pruning, 3D Scene Understanding

In recent years, consid-erable progress has been made in Large Language Models (LLMs), extending their applications from traditional natural language processing to advanced multimodal tasks, including complex 3D scene understanding [5], [6]. Building upon these advancements, recent LLMs have demonstrated strong potential in 3D-related tasks. By integrating and analyzing rich spatial point cloud data, LLMs have demonstrated strong potential in a range of 3D-related tasks such as precise scene interpretation [7], robust object perception [8], and advanced spatial reasoning [9]. Such capabilities substantially enhance the contextual awareness and environmental comprehension of AI systems, ensuring more reliable and efficient performance in real-world applications. Therefore, continued exploration and development in this interdisciplinary field promise to fos-ter deeper and more meaningful interactions among humans, machines, and complex physical environments. Although these methods have achieved significant advance-ments, comprehensive 3D scene understanding remains hin-dered by two major challenges: (i) Numerous Object Token Noise : current 3D-LLMs [9], [10] typically employ 3D en-coders (e.g. PointNet [11] and PointNet++ [12]) to convert 3D scenes into a large number of object tokens, among which only a small subset is task-relevant, introducing noise and increasing the likelihood of hallucinations [13]. 2D Semantic Cues : existing models [8], [12] for 3D scene understanding primarily rely on point cloud data, which in-herently lacks rich semantic cues such as color, material, and high-level contextual relationships, all of which are typically provided by 2D visual features. However, most contemporary SOTA 3D LLMs, including Leo [3] and Point-LLM [14] (see Fig. 1(a)), ignore these two critical challenges and simply feed the LLM a concatenation of text and 3D object tokens, which limits the model’s ability to reason about spatial semantics and to attain a nuanced understanding of complex 3D scenes. To address these challenges, we introduce 3D-SceneQ, a novel 3D LLM designed to enhance scene perception, grounding, reasoning, and planning. As illustrated in Fig. 1(b), 3D-SceneQ comprises two lightweight components: Query-Guided Adaptive Pruning (QGAP) and Multi-modal Object-level Feature Enhancement (MOFE). First , QGAP tackles the challenge of task-relevant object selection by dynamically adjusting the number of objects retained based on the specific requirements of different questions. To achieve this adaptabil-ity, QGAP introduces a learnable latent query that adjusts the filtering ratio according to the question’s informational needs. By tailoring object selection to the task, QGAP reduces noise, mitigates hallucinations, and enhances the model’s effective-ness. Second , MOFE addresses the semantic limitations of point cloud data by incorporating 2D semantic features derived from multi-view scene images processed by pre-trained models such as CLIP [15]. Exploiting the camera intrinsics and extrinsics, MOFE projects multi-view 2D features onto the 3D space, pools the resulting point-wise embeddings into object-level embeddings, and integrates them with 3D tokens through cross-attention to produce a unified multi-modal representa-tion. Through object-level feature fusion, MOFE effectively integrates multi-modal information to enhance the semantic representation of task-relevant objects in 3D LLMs, enabling a richer and more comprehensive understanding of the scene. Unlike traditional multi-view stereo (MVS) methods [16], [17] that aim to reconstruct dense 3D geometry, or point-level contrastive approaches such as OpenScene [18], MOFE focuses on injecting language-aligned semantic features into object-level 3D representations, enabling deeper multi-modal fusion without relying on geometric reconstruction. Beyond their individual strengths, the synergistic integration of QGAP and MOFE gives rise to a virtuous cycle: QGAP filters out redundant clutter, sharpening the model’s focus on task-relevant objects, while MOFE enriches these objects with multi-modal semantics, which in turn reinforces the relevance signals that guide QGAP’s pruning. This synergy enables 3D-SceneQ to achieve a compact yet semantically rich understanding of complex 3D scenes, leading to consistently strong performance across diverse tasks. We evaluate 3D-SceneQ on a diverse set of 3D benchmarks, including 3D captioning [19], 3D question answering [20], and situated question answering [21]. Comprehensive experimental results show that 3D-SceneQ achieves state-of-the-art (SOTA) per-formance across all these tasks, achieving 78.1 CIDEr on Scan2Cap, 104.7 CIDEr on ScanQA, and 67.0 EM@1 on SQA3D, underscoring its strong generalization and reasoning capacity in complex 3D scenes. A. Point Cloud Understanding 

Understanding 3D scenes is essential for intelligent systems to interact with the physical world. Unlike 2D images, 3D point clouds capture rich spatial and geometric information, making them crucial for tasks such as object recognition, manipulation, and reasoning in applications like robotics and autonomous driving. Early advances in 3D deep learning [22] were led by architectures such as PointGT [23] and DT-Net [24], which laid the foundation for 3D representation learning by effectively processing raw point clouds for classifi-cation and segmentation tasks. Meanwhile, inspired by vision-language models like CLIP [15], there has been growing inter-est in aligning 3D point clouds with language. Methods such as PointCLIP [25] and CLIP2Point [26] project point clouds into 2D depth images for zero-shot classification using 2D CLIP models. Later works like ULIP [27] and OpenShape [28] employ contrastive learning to align 3D encoders with image and text through point-image-text triplets, improving the trans-ferability of 3D representations. Unfortunately, these methods are inherently constrained to object-level tasks and struggle to generalize to scene-level understanding. Recently, there has been growing interest in combining 3D point clouds with natural language to enable tasks [10], [19], [29]–[31]. 3D Question Answering [29] which focuses on answering questions grounded in 3D scenes, 3D Visual Grounding [30], [32] which evaluates object localization from text and 3D captioning [19] further tests a model’s ability to describe 3D scenes in natural language. However, most existing methods are designed for specific tasks and struggle to generalize beyond the distribution of their training data. In contrast, recent 3D Multi-modal Large Language Models (3D-LLMs) unify diverse 3D-language tasks within a single architecture, leveraging LLMs’ reasoning abilities to handle QA, grounding, captioning, and more complex tasks like 3D dialogue and task decomposition.

C. 3D Multi-Modal Large Language Models 

The recent success of multi-modal large language models (LLMs) has spurred growing interest in extending their capa-bilities to the 3D domain [3], [7], [8], [10], [14], [43]–[48]. Among these efforts, 3D-LLM [10] introduces 3D point cloud features into pre-trained 2D vision-language models (VLMs), enabling spatially grounded understanding across diverse 3D-language tasks. SegPoint [7] adopts a unified architecture that directly feeds point cloud data into LLMs for a wide range of segmentation tasks. Point-LLM [14] employs a two-stage training approach—first aligning 3D features with the lan-guage space, followed by instruction tuning—which facilitates stronger integration between geometric understanding and linguistic reasoning. In contrast, 3D-MiT [8] proposes a more streamlined framework by eliminating the explicit alignment stage, directly fusing visual features and textual prompts to construct multimodal 3D inputs with reduced training com-plexity. OneLLM [44] and Point-Bind LLM [45] further ex-plore the alignment of 3D point clouds with other modalities— including images, audio, and text—within a shared embedding space, thereby enhancing cross-modal reasoning and enabling seamless interaction between geometric and semantic cues. GPT4Point [46] extends this direction with a generative multi-modal model that supports 3D understanding and controllable output generation. LEO [3] represents a different line of work, focusing on embodied agents that integrate 3D point clouds and egocentric RGB images for manipulation tasks. However, in LEO, visual inputs are primarily used as low-level control signals, without integrating the high-level semantics of images 4

into a unified 3D representation. Despite these advancements, a fundamental challenge re-mains: point clouds provide rich geometric information but are inherently sparse in semantic content, and often contain sig-nificant structural redundancy. Most existing architectures do not explicitly model these characteristics, limiting their ability to fully leverage the expressive potential of 3D representations for downstream reasoning and decision-making tasks. III. M ETHODOLOGY 

A. 3D-SceneQ 

3D-SceneQ adopts a two-stage learning scheme to integrate multi-modal information for downstream tasks: (i) 3D Vision-Language Alignment, aligning 3D object features with lin-guistic representations to form a shared feature space; and (ii) Instruction Tuning, fine-tuning the model with task-specific instructions to improve 3D understanding and reasoning. In the first stage, we employ the object-centric 3D point cloud encoder, PointNet++ [12], to interpret the scene. This encoder extracts geometric features for each 3D object and represents them as point cloud embeddings. Spatial Transformer [49] is then applied after feature extraction to process the point cloud embeddings of all objects, transforming them into object-centric 3D object tokens. Subsequently, these 3D object tokens are projected through a linear layer to align their dimensionality with the requirements of the LLM, producing the final 3D object representation f3D ∈ RN ×d, where N

is the number of objects in the scene, and d is the feature dimensionality compatible with the LLM. The input in this stage includes system messages, user’s questions, and 3D object tokens f3D , forming a multi-modal sequence. This sequence is then processed by the pre-trained LLM to generate the final response. Text-based inputs, such as system messages and user’s questions, are tokenized using SentencePiece [50] with a 32k vocabulary. The second stage applies instruction tuning for 3D task, it builds upon the first stage but introduces additional mech-anisms to enhance task-specific performance. As shown in Fig. 2, the 3D features are refined through Query-Guided Adaptive Pruning (QGAP) and enriched using Multi-modal Object-level Feature Enhancement (MOFE). Details of QGAP and MOFE are provided in Section III-B and Section III-C, respectively. These two processes result in enhanced 3D object tokens fE3D ∈ RK×d, where K represents the number of objects remaining in the scene after pruning by QGAP. The input multi-modal sequence in this stage includes system messages, questions and enhanced 3D object tokens fE3D . As shown in Fig. 4, we extract dense, per-pixel embeddings from multi-view images and project them onto the 3D surface to integrate 2D image features with 3D point cloud data. A. Experimental Setups 

Benchmarks. Following [3], we utilise a diverse set of 3D vision-language datasets tailored for alignment and instruction tuning. In the 3D vision-language alignment phase, we train the model to bridge the gap between 3D scene representations and natural language using three types of captioning data. These include (i) object-level captions from Cap3D [53], which provide fine-grained textual descriptions of objects from 

Objaverse [54]; (ii) object-in-context referring expressions from ScanScribe [55] and ReferIt3D [31], further aug-mented with LLM-generated annotations to enhance contex-tual diversity; and (iii) scene-level summaries from 3RScan 

[56], capturing global information about object layouts, spatial relations, and scene semantics. In the instruction tuning phase, we refine the model’s ability to understand, reason, and re-spond to natural language instructions in 3D environments. We utilize tasks based on ScanNet [57] and 3RScan , covering open-ended 3D captioning and question answering, where the model is expected to describe scenes or answer queries about object properties, locations, and relationships. 

Evaluation Metrics. We use a set of standard evaluation metrics to assess model performance across various tasks. For language generation tasks, we report CIDEr, BLEU, METEOR, and ROUGE, which measure relevance, fluency, and overlap with reference texts from different perspectives. In open-ended generation tasks, where multiple correct answers may exist for a given input, we additionally use sentence sim-ilarity [58] to better capture semantic alignment. For question-answering tasks that require factual precision, we report top-1 exact match accuracy (EM@1), which reflects the proportion of predictions that exactly match the ground truth.

B. Comparison with State-of-the-Arts 

We evaluate our method across three prominent 3D scene understanding tasks: 3D scene captioning with Scan2Cap [19], 3D question answering (QA) with ScanQA [20], and 3D scene-based reasoning with SQA3D [21]. As summarized in Table III, 3D-SceneQ consistently achieves state-of-the-art performance on all these benchmarks, surpassing strong task-specific baselines as well as fine-tuned specialized mod-els across all evaluation metrics considered. Specifically, on Scan2Cap it lifts METEOR to 30.5 and ROUGE to 65.3, exceeding the previous best by 2.6 and 7.2 points, respec-tively; on ScanQA it pushes CIDEr to 104.7 and BLEU-4 to 13.6, reflecting more relevant and fluent answers; and on the SQA3D test set it attains an EM@1 of 67.0%, a striking 17 point leap over the strongest prior method, demonstrating exceptional proficiency in generating precise and contextually informative outputs. In contrast to conventional methods that depend on carefully engineered task-specific architectures, 3D-SceneQ adopts a unified LLM-based framework augmented with lightweight components—QGAP and MOFE. This uni-fied architecture allows the model to flexibly and effectively adapt to diverse 3D reasoning tasks, enabling robust open-ended response generation while maintaining highly competi-tive quantitative performance achieved by specialized methods. 7

Ablation Studies 1) Effectiveness of Core Components: We conducted ab-lation studies in Table IV to disentangle the individual and combined effects of 3D-SceneQ’s two core modules: Query-Guided Adaptive Pruning (QGAP) and the Multi-modal Object-Feature Enhancer (MOFE). Using the LEO [3] ar-chitecture with embodied agent related components removed as the Baseline model, we draw four nuanced observations: 

First, random pruning helps reduce redundancy but risks discarding crucial cues. The RandFilter variant stochastically discards 40% of object tokens whenever a scene contains more than 20 objects. This naïve compression alone propels SQA3D EM@1 from 50.0 to 68.9 by reducing extraneous information evidence that minimizing redundant information helps the model reason more effectively. Yet the indiscriminate pruning also removes salient objects and attributes, collapsing Scan2Cap CIDEr from 72.4 to 52.3 and diminishing ScanQA performance as well. The result exposes a core tension: token economy is vital, but pruning must be relevance-aware. 

Second, QGAP enhances both descriptive quality and reasoning accuracy. QGAP learns a filtering ratio over object tokens conditioned on the user’s question, excising only those entities that contribute negligibly to the downstream objective. This targeted sparsification raises Scan2Cap CIDEr to 78.4 and SQA3D EM@1 to 70.1, outperforming both the Baseline and RandFilter. The gains affirm that relevance-aware pruning pre-serves key geometric and semantic cues while still reaping the efficiency benefits of a lighter context. Third, MOFE boosts holistic performance, especially on ScanQA. By fusing geometric, visual, and linguistic signals into a refined object embedding, MOFE elevates Scan2Cap CIDEr to 77.8 and boosts SQA3D EM@1 to 66.7. The improvement on ScanQA is particularly pronounced, suggesting that MOFE’s cross-modal representations equip the model to resolve subtle spatial references and attribute dependencies that commonly confound 3D question answering. Finally, synergistic coupling of QGAP and MOFE yields the most balanced, robust gains. 

Our full model, 3D-SceneQ, combines QGAP’s task-relevant pruning strategy with MOFE’s semantic enrichment, produc-ing consistent improvements across all benchmarks (e.g., +5.7 CIDEr over Baseline on Scan2Cap, +20.0 EM@1 on SQA3D). Guided by the user query, QGAP selects task-relevant object tokens. MOFE then enriches these tokens with 2D features and feeds back sharper relevance cues to QGAP. This virtuous loop enables 3D-SceneQ to form a compact yet information-dense representation of complex 3D scenes, yielding balanced gains across diverse tasks.

To evaluate 3D-SceneQ’s effectiveness in mitigating hallu-cinations, we carried out a series of visual analyses (Fig. 6). The results indicate that our method markedly reduces hal-lucinations commonly afflicting 3D LLMs, from which we derive three main observations: First, 3D-SceneQ reduces hallucinations of irrelevant or nonexistent objects. Unlike the baseline, which invents items absent from the scene, such as a “dirty tower” (Fig. 7(a)), a “cutting board” (Fig. 7(b)), or a “cooking pot” (Fig. 7(c)), 3D-SceneQ always remains faithful to the scene content. This robustness stems from the collab-orative mechanisms of QGAP and MOFE. When the queried object is absent, QGAP adaptively reduces the pruning ratio, as the similarity between all object-category embeddings in the scene and the question embedding is low, thereby preventing forced matches. Meanwhile, MOFE enriches object under-standing with multi-view image features, offering stronger semantic cues. Together, they help 3D-SceneQ recognize in-valid queries and respond cautiously instead of hallucinating incorrect answers. Second, 3D-SceneQ substantially miti-gates perceptual errors in recognizing object attributes. 

Hallucinations are not limited to whole objects, they also distort geometry and material. In Fig. 7(d)–(f) the Baseline warps a square window into a triangle, morphs a round coffee table into a rectangle, and turns wooden cabinets metallic. In contrast, 3D-SceneQ consistently preserves accurate object properties, maintaining robust attribute recognition. Third, 3D-SceneQ excels at distinguishing objects with similar visual features. Objects with similar silhouettes or textures often confound vision-language models. The Baseline misla-bels a whiteboard as a bulletin board (Fig. 7(g)), and a stool as a trash can (Fig. 7(h)). 3D-SceneQ consistently delivers reliable responses, demonstrating an exceptional ability to dis-tinguish genuine scene elements from hallucinations. VI. C ONCLUSION 

In this paper, we present 3D-SceneQ, a novel 3D LLM framework designed to advance scene understanding, rea-soning, and planning within complex 3D environments. To tackle the challenges of redundant object interference and hallucination issues in 3D LLMs, we introduce QGAP, an adaptive pruning mechanism that selectively eliminates irrel-evant object tokens. Additionally, to overcome the semantic limitations inherent in point cloud data, we develop MOFE, a multi-modal feature integration module to overcome the semantic limitations of point cloud data by integrating 2D features to enhance the model’s comprehension of 3D scenes. Extensive experimental evaluations on benchmark tasks, in-cluding 3D captioning, question answering, and task planning, consistently demonstrate the superior performance of 3D-SceneQ compared to SOTA approaches. These results high-light the framework’s promising generalization capabilities and enhanced reasoning abilities in challenging 3D environments. Overall, 3D-SceneQ represents a significant advancement in various 3D understanding tasks, offering a strong foundation for future research and applications in intelligent interaction with complex 3D environments.
