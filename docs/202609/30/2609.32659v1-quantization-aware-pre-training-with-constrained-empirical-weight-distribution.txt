## ABSTRACT 

Quantization-Aware Pre-Training (QAPT) can increase the inference efficiency of DNNs, but a problematic behaviour known as rounding boundary weight oscilla-tion can introduce detrimental noise into the training process and significantly reduce convergence speed. While existing methods can reduce this detrimen-tal noise, they either introduce additional hyperparameters or memory overhead, or cannot consistently improve model accuracy. In this work, we propose op-timization with Constrained Empirical Weight dis Tribution (CEWT), the first hyperparameter-free memory-overhead-free oscillation suppression method that consistently improves QAPT performance: an optimizer post-update step that projects weights to the nearest point in weight space whose empirical distribu-tion (histogram) matches a zero-mean Gaussian. Our key insight is many quan-tizers are designed with the implicit assumption that the to-be-quantized data are permutations of samples from a zero-mean Gaussian, and this assumption is not true during QAPT. By enforcing the zero-mean Gaussian prior as a hard con-straint, CEWT can suppress this detrimental noise. Empirical results on various combinations of SOTA quantizers and hypersphere optimizers suggest, that with a geomean increase of 4% in training time, CEWT can consistently reduce the pre-training perplexity (by an average of 2.5 and up to 21 points) of low-precision (down to 1-bit activations and weights and up to 610M parameters) LLaMA/GPT models without introducing any hyperparameters or storage overhead. Code is available at https://github.com/1733116199/cewt .

However, naively quantizing DNNs often degrades their quality. While Post-Training Quantization (PTQ) methods (Liu et al., 2025a; Lin et al., 2024; Shao et al., 2024; Ma et al., 2024b; Liu et al., 2024a; Kim et al., 2024) can mitigate quality degrada-tion at higher precisions without re-training, PTQ methods generally result in a large accuracy gap when both activations and weights are quantized to 4-bit and below (Panferov et al., 2025; Kumar et al., 2025; Ma et al., 2024a; Esser et al., 2020). In contrast, Quantization-Aware Training (QAT) methods (Liu et al., 2025b; Panferov et al., 2025; Ma et al., 2024a; Kumar et al., 2025; Shen et al., 2024; Yamamoto, 2021; Esser et al., 2020) can narrow this accuracy gap (Du et al., 2024; Pan-ferov et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2024b) by introducing quantization into the optimization process. QAT can be further categorized into Quantization-Aware Pre-Training (Panferov et al., 2025; Yang & Aamodt, 2025) (QAPT) and Quantization-Aware Fine-Tuning (Malinovskii et al., 2024; Du et al., 2024) (QAFT). QAFT starts from a full-precision pre-trained checkpoint and briefly trains a low-precision model on a comparatively small (Du et al., 2024) downstream dataset. In contrast, QAPT trains a low-precision model from scratch on a much larger dataset and for substantially longer. Compared with full-precision pre-training followed by PTQ or QAFT, QAPT may accelerate pre-training (Kumar et al., 2025; Xi et al., 2023; Castro et al., 2025) by performing the forward pass in low precision. This work focuses on improving the accuracy of QAPT when both weights and activations are quantized to 2-bits and below. In QAT, a problem known as rounding boundary weight oscillation (Nagel et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2023; Wenshøj et al., 2025), often attributed (Nagel et al., 2022) to the gradient bias of the Straight-Through Estimator (Bengio et al., 2013) and other rounding Jacobian estimators, can introduce detrimental noise into the optimization process and slow down convergence, especially at the later stages of training. A significant portion of parameters gather at the rounding boundaries of the quan-tization grid, and constantly oscillate from one side of the rounding boundaries to the other. Existing methods attempt to address the slow convergence problem by freezing oscillating parameters (Nagel et al., 2022), by reducing the number of parameters sufficiently close to rounding boundaries (Nagel et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2023; Li et al., 2026), or by simulating deterministic quantization error with random noise (Shin et al., 2023). However, these methods either introduce additional hyperparame-ters, which when under-tuned can reduce the effectiveness, while proper tuning can increase training budget, or they cannot consistently improve the performance of QAPT due to the use of biased op-timization objectives. Inspired by Hypersphere Optimization (Wen et al., 2026; Xie et al., 2026; Ren et al., 2026), which removes weight decay as a hyperparameter by enforcing what weight decay does (i.e. controlling the Frobenius norm of weights) as a hard constraint, we propose optimization with Constrained 

Empirical Weight Dis Tribution (CEWT, pronounced cute ), a hyperparameter-free extension to ex-isting Hypersphere optimizers (e.g. AdamH (Kingma & Ba, 2017; Wen et al., 2026), MuonH (Jor-dan, 2024; Wen et al., 2026), LionH (Chen et al., 2023; Wen et al., 2026), and SSO (Xie et al., 2026)) that projects weights to the nearest point in weight space whose empirical distribution (his-togram) matches a Gaussian. CEWT is inspired by our observation that many quantizers (Yang & Aamodt, 2026; Panferov et al., 2025; Dettmers et al., 2023; Tu et al., 2022) are designed based on the assumption that the empirical distribution of raw weights are, or can be approximately con-verted to, a bell-shaped distribution approximately centered around zero — an assumption that is 

not enforced, and is generally false , in practice. To address this gap between assumption and reality, CEWT enforces the distribution shape to match a zero-mean Gaussian as a hard constraint. CEWT can suppress rounding boundary weight oscillation as it regulates, by design , the number of parame-ters near rounding boundaries and can significantly accelerate convergence without introducing any hyperparameters. 

## 2 BACKGROUND AND MOTIVATION 

In this section, we discuss the rounding boundary weight oscillation problem and existing solutions, and Hypersphere Optimization which removes the weight decay hyperparameter by adding a hard constraint to the optimization problem. As we will discuss later in Section 3, CEWT also removes a hyperparameter by adding a hard constraint. 22.1 ROUNDING BOUNDARY WEIGHT OSCILLATION 

Rounding boundary weight oscillation (Nagel et al., 2022) is the phenomenon that during QAT, a significant portion of parameters gather at a rounding boundary, and constantly move from one side of the rounding boundary to the other, generating significant noise which prevents convergence. Weight oscillation manifests as irregularly high density spikes near rounding boundaries (Nagel et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2023), and can be detected by plotting the histogram of pre-rounding weights. Unless explicitly addressed, weight oscillation happens regardless of the quantizer used. For ex-ample, Figure 1 shows the pre-rounding weight distribution of three SOTA quantizers and their density spikes near rounding boundaries. Weight oscillation tends to happen when a parameter is close to a locally optimal value (Nagel et al., 2022). Weight oscillation happens regardless (Nagel et al., 2022) of the rounding Jacobian estimation method 1. Section 4.2 empirically demonstrates rounding boundary weight oscillation cannot be effectively suppressed with the use of optimizers equipped with momentum (e.g. MuonH/LionH) and/or preconditioning (e.g. AdamH/MuonH). In DNN pre-training, where parameters are typically interdependent 2, a subset of oscillating parame-ters can introduce noise into the gradient of other non-oscillating parameters. The gradient noise becomes more severe as precision (number of bits) reduces and the distance between two quantiza-tion bins increases. Section A shows two simple example problems that demonstrate the root cause of weight oscillation. Existing methods are effective in oscillation suppression but may degrade model accuracy and in-troduce additional hyperparameters and memory overhead. Nagel et al. (2022) proposes to detect oscillating weights and freeze them until the end of training. Weight freezing requires additional storage to record the history of weights for oscillation detection and an oscillating frequency thresh-old hyperparameter which when under-tuned, as Nagel et al. (2022) noted, can freeze too many parameters too early, degrading the model’s final accuracy. Nagel et al. (2022) also proposed an os-cillation dampening loss to regularize the squared distance between quantized weights and clipped weights, effectively pulling unclipped parameters away from rounding boundaries, and can therefore prevent oscillation. However, Nagel et al. (2022) noted that overly strong regularization can inhibit beneficial weight movement between quantization bins and harm final accuracy. Liu et al. (2023) proposes Confidence-Guided Annealing (CGA), which trains only parameters sufficiently close to rounding boundaries while freezing parameters far from boundaries. CGA requires the schedule of a hyperparameter that defines “sufficiently close” to rounding boundaries. Liu et al. (2023) stated that when the threshold hyperparameter is overly small, too many parameter are frozen, inhibit-ing beneficial weight movement which leads to lower final accuracy. Li et al. (2026) proposed to periodically re-initialize weights to a linear interpolation between weights and quantized weights and showed this interpolation is equivalent to regularizing the squared distance between quantized and raw weights. While not originally proposed to suppress weight oscillation, empirically we show Periodic Interpolation also suppresses oscillation (Section 4.1) and we hypothesize this is due to the theoretical connection to dampening loss—both periodic interpolation and dampening loss explicitly pulls weights away from rounding boundaries. Periodic interpolation requires an inter-polation ratio hyperparameter. Li et al. (2026) noted that too large or too low interpolation ratio can lead to suboptimal performance. Pseudo-Quantization Noise (PQN) (Shin et al., 2023) simu-lates the behaviour of rounding with addition of noise from U (−0.5, 0.5) , effectively replacing the original optimization objective (e.g. L(s · ⌊ w/s ⌉), w ∈ Rd, s ∈ R) with a biased objective (e.g. 

Eξi∼U (−0.5,0.5) ,∀i[L(s · (w/s + ξ))] , w ∈ Rd, s ∈ R, ξ ∈ Rd). Sections 4.1 and I empirically show that PQN cannot consistently outperform baseline QAT methods like the STE, and we hypothesize this is due to the biasedness of the optimization objective. Sections 4.1 and D.3 empirically and theoretically demonstrate this biasedness for a toy problem. Sections 4.1 and I show that CEWT consistently outperforms both PQN and the STE. Existing methods demonstrates that reducing the rate of weight oscillation leads to significant accu-racy improvement, but tuning hyperparameter is non-trivial and a tuned hyperparameter for a model size/architecture might not transfer to another. On the other hand, existing hyperparameter-free methods like PQN cannot consistently outperform the STE. We therefore ask the research question:

## 5 LIMITATIONS 

CEWT relies on the law of large numbers to guarantee that a random permutation of weights look indistinguishable from Gaussian noise. Therefore, for extremely small models with only a few hundred parameters in a single layer, CEWT does not work well as it forbids making small changes to parameters. Our ablation studies in Section D.9 shows that CEWT generally works well as long as the number of parameters in a layer exceed 210 .CEWT improves accuracy/reduces loss by suppressing rounding boundary weight oscillation, which is most severe during Quantization-Aware Pre-Training (QAPT), where dataset size is large and training time is long. Section D.9 shows that when training time is short and dataset size is much smaller compared to model size, weight oscillation is less severe, and the accuracy improvement of CEWT can be small and inconsistent. These insights from Section D.9 suggest CEWT might not be suitable for QAFT. However, Section 4.2 shows that CEWT consistently improves accuracy in large-scale QAPT. CEWT maintains the zero-mean Gaussian constraint using argsort, which has an O(mn log( mn )) 

time complexity. The runtime overhead can become more severe for wider models, but the over-head can potentially be reduced with engineering tweaks. For example, CEWT may be applied to each channel individually, reducing the time complexity to O(mn log( n)) . In practice we observe CEWT results in a geomean increase of 4% in training time, while matching the accuracy of existing methods with the best hyperparameters found using computationally expensive grid searches .96 CONCLUSION 

In this work, we introduce optimization with Constrained Empirical Weight disTributions (CEWT), an optimizer post-update step that projects weights to the nearest point in weight space whose empir-ical distribution matches a zero-mean Gaussian. CEWT enforces the zero-mean Gaussian assump-tion, which is built into the design of many existing quantizers. CEWT suppress the irregularly high density spikes that tend to occur due to the weight oscillation problem, thereby reducing gradient noise and improving accuracy.

## B IS R A HYPERPARAMETER OF CEWT? 

In this section, we discuss that R is not a hyperparameter of CEWT or HO, as every deep learning pre-training script choose a value of R by randomly initializing weight matrix W ∈ Rm×n from a zero-mean distribution with a variance of σ2. For a sufficiently large mn , the Frobenius norm radius is R ≈ σ√mn and the spectral norm radius is R ≈ σ(√m + √n). Therefore, at random initial-ization time, every deep learning model lie on some radius-R hypersphere. Hypersphere optimizers (e.g. AdamH/MuonH) proposes to keep the model on this radius-R hypersphere, thereby reusing 

the hyperparameter R, bounding the Frobenius norm of W , and removing the need for weight decay. In this work, we use the Lecun initialization (Le Cun et al., 1998) which results in the Frobenius norm radius R ≈ √m. We additionally perform ablation studies on other possible weight initial-ization strategies/values of R: PyTorch (Paszke et al., 2019) default initialization, Spectral μP (Yang et al., 2024), and the GPT-2 (Radford et al., 2019) initialization strategy. Table 4 shows that the perplexity reduction of CEWT compared to HO is consistent across multiple values of R. For clarity, Table 5 lists multiple combinations of regular/hypersphere optimizers and weight oscillation suppression methods and shows that CEWT has the least number (one to be exact) of hyperparam-eters among all combinations. In particular, Table 5 shows R is not a hyperparameter introduced by CEWT but is a part of every deep learning pre-training script that performs weight random initialization. ## M EVALUATION ON VISION MODELS 

While our main empirical claims are on LLMs, we additional evaluate CEWT with the vision model DEIT-Base (Touvron et al., 2021) pre-trained on Imagenet-64x64 (Russakovsky et al., 2015; Chrabaszcz et al., 2017). Following Touvron et al. (2021), we use 16x16 pixels as a token, and therefore each 64x64 image is converted to a flattened sequence of 16 tokens. We use a batch size of 1024, a hypersphere learning rate of 0.004, and AdamH/Adam-CEWT to conduct our experiments. We first run weight-only quantization experiments with IDs 1-8 in Table 15. Results confirm that rounding boundary weight oscillation can decrease training efficiency, and that CEWT can suppress weight oscillation and achieve lower loss. We next run weight-activation quantization experiments with IDs 9-16 in Table 15. Results suggest that rounding boundary weight oscillation is less severe (when CEWT is not used) and therefore the loss reduction of CEWT is less consistent. We believe weight oscillation is less severe because activation quantization can reduce training efficiency (especially in a training recipe with heavy data augmentation) and delay the behaviour of weight oscillation to a much later stage in training.
