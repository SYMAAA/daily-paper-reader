# ABSTRACT 

How should a Kronecker-factored Hessian approximation be chosen for post-training quanti-zation? We address this question through WaterKron , which combines two-sided GPTQ with row- and column-dependent waterfilling scales and entropy coding. We derive its high-rate distortion with respect to the full Hessian using an explicit Kronecker-Hessian mismatch fac-tor Φ. This factor quantifies the asymptotic distortion penalty due to the Kronecker Hessian approximation and provides a criterion for selecting the factors optimally. Minimizing Φ

leads to a Gaussian covariance-fitting problem with classical “flip-flop” updates. We thus give a rate-distortion justification for using the resulting FlipFlop Hessian in quantization. We evaluate it empirically, finding that the FlipFlop Hessian consistently improves KL divergence and perplexity over input-only, marginal, and Frobenius-based Hessian choices. 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Post-training quantization of neural networks is the process of taking a fully trained neural network, and converting its high-precision weights into a low-precision format, with the goal of retaining accuracy while reducing memory requirements. Usually, each linear module W is quantized separately. Hence the goal is to find a low-precision approximation V of W .There exist different classes of algorithms for finding such a V , which can also be combined. Some methods focus on making the entries of W incoherent and thus easier to quantize, for example by utilizing random or learned rotations (Chee et al., 2023; Ashkboos et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025). Other methods change the base quantizer that is applied to a single or small group of entries, for example by using codebooks or lattices instead of simple rounding (Tseng et al., 2024a; Savkin et al., 2025). A third class of methods does “adaptive rounding” by conditioning the quantization of later entries within W such that they correct the errors introduced by quantizing earlier entries of W .Our work falls within the realm of this third class of methods, more concretely into its largest subclass that might be labeled “error propagation under a quadratic loss”. A prominent example within this line of research is the GPTQ algorithm by Frantar et al. (2023). In order to measure the distortion when W is replaced by V , the algorithm uses a quadratic loss. However, instead of just looking at ∥V − W ∥F , for which simple “round to nearest” is the optimal quantization algorithm, it takes into account the input x ∈ Rn of the linear module W , and measures the distortion as 

E∥V x − W x ∥22. In practice, the expectation is taken over a calibration dataset. The information-theoretic view and waterfilling formula analyzed by Ordentlich and Polyanskiy (2026) and demonstrated experimentally by Lifar et al. (2026). Standard GPTQ allocates the same number of bits for every entry of V . An alternative is to use entropy coding (Chen et al., 2026b; Lifar et al., 2026) for the quantized matrix. This allows in particular to ask about precision/bit/rate allocation: We have the freedom to store some entries in “higher precision” (usually costing more bits), and other entries with “lower precision” (using fewer bits). Concretely, “precision” corresponds to the scaling factors of the quantization grid. For example, Huffman-GPTQ (Chen et al., 2026b), which does use Huffman coding and thus encodes some entries with more bits and others with fewer, still distributes the precision/rate uniformly across all entries. If the variance 

Var( xj ) of the jth input channel is known, a more effective way to distribute precision would be to assign to the jth column of W a scale proportional to 1/pVar( xj ). However, Ordentlich and Polyanskiy (2026); Lifar et al. (2026) notice that even that is suboptimal: In GPTQ, we can exploit linear correlation between different input channels, and what matters is only the residual variance that remains after accounting for this correlation. So the scale for the jth column should be chosen proportional to 1/pmin ej+1 ,...,e n Var( xj − ej+1 xj+1 − · · · − enxn), and this is essentially optimal. In this paper we use the information theoretic lens on the two-sided algorithm. First, this yields WaterKron ,an extension of the existing waterfilling allocation, WaterSIC, to the Kronecker-factored geometry. Then we characterize its distortion in the full Hessian geometry, identify the penalty caused by the Kronecker approximation, and use it to find the optimal choice of Kronecker approximation. Concretely, we make the following contributions: 1. WaterKron: two-sided precision allocation. We extend the high-rate waterfilling results to two-sided GPTQ, deriving optimal row and column scales for the analyzed scalar-grid alphabet. Under the Gaussian and entropy-coding assumptions, this retains the approximately 0.25 -bit asymptotic gap to the information-theoretic limit for the Kronecker distortion (Theorem 3.2). We demonstrate empirically that this outperforms allocating the waterfilling precision using only the input-sided Hessian (WaterSIC). 2. Quantifying the cost of Hessian mismatch. We characterize WaterKron’s high-rate distortion in the full Hessian geometry under the same assumptions. We introduce a mismatch factor Φ that quantifies the multiplicative distortion penalty caused by the Kronecker approximation, providing a criterion for choosing its factors (Theorem 4.1). 3. The FlipFlop Hessian. Minimizing the mismatch factor, we recover the flip-flop algorithm from Gaussian covariance fitting. We propose the resulting FlipFlop Hessian approximation , which is justified by its distortion-minimizing objective, and to the best of our knowledge has not been explored in this line of quantization research (Section 5). We demonstrate empirically that it outperforms previous choices of Hessian approximations from the quantization literature. Our results extend the high-rate waterfilling theory of Ordentlich and Polyanskiy (2026); Lifar et al. (2026) to Kronecker-factored quadratic losses. It recovers (and improves), as a special case, the row- and column-dependent grid allocation of SoftWater (Cavalcanti and Wilson, 2026) which is designed for the Softmax module with a diagonal approximation of the output curvature B.2The rest of the paper is structured as follows. In Section 2 we recall the setting and previous work: standard GPTQ, its two-sided extension, and the waterfilling precision allocation. In Section 3 we first derive the optimal precision allocation for the two-sided algorithm. In Section 4 we take an information-theoretic viewpoint on the optimal choice of the Kronecker-factored Hessian approximation. We introduce the Kronecker-Hessian mismatch factor Φ and provide a variant of the rate-distortion result that takes the full Hessian into account. In Section 5, informed by the mismatch theory, we derive the ideal Kronecker approximation, proposing the FlipFlop Hessian. Lastly, we provide empirical evidence for the success of our methods in Section 6.

Recall that the goal of weight-only post-training quantization is to approximate W ∈ Rm×n with a matrix 

V that has lower precision. For this paper, V has the following structure: • At its core, V has essentially integer entries . We denote this integral matrix by Z ∈ Zm×n. The integers are unbounded (not clipped), and later encoded with a good coding scheme. Thus the memory cost of Z is determined by the entropy of the distribution of its entries. • Additionally, there are real-valued scaling factors per row and per column. Concretely, the jth 

column of V is scaled by αj ∈ R>0 and the ith row of V is scaled by βi ∈ R>0. These input/output scaling factor vectors α = ( α1, . . . , α n) ∈ Rn and β = ( β1, . . . , β m) ∈ Rm are stored in high precision. Their memory cost is very small. So V is an integer matrix Z whose rows and columns have been rescaled by β and α. Concretely, the scaling factors form a rank-1 matrix βα T ∈ Rm×n, and we have: 

V = βα T ⊙ Z Vi,j = αj βi · Zi,j 

Most importantly, the scaling factors can be used for distributing precision/rate across rows and columns. A small scaling factor corresponds to a high rate. For example, if α1 is chosen to be very small, that means the scaled integer grid of column 1 is very fine-grained, so column 1 is stored in relatively high precision. # 5 THE FLIP FLOP HESSIAN APPROXIMATION 

In order to minimize the distortion in Theorem 4.1, we have to find a Hessian approximation A ⊗ B of H

that minimizes Φ( H, A ⊗ B). In general, this minimization problem has no closed form solution. But it turns out that there is an efficient algorithm that converges to the solution. We work in the setting where H can be written as an expectation over outer products of Kronecker-factored vectors: 

H = Ex,g [( x ⊗ g)( x ⊗ g)T ] = Ex,g [xx T ⊗ gg T ]

Here x ∈ Rn and g ∈ Rm, and we will drop the subscript x, g from the expectation for the remainder of the section. There is a very natural setting where this is the case, namely when H is the Fisher information matrix of W , or equivalently the Hessian of the cross entropy loss with respect to the network’s own predictive distribution. In that case, x would be a calibration input of W , y = W x , a network output is sampled from the model’s predictive distribution, and g is the gradient of the negative log-likelihood of this network output with respect to y (see for example Birnick and Saab (2026)). 9We now wish to find A, B that minimize Φ( H, A ⊗ B) = tr( H(A⊗B)−1)/mn

# A TECHNICAL DETAILS 

For the theorems in this paper there are two technicalities that we deliberately left without comment in order to focus on the core statements. In this section we will clarify these technicalities. A.1 ENCODING OF THE SCALING FACTORS α, β

Theorem 2.2, Theorem 3.2, Theorem 4.1 only consider the encoding of Z ∈ Zm×n and ignore the encoding of the scaling factors α ∈ Rn and β ∈ Rm. This is to keep the theorem statement clean. In practice, the memory cost of the scaling factors is negligible. For the theoretical coding argument, we can give the decoder direct access to α, β , so there is no need to encode them. In fact, the decoder can even be given full access to A,B (which in particular recover α, β ), as the lower bounds of Theorem 2.1, Theorem 3.1 still hold true even with this more powerful decoder. Concretely, the decoder g would then be a function g : ( {0, 1}∗, Rn×n, Rm×m) → Rm×n, taking a string and A, B, and producing V . For Algorithm 2, the string would be the entropy-coded Z, and α, β as defined in Theorem 3.2 can be found from A, B , so the decoder can find V = βα T ⊙ Z. Now, with this more powerful decoder, one could worry that the lower bound Theorem 3.1 would not hold anymore, as a decoder with access to A, B perhaps allows for a much more efficient scheme; but this is not the case. Indeed, the standard proof of Theorem 3.1 already assumes decoder access to A and B, see Ordentlich and Polyanskiy (2026); Lifar et al. (2026). A.2 LIMIT γ → 0 AND mn → ∞ 

Theorem 2.2, Theorem 3.2, Theorem 4.1 all consider the limit γ → 0 and mn → ∞ . The limit γ → 0

makes sure that we are in the high rate regime where the transformed quantization errors become uniformly distributed. The limit mn → ∞ , i.e. growing matrix size, is to ensure the efficiency of the coding scheme, so that it can approach the entropy of the distribution of Z. Note that due to the second limit, these theorems are technically not about a fixed A, B , but about all of them at the same time. In this case, one needs to be careful how the joint limit γ → 0 and mn → ∞ is taken. We understand it in an iterated sense: First take γ → 0 for fixed A, B , then take mn → ∞ . Then for each concrete instance of A, B 

it is ensured that we are in the high-rate limit where the transformed quantization errors are uniformly distributed. 14
