# VisionMX: Unlocking Microscaling Post-Training Quantization for Vision Models

## Arm AI Research (AAIR), Arm Ltd 

## Abstract 

Microscaling (MX) formats are emerging as a hardware-supported approach to efficient training and inference. They combine low-precision elements with shared block scales, but their impact on vision models remains underexplored. We systematically investigate post-training MX quantiza-tion across vision models and tasks. An analysis of di-rect conversion identifies three sources of error: block-scale representation, the poor alignment of some small con-volutional weight tensors with nonuniform element grids, and the underuse of signed codes by nonnegative activa-tions. These findings motivate VisionMX, a post-training MX quantization method that optimizes bounded weight rounding and applies a foldable affine correction to acti-vations. We evaluate VisionMX across image classification, object detection, semantic segmentation, and low-light im-age enhancement using several MX-style formats. It im-proves on direct conversion and the evaluated post-training quantization baselines, with the largest performance recov-eries in architectures most sensitive to MX conversion. 

## 1. Introduction 

Modern deep learning models have led to significant progress in computer vision, spanning a broad set of tasks and applications such as image classification [5, 38], ob-ject detection[35, 41], and image restoration[48]. These advances, however, have come with growing computa-tion and memory demands that complicate efficient de-ployment and inference. This is especially important for high-resolution inputs and deployments with tight latency, memory-bandwidth, or power budgets. Quantization [11, 12, 16, 18, 31, 44] helps meet these requirements by representing weights and intermediate ac-tivations at lower precision, reducing both storage and com-putation. Quantization methods are commonly divided into quantization-aware training (QAT) [16, 32] and post-

training quantization (PTQ) [12, 14, 18, 26, 31, 44]. QAT trains or fine-tunes a model while accounting for quantiza-tion, often using labeled data and a task-specific training recipe. PTQ instead starts from a pretrained model and uses a small calibration set, typically without labels, to choose quantization parameters or apply limited fine-tuning. Quan-tization of vision models [12, 18, 20, 31, 44, 50] has been studied extensively in integer formats, including learned rounding, which optimizes weight rounding decisions to preserve layer outputs, as well as block reconstruction, activation-aware reconstruction, and transformer-specific reparameterization methods. Recently, the Open Compute Project (OCP) introduced Microscaling (MX) formats [36], which combine low-precision element formats with a shared scale over small tensor blocks. Moreover, NVIDIA introduced NVFP4 as its own MX-like format for low-precision computation [1]. We collectively refer to these MX-style formats as block-scaled formats. Recent work on MX quantization for LLMs has re-ported a significant performance drop when activation and weights are quantized via direct casting to low-bit formats. To mitigate this, several studies have addressed the prob-lem in different ways: (i) [9, 13] propose transforming the feature distribution to better match the MX block structure; and (ii) others explore alternatives to power-of-two scaling factors [10]. Vision-specific evidence remains limited and mixed, with existing studies reporting substantial MXFP4 degradation in some architectures and settings [7, 36, 45]. This leaves a central question: When are MX quantization effective for vision inference, and what PTQ adaptations are needed when direct conversion falls short? 

To address this question, we study post-training quan-tization with MX quantization across diverse vision archi-tectures and tasks. We examine how model architecture, element format, block-scale format, and the quantization of weights and activations affect performance. Our analysis re-veals substantial variation in sensitivity to MX quantization across architectures. Several evaluated transformer mod-els remain relatively robust, whereas compact convolutional and hybrid models can suffer severe degradation. In addi-tion to variation at the architectural level, the block-scale 1arXiv:2610.03218v1 [cs.CV] 2 Oct 2026 format can significantly impact accuracy. We further note two representation mismatches: (1) a discrepancy between adaptive rounding methods[12, 18, 31, 44] and the nonuni-form floating-point grid, and (2) signed grids can waste rep-resentational capacity when activations are nonnegative due to nonlinearities such as ReLU. These findings point to two complementary adaptations: more flexible weight rounding on nonuniform element grids and better alignment of nonnegative activations with signed grids. Guided by this analysis, we introduce VisionMX: a vision-specified MX PTQ approach proposing a new adap-tive rounding, the learned quantizer RangeRound, which addresses the mismatch with floating-point values and ex-tending the asymmetric correction [3, 14] to MX quanti-zation, which we refer to as affine activation correction. RangeRound addresses the first issue by relaxing the con-ventional restriction of optimizing the rounding decision only between the two nearest quantization levels. Instead, RangeRound allows each value to select its quantized rep-resentation from a fixed local range of quantization levels. This additional flexibility enables the reconstruction objec-tive to better adapt to the nonuniform grid of floating-point formats: values near zero can move across multiple densely spaced quantization levels, while values near the clipping boundary can be rounded either upward or downward to better minimize the reconstruction error. For activation alignment, we adapt affine reparameterization to the MX setting through Activation Affine Correction (AAC), which shifts and scales activations before quantization and folds the inverse transformation into the subsequent linear oper-ation. In our experiments, we evaluate VisionMX across convolutional, transformer, and hybrid architectures on im-age classification, object detection, semantic segmentation, and low-light image enhancement. The results show im-provements over established PTQ baselines in most evalu-ated settings under joint 4-bit weight and activation quanti-zation. Moreover, we conduct ablation studies to assess the contribution of each component and examine the effect of the rounding-range. Our contributions are threefold. 1 We provide a sys-tematic analysis of post-training MX quantization across diverse vision architectures, characterizing architecture-dependent sensitivity, examining how floating-point for-mats affect adaptive rounding weight quantization, and identifying the underutilization of signed element grids by nonnegative activation distributions. 2 We introduce Vi-sionMX, a vision-specific MX PTQ method which intro-duce RangeRound for adaptive weight rounding of float point with Activation Affine Correction. 3 We thoroughly assess the proposed method against established baselines across representative convolutional, transformer, and hybrid vision architectures, observing performance improvements on most models and tasks.

## 3. Related Work 

MX Quantization. The Microscaling (MX) Data For-mats, recently introduced by the Open Compute Project (OCP), are driven by key hardware vendors such as Arm, AMD, NVIDIA, Qualcomm, Intel, Meta, and Microsoft, and aim to standardize data formats for neural-network de-ployment [36]. MX introduces block-scaled quantization 2with several numerical formats, including floating-point and integer variants. In addition, NVIDIA developed its own block-scaled format, NVFP4 [1]. Most subsequent MX research has focused on language models. MicroScopiQ, MicroMix, MR-GPTQ, and LAT-MiX explore mixed formats, feature transformations, and reconstruction methods for LLM inference [9, 13, 23, 33]. FAAR [17] adapts learned rounding [31] to NVFP4’s nonuniform grid by optimizing a relaxed decision between adjacent representable values for each weight. Separately, a study [10] analyzes how block size interacts with quantized block scales, showing that finer blocks can hurt LLM per-formance when the scale format has limited dynamic range, and proposes UE5M3 as an alternative block-scale format. Early ImageNet results for MX quantization of vision models showed that post-training MXFP4 can cause sub-stantial accuracy degradation, particularly for compact con-volutional networks [36]. The limited vision-specific litera-ture includes TetraJet [7], which studies oscillation-reduced MXFP4 training for vision transformers rather than PTQ, and [45] is a recent study that evaluates emulated MX in-ference for classification, detection, and segmentation but reports substantial MXFP4 accuracy losses.In addition, a re-cent study [39] investigates NVFP4 quantization for image classification, assessing block size, weight precision, and how activation scaling affects performance both with and without retraining. 

Vision Quantization. Integer quantization has been extensively studied for vision models through both quantization-aware training and PTQ, including rounding optimization and reconstruction-based methods. We focus on PTQ because it avoids costly model retraining. Meth-ods such as AdaRound [31], BRECQ [18], QDrop [44], and EPTQ [12] have substantially improved low-bit inte-ger PTQ through learned rounding and different optimiza-tion strategies, such as blockwise reconstruction, stochastic activation bypass, and network-wide optimization. These techniques enable practical 4-bit quantization and improve more challenging lower-bit settings, although severe degra-dation can remain at extremely low precision. Earlier CNN-oriented PTQ work established calibration, bias correc-tion, and cross-layer weight equalization as effective mech-anisms for reducing quantization error [2, 30]. More re-cent work targets vision transformers, whose activation dis-tributions and outliers are particularly difficult to quan-tize. Other methods address additional difficulties arising from attention, Softmax, GELU, LayerNorm, inter-channel variation, and calibration under low-precision quantization [25, 27, 29, 46, 49]. RepQ-ViT reparameterizes activation scales, while ERQ reduces errors arising from both weight and activation quantization [20, 50]. All vision PTQ meth-ods discussed above target integer quantization. Because MX is a newly emerging data format and PTQ in low-bit regimes is still insufficiently studied with a sub-stantial performance gap, this paper explores post-training MX quantization by analyzing where accuracy degrades across major vision architecture families and proposing Vi-sionMX, which leverages these findings to combine learned weight rounding with affine activation correction. 

## 4. Analysis 

We study how model architecture, block-scale for-mat, weight structure, and activation distribution af-fect vision models’ sensitivity to MX quantization. The analysis is organized around three questions that characterize the main sources of this sensitivity. 

Q1: How does sensitivity to MX quantization vary across vision architecture families and between weights and ac-tivations? 

Q2: How do nonuniform element grid affect adaptive rounding algorithm ? 

Q3: How well do signed element grids represent activa-tion distributions produced by nonlinear operations? To identify the sources of degradation, we combine empiri-cal comparisons across vision architectures and theoretical analysis of weight rounding and an examination of acti-vation code utilization. We begin with round-to-nearest evaluations to establish baseline sensitivity before applying learned PTQ adaptations.

4.3. MX Quantization after Nonlinearities 

Activation quantization introduces a mismatch between ac-tivation distributions and the available quantization levels. Nonlinearities such as ReLU produce nonnegative outputs, leaving the negative levels of signed MX element grids un-used. Figure 1c illustrates this restriction for ResNet18’s final post-residual ReLU output. The bars report quantized FP4 element-code occupancy; each distribution uses its own dynamically recomputed block scales. The unshifted output is nonnegative and therefore cannot use negative element codes. For an approximately symmetric signed grid, this leaves roughly half of the nonzero representable values un-used, corresponding to an effective loss of approximately one bit of code-space capacity. Changing the shared scale can expand or contract the represented range, but it cannot recenter the distribution or make the unused negative codes accessible. Applying an affine shift before quantization moves part of the originally nonnegative activation distribution onto the negative side of the signed grid, making previously unused codes accessible. At the same time, the scale adjustment controls its range. The resulting change in code utiliza-tion diagnoses the mismatch between post-nonlinearity ac-tivation distributions and signed block-scaled formats. This observation motivates Activation Affine Correction (AAC), which shifts and scales activations before quantization and folds the inverse transformation into the subsequent linear or convolutional operation. 

## 5. Method 

Motivated by the distinct challenges identified for weight and activation quantization in Section 4, our proposed PTQ pipeline, VisionMX, has two complementary components. First, Activation Affine Correction (AAC) shifts and scales activations before MX activation quantization. Second, RangeRound optimizes weight-rounding decisions within a bounded region of the nonuniform element grid while keep-ing the deployed block scales fixed. 

5.1. Activation Affine Correction 

We address the issue in Figure 1c, where enforcing non-negativity leaves part of a signed MX quantization grid un-used. Following LSQ+ [3] and HPTQ [14], we apply an affine transform to better align the feature distribution with the quantization range. Specifically, before dynamic MX activation quantization we shift and scale the input to each linear operation, then fold the inverse transform into the lin-ear operation. The shift recenters activations to better utilize the signed grid, and the scale adjusts the range. We adapt affine correction to the MX block structure by calibrating per-channel shifts and scales block by block, accounting for their shared scale when selecting the correction parameters. ## 7. Conclusion 

This work studies post-training Microscaling quantization across convolutional, transformer, and hybrid vision ar-chitectures. Our analysis shows that, among the evalu-ated models, compact convolutional and hybrid architec-tures tend to be more sensitive to MX quantization, while transformers are generally more robust. We identify two sources of accuracy degradation: (i) nonuniform floating-point grids, which can limit weight reconstruction when rounding is restricted to adjacent levels; and (ii) nonneg-ative activations produced by nonlinearities, which leave negative quantization codes unused. We address these limitations by introducing a new quan-tization method, VisionMX, that extends adaptive round-ing to nonuniform floating-point grids through range-rounding, allowing weight assignments to be optimized over a bounded range of quantization levels. Additionally, we adapt affine activation correction to the MX block struc-ture, shifting and scaling activations to utilize the signed grid better. Our experimental results show that the proposed method outperforms established PTQ baselines on MXFP4-UE8M0, MXFP4-UE5M3, and NVFP4, across a broad range of vision tasks. Finally, using VisionMX, we have shown that MX-aware post-training quantization enables state-of-the-art performance in most benchmarked models. Moreover, these results demonstrate that the new MX data format is well suited for vision applications.

A.1. Experimental Details 

Unless otherwise stated, we use the following settings in all experiments. We quantize the input activations and weights of all linear operations, including the input and output layers. Weight block scales are initialized by min-imizing the mean squared error between the original and quantized weights, as described in Appendix A.2. We use the Adam optimizer with weight decay 0.01 and a warmup fraction of 0.2. When QDrop is enabled, its probability is 

0.5.

ImageNet-1K classification. We select calibration im-ages with a deterministic class-round-robin sampler and use a batch size of 32. We run reconstruction for 20,000 steps per layer or block, except for ViT-S/16, for which we use 10,000 steps. For layerwise reconstruction, we set the bias learning rate to 10 −3 and disable both the bias penalty and QDrop. For blockwise reconstruction, we set the bias learn-ing rate to 10 −5 and the bias penalty to 10 −3, and enable QDrop. For MobileNetV2 with layerwise UE8M0 quanti-zation, we set the rounding learning rate to 0.003 and the regularization weight to 1.0. We evaluate all 50,000 vali-dation images using the preprocessing associated with each pretrained checkpoint and report top-1 accuracy. 

COCO detection and segmentation. We select calibra-tion images from train2017 with a class-aware round-robin sampler. We run adaptive reconstruction for 20,000 steps per layer, with a rounding learning rate of 10 −3 and a regu-larization weight of 0.01 . We use a batch size of 1 to eval-uate all 5,000 val2017 images. For detection, we report bounding-box average precision (AP). For segmentation, we resize predictions to the target mask resolution and re-port mean intersection-over-union (mIoU) over classes with nonzero union. 

Low-light image enhancement. We use deterministic calibration crops from the LOLv2-Real Train/Low split. We run both layerwise and blockwise reconstruction for 15,000 steps and set the rounding learning rate and regularization weight to 10 −2. We enable QDrop only for blockwise re-construction. We evaluate at native resolution with a batch size of 1 and report per-image RGB PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS-Alex without mean correction.

A.5. Activation Affine Correction Details 

Activation affine correction stores a per-channel scale α and shift γ with each quantized layer. The transform is applied before dynamic block activation quantization: 

u = diag( α)( x − γ).

Before weight quantization, the inverse transform is folded into the following floating-point linear operation: 

W′ = W diag( α)−1, b′ = b + Wγ. (16) Replacing activation quantization by the identity therefore preserves the original computation algebraically, and up to floating-point rounding in the implementation. For convolutions, weight division and bias compensation are performed per input channel within each convolution group. The bias compensation sums the original kernel mul-tiplied by the corresponding channel shift over input chan-nels and kernel positions. This also covers depthwise con-volution. At inference, spatial padding is applied before the affine transform and activation quantization, and the convo-lution then uses zero internal padding. Calibration instead uses the captured real layer-input positions, excluding spa-tial padding.
