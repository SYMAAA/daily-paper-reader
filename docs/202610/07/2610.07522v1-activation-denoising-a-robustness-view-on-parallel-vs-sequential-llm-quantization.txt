ABSTRACT 

Post-training quantization is a powerful tool for compressing large language mod-els. The most scalable methods quantize every layer in parallel, but quantiza-tion errors then compound through the residual stream, as no layer corrects for the errors of the layers before it. Sequential quantization accounts for this error compounding by re-calibrating each layer on the already-quantized outputs of its predecessors, yielding stronger results but at the cost of a serial schedule that be-comes a bottleneck at scale. As a solution, we propose parallel quantization with activation denoising , which recovers much of the sequential benefit while keep-ing quantization fully parallel. Rather than re-calibrating layer-by-layer, we take a robustness perspective and model the upstream error as noise, regularizing to be robust to it through a preprocessing step followed by metric-weighted rounding. Applied at every layer, this regularization forms a depth-compounding smoothness penalty that dampens how strongly quantization errors amplify through the model. Unlike orthogonal rotations commonly used in quantization, which must preserve the model’s function, we multiply the weights by a more general linear trans-formation. We find that the two are complementary and their effects compound. Empirically, our robustness regularization recovers a significant part of sequential quantization’s benefit in a single parallel pass, at a fraction of its time. Overall, by treating compounding quantization errors as a robustness problem, we offer a principled foundation for more efficient and accurate LLM quantization at scale. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Post-training quantization (PTQ) is a powerful tool for deploying large language models (LLMs) un-der memory and bandwidth budgets, mapping high-precision weights to a few bits without retraining (Dettmers et al., 2022; Dettmers & Zettlemoyer, 2023; Frantar et al., 2023). Quantization usually in-volves two steps: (1) a forward pass that collects each layer’s input activations, and (2) rounding the weights onto the quantization grid, a challenging optimization problem that dominates quantization time. The most scalable PTQ methods therefore round all layers independently and in parallel. The key problem of existing parallel methods is that they sacrifice accuracy for efficiency: each layer is calibrated on clean inputs and never corrects for quantization errors it receives at inference time from previous layers, so errors compound through the model (Arai & Ichikawa, 2025). Sequen-tial quantization corrects this by calibrating each layer on outputs of the layers quantized before it (Frantar et al., 2023; Li et al., 2025). This becomes a bottleneck at scale since layers must then be quantized one after another. Recovery fine-tuning (Tseng et al., 2024a; Malinovskii et al., 2024) can partially recover lost accuracy, but only as a separate costly training stage. As a solution, we take a robustness perspective on the gap between parallel and sequential quanti-zation and propose parallel quantization with activation denoising (Figure 1): instead of correcting for the upstream quantization error one layer at a time, we make each layer robust to upstream per-turbations. In a single additional forward pass through the full-precision model, we inject random noise into the activations and propagate it through the model. Overall, our findings suggest that accumulated quantization error can be treated as a robustness problem: regularizing all layers against noise in parallel offers an efficient alternative to correcting the error sequentially. into the weights. After this step, we round the modified weights in a noise-aware metric, indepen-dently and in parallel. The denoising filter acts as a depth-compounding smoothness penalty that limits how strongly errors from quantized layers amplify through the LLM at inference. Activation denoising adds weighted column-wise L2 regularization, complementing rotations. Ro-tations reduce weight outliers by redistributing magnitude across channels (Chee et al., 2023; Ashk-boos et al., 2024b), but preserve the Frobenius norm. To preserve the model’s function, each rotation must be paired with its inverse, either fused into adjacent weights or applied online at inference. Our denoising filter instead absorbs its regularization into a general linear weight transform. Empirically, it lowers the Frobenius norm and improves over both random and learned rotations. Our main contributions are: • Activation denoising framework. We propose a quantization objective that makes each layer robust to perturbed inputs and generalizes existing parallel and sequential objectives. We show that it reduces exactly to metric-weighted rounding with a transformed target and metric, allow-ing the use of existing rounding algorithms and adding no inference cost. • Noise injection as a robustness regularizer. To enable parallel and robust quantization, we estimate the required statistics from a single additional forward pass with injected Gaussian noise at the input of each weight, and prove that the local injection induces a weighted column-wise 

L2 penalty toward down-scaled targets. • Empirical validation. We demonstrate that parallel activation denoising consistently improves 

3- and 4-bit scalar and 2-bit vector quantization across models from 1B to 13 B parameters, recovering a significant part of the benefit of sequential quantization while all layers are still quantized in parallel. Finally, we show that the remaining gap to sequential quantization can be closed through cheap trade-offs rather than a full sequential pass. Overall, addressing quantization errors from a robustness perspective pushes the efficiency–accuracy frontier of LLM quantization, narrowing the gap to sequential quantization at a fraction of its time. 

2 RELATED WORK 

Post-training quantization for LLMs. PTQ compresses a pretrained LLM using a small calibration set without retraining (Yao et al., 2022; Dettmers et al., 2022; Dettmers & Zettlemoyer, 2023). Below 4 bits, the dominant approach minimizes a layer-wise reconstruction error with second-order information (Nagel et al., 2020; Frantar & Alistarh, 2022; Frantar et al., 2023), recently refined with Kronecker-factored curvature (Tseng et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2026). Complementary directions protect salient or outlier weights (Lin et al., 2024b; Dettmers et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2024), allocate mixed precision (Huang et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2024), learn quantization parameters block-wise (Shao et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024a), or replace the uniform grid with floating-point formats (Liu 2Preprint. et al., 2023) or vector or trellis codes (Egiazarian et al., 2024; van Baalen et al., 2024; Tseng et al., 2024a;b; van der Ouderaa et al., 2024). Our proposed framework is general and supports both scalar and vector quantization. Moreover, we focus on finetuning-free PTQ, avoiding the extra training stage of quantization-aware training and recovery fine-tuning (Liu et al., 2024b; Malinovskii et al., 2024) or the inference cost of full-precision low-rank corrections (Scetbon & Hensman, 2024). 

Outliers and incoherence processing. Outliers in weights and activations limit low-bit precision (Wei et al., 2022b; Bondarenko et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024) and can be migrated between acti-vations and weights by equivalent channel-wise scaling (Xiao et al., 2023; Wei et al., 2023). Inco-herence processing instead rotates weights and activations using random orthogonal or Hadamard matrices (Chee et al., 2023; Tseng et al., 2024a), which spread outliers without changing the model output, but modify the forward pass. Exploiting the computational invariance of transformers (Ashk-boos et al., 2024a), some of these rotations can be fully fused into weights, leaving the forward pass unchanged but tying the rotations across layers (Ashkboos et al., 2024b). Recent work optimizes these transformations rather than sampling them (Lin et al., 2024a; Liu et al., 2025; Akhondzadeh et al., 2025; Shao et al., 2025; Gadhikar et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2025). These transformations make weights and activations easier to quantize while preserving the full-precision model’s output. Our denoising instead changes each layer’s mapping to compensate for upstream errors and reduces the weights’ Frobenius norm, complementing the outlier redistribution of orthogonal rotations. 

Cross-layer error compensation. Sequential methods compensate for the error accumulated in already-quantized layers (Nagel et al., 2020; Li et al., 2021; Ding et al., 2025): GPTAQ (Li et al., 2025) matches each quantized layer to the full-precision output within GPTQ’s column-wise up-dates, QEP (Arai & Ichikawa, 2025) achieves the same through a closed-form weight correction applied before rounding, Qronos (Zhang et al., 2025) corrects it within the rounding updates, and CoreQ (Cha et al., 2026) derives the correction strength in closed form. The corrections above require measuring the accumulated error in a fully sequential pass. We instead replace the measure-ment with a noise model estimated in parallel and account for it in the rounding target and metric; our objective recovers those of GPTQ, GPTAQ, and QEP as special cases (section 4). Our random noise model relates to randomly dropping activation quantization during reconstruction (Wei et al., 2022a), but acts as a quantization-time regularizer that requires no gradient-based optimization. 

3 BACKGROUND 

Post-training quantization. Post-training quantization (PTQ) compresses a trained model by map-ping its weights from high precision (e.g. 16 bits) to a few bits, without retraining. Let Gb denote the set of weight matrices representable with b bits per weight: uniform scalar quantization rounds each weight to one of 2b levels of a uniform grid, with scale and zero-point shared within a row or group, whereas vector quantization rounds groups of g weights jointly to one of 2bg entries. 4 ACTIVATION DENOISING QUANTIZATION : A FILTERING PERSPECTIVE 

Quantization errors compound across layers: each layer’s rounding error propagates downstream. Existing parallel methods ignore the upstream error during quantization. Sequential quantization corrects it, but has to round the layers one after another, which prevents parallelizing the rounding step. This leaves a gap between efficient parallel and accurate sequential quantization. As a solution, our main idea is to denoise each layer’s input activations in parallel. To this end, we first propose a denoising quantization objective and show that it reduces to a linear weight transform followed by rounding in a new metric, at zero additional inference cost. 4.1 QUANTIZATION WITH ACTIVATION DENOISING

The first term is a reconstruction objective on the layer’s output: the quantized weights receive the noisy input x + λη and must match the full-precision output evaluated on the target input x+ρη. The input noise scale λ ∈ [0 , 1] sets how much error the quantized layer’s input carries, and the target noise scale ρ ∈ [0 , 1] how much of it the target keeps. The second term is a proximity penalty on the weights, where α ≥ 0 is a proximity ridge. 

Partial vs. full denoising. Keeping less noise in the target than in the input ( ρ < λ ) asks the layer to denoise its input. Here, ρ = 0 is an asymmetric correction that requires the layer to remove the noise entirely, and ρ = λ yields a symmetric correction, where the layer adapts to the noise without removing it. Values in between ( 0 < ρ < λ ) correspond to partial denoising. We treat λ and ρ as hyperparameters and find that partial denoising typically performs best in practice, even when η is the exact sequential error (see Appendix A.2). 

The activation denoising objective establishes a general and flexible quantization framework.

We show this in detail in Appendix C. In contrast to these baselines, our framework is more general and supports general noise models beyond the sequential ηseq and flexible noise scales λ and ρ. 6 EXPERIMENTAL EVALUATION 

Parallel quantization processes layers independently but ignores upstream quantization errors. Se-quential quantization corrects them, but must process layers one after another. Our method keeps quantization fully parallel: it regularizes each layer to be robust to input noise (section 5) via activa-tion denoising (section 4). In this section we demonstrate the effectiveness of our parallel denoising and show that, across model scales, it recovers a significant fraction of the sequential quality. We report additional results and ablations in Appendix A and describe the full setup in Appendix B. 

Setup. We quantize Llama-3.2-1B, Llama-3.2-3B, Llama-3-8B, and Llama-2-13B (Touvron et al., 2023; Llama Team, 2024), which are widely used in the quantization literature and span a broad range of scales. We focus on weight quantization, keeping activations and KV cache at 16 bits, and calibrate on 128 2048-token sequences of the WikiText-2 (Merity et al., 2017) training set. 

Solvers. Our approach is agnostic to the rounding solver, and we evaluate it with scalar and vector quantization via GPTQ (Frantar et al., 2023) at 3 bits and QuIP# (Tseng et al., 2024a) (without fine-tuning) at 2 bits. For GPTQ, we rotate weights before quantization, using the random Hadamard rotations R1, R2, R4 of QuaRot (Ashkboos et al., 2024b), optionally replacing R1, R2 with learned rotations as proposed in SpinQuant (Liu et al., 2025). QuIP# applies its own randomized Hadamard incoherence transform. The rotations R1 and R2 are fused into the model’s weights and modify the forward pass during moment estimation, whereas QuIP#’s rotations are applied during quantization, leave the forward pass unchanged during moment estimation, and modify it instead at inference. 

Baselines. For each solver, we compare against the two ends of the parallel–sequential spectrum: parallel quantization on clean inputs, and sequential quantization on outputs of the already quantized layers, with either the symmetric correction of GPTQ (Frantar et al., 2023) or the asymmetric cor-rection of GPTAQ (Li et al., 2025). The asymmetric correction is the strongest sequential baseline. 

Metrics. For each method, we select the best configuration by WikiText-2 (Merity et al., 2017) test perplexity and report perplexity averaged over WikiText-2, C4 (Raffel et al., 2020), and FineWeb (Penedo et al., 2024) test (i.e. not calibration) sequences. Excluding WikiText-2 from the reported average yields the same conclusions (Appendix A.6). We also report zero-shot accuracy averaged over eight commonsense tasks (Liu et al., 2025), and the fraction of the gap in avg. perplexity from

Table 1 reports our main results. At 3 bits, parallel denoising closes 18 –31% of the perplexity gap across all four scales, recovering a significant fraction of the sequential benefit in a single parallel pass. At 2 bits it closes 38% and 86% of the gap on Llama-3.2-1B and 3B, and on Llama-3-8B and Llama-2-13B it even outperforms the sequential method. We see two plausible reasons why our denoising outperforms the sequential method at 2 bits. First, QuIP#’s randomized incoherence transform may already spread errors well enough that sequential correction helps less: at 3B– 13 B it recovers only 0–16% of the perplexity lost by parallel QuIP#, compared to 18 –28% for GPTQ with QuaRot rotations, whereas our denoising recovers a similar or larger fraction under QuIP#. Second, the sequential method is the asymmetric correction of Li et al. (2025) applied to QuIP#. While it is a state-of-the-art baseline, it is not necessarily optimal: it is a special case ( λ=1 ) of our objective with the sequential error (Appendix C), which fixes how much upstream error the layer is assumed to receive. Our framework is more flexible and treats λ as a free parameter, and tuning it improves on the asymmetric correction even with the sequential error (Appendix A.2). Lastly, on zero-shot accuracy, most differences are within about one point, so we view our denoising as comparable to parallel and sequential quantization on accuracy; only at 2 bits does the sequential asymmetric correction fall behind, which our denoising avoids. Our denoising already recovers much of the gap in a single parallel pass. In this section we explore two approaches to further close the remaining gap without quantizing every layer sequentially. 

Trading quantization time: a short sequential prefix suffices. First, we compute sequential GP-TAQ for the first K of L blocks and continue quantizing the remaining blocks with parallel-GPTQ (Figure 4, middle). We find that the benefit of sequential quantization is strongly front-loaded across all models: the first quarter of the blocks already closes 40 –60% of the gap, whereas closing the last few percent requires sequential quantization to span most of the depth. We see two complementary explanations for this front-loading: First, quantization error compounds through depth, and errors introduced at early blocks propagate through every downstream block, so correcting them early may have a disproportionately large effect. Second, later layers may contribute less to the output, con-sistent with evidence that language models do not use their depth efficiently and that deeper layers can often be removed with little effect (Csord´ as et al., 2025; Men et al., 2025), leaving less error to correct. We leave attempts to disentangle these effects to future work. 

Trading inference cost: a few 4-bit modules suffice. Second, we explore promoting a small prefix of modules to 4 bits at inference time, while keeping the rest of the network at 3 bits. We find that the value projections vproj dominate: promoting only them to 4 bits in a single parallel pass matches full sequential-GPTAQ quality at an average width of just 3.03 bits, i.e. the whole gap for 0.03 bits over W3. Because vproj carries so few parameters under grouped-query attention, this undercuts every finer group size (g 32 needs a full 3.5 bits for the same quality) and any other prefix in the comparison. When a small inference-time cost is acceptable, this is a cheap and effective way to close the remaining gap without any sequential quantization. 8Preprint.

7 DISCUSSION 

Future work. We see several promising directions for future work: extending our activation denois-ing to activation and KV-cache quantization, combining it with stronger rounding schemes (Tseng et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2026), and jointly learning the noise injections and rotations. Recovery fine-tuning (Tseng et al., 2024a; Malinovskii et al., 2024) is a further, orthogonal future direction. 

Closing the remaining gap. In a single parallel pass, our denoising already recovers a large frac-tion of the sequential benefit, though a gap to fully sequential quantization remains. We see the robustness perspective as a promising way to close it further, e.g. by using noise models that carry signal-correlated structure. We demonstrate this in Appendix A.3: the propagated error of an al-ready parallel-quantized model provides a signal-correlated noise model, so an existing quantized model can be improved by denoising against its measured error, closing more of the gap without any sequential quantization. Moreover, because all rounding steps stay independent, the efficiency advantage over sequential quantization should grow at larger scale, which we already demonstrate up to 13 B-parameter models. Lastly, where a small trade-off is acceptable, the remaining gap can already be closed with a short sequential prefix or by promoting a few modules to 4 bits (section 6.3). 

Robustness regularization. Our results suggest that much of the benefit of sequential quantization does not require the exact sequential upstream error: making each layer robust to propagated pertur-bations already recovers a significant part of the gap. While our regularization introduces additional hyperparameters that require tuning (noise scales λ, ρ ; injection magnitude γ), we believe this can be overcome, since their optimal values are similar across models (Appendix A). More broadly, our regularization is similar to the equivalence between training with input noise and Tikhonov regu-larization (Bishop, 1995), here it arises at quantization time. This corresponds to average-case ro-bustness against random perturbations; its worst-case, adversarial counterpart (Szegedy et al., 2014; Goodfellow et al., 2015; Madry et al., 2018) is a promising direction for future work. 

8 CONCLUSION 

We introduce activation denoising, which recovers much of the benefit of sequential post-training quantization while keeping quantization fully parallel. Our central take-away is that the up-stream quantization errors, which sequential methods handle through expensive layer-by-layer re-calibration, can instead be addressed by regularizing every layer to be robust to noise, in a single parallel pass. Treating quantization errors as a robustness problem thus offers a principled and more efficient alternative to sequential quantization, providing a promising direction for efficient and ac-curate LLM compression at scale. 9Preprint.

A ADDITIONAL EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS AND ABLATIONS 

In this section we provide additional experimental results and ablations extending our main findings. A.1 NOISE -MODEL MECHANISM ABLATION : LOCAL VS . PROPAGATED NOISE INJECTION 

Section 5.1 attributes the effect of the injected noise to two mechanisms: a local column-wise reg-ularization from the injection ξ at each layer (effect (i), formalized by Theorem 2), and a depth-compounding regularization from propagating the injected noise downstream (effect (ii)). Figure 5 isolates the two by ablating the noise model on the 5×5 ( ρ, δ ) grid for Llama-3.2-1B at W3A16 (Gaussian injection, random Hadamard rotation, ridge α=0 .01 ), under a shared color scale. Turning off all injected noise reduces our objective to parallel GPTQ: the noise moments vanish (N=C=0), so ρ and δ have no effect and the grid is flat at the parallel-GPTQ value (here 17 .07 at the fixed ridge). Injecting the noise locally without propagation, i.e., on top of the clean forward pass (so each layer sees only its own newly sampled ξ), already closes 11% of the parallel–sequential gap, confirming that effect (i) works as a standalone regularizer. With propagation, so that the injected noise compounds across depth as in our full method, the gain roughly doubles to 28% . The optimum sits at the same cell (ρ=0 .45 , δ =0 .0375) in both cases. Thus both effects contribute, with the propagated, depth-compounding term (ii) providing the majority of the benefit.

Improving upon sequential asymmetric correction. We compare the injected-noise proxy against the real sequential upstream error ηseq (Figure 6, right). With this error, the asymmetric corrections of GPTAQ (Li et al., 2025) and QEP (Arai & Ichikawa, 2025) are special cases of our objective: both fix the input noise scale to λ=1 and only tune the correction strength 1−ρ (Appendix C), which restricts them to the rightmost column of the landscape. Our framework instead treats λ as a free parameter. Along the column λ=1 , the best configuration reaches an average perplexity of 15 .2

(ρ ∈ { 0.5, 0.6}), whereas the optimum of our sweep at λ=0 .8, ρ =0 .4 reaches 15 .12 . Scaling down the upstream error ( λ< 1) thus improves upon the best asymmetric correction, even though both use the same sequential error. The flexibility of our objective therefore benefits not only parallel quantization with a noise proxy, but also sequential quantization. 

Why the optimum lies below the diagonal. We find the best configurations sit below the diagonal (ρ<λ ). This shape follows from the objective in Equation 2. On the diagonal ρ=λ, the first factor of F in Equation 3 equals G, so F=I and ˜W=W: the noise only changes the rounding metric G

and does not correct the weights. Off the diagonal, Theorem 2 shows that injected noise pulls each weight column toward scW:,c , where sc<1 if and only if ρ<λ . Below the diagonal the objective therefore shrinks the weights, which makes the layer more robust to input perturbations. Above the diagonal ( ρ>λ ) we instead have sc>1: the target carries more noise than the input, so the layer is asked to amplify the error and its weights grow. Proxy-model quantization. The random noise models a signal-independent input perturbation, pro-viding a principled regularization approach to input activation denoising. Here we provide additional results for a second noise model: we propose to estimate upstream error from a model quantized in parallel, which is particularly efficient when a previously quantized model is already available and can be reused to further improve the quantization quality. Concretely, we quantize an LLM once in a single parallel pass (for example with parallel GPTQ at the deployed bit-width), and estimate the noise from the difference of input activations between the full-precision and proxy-quantized models. Instead of expensive sequential quantization, the cost of this proxy model corresponds to an additional parallel quantization pass to obtain the proxy quantized model, which can then be used for denoising the input activations in a second parallel quantization pass. In practice one may quantize a dedicated proxy or just reuse an existing quantized model at no additional cost. In either case, the proxy model can provide a more accurate estimate of the deployed error than random noise injec-tion alone. The following results therefore include both random noise injection and proxy-model denoising results, which we find to be working best when combined. The results suggest that any existing parallel-GPTQ quantized model can be used as a proxy to improve the quantization quality in a second parallel pass with our activation denoising. Measurement details. All runtimes in this paper are wall-clock times measured on a single GPU. Our implementation runs parallel quantization on one GPU in two stages. First, it runs the calibration forward passes that collect the statistics of every layer (for our denoising, the clean pass and the noisy pass that accumulates the noise moments). Second, it computes each layer’s pretransform and rounds its weights. In the parallel schedule, the second stage of a layer depends only on the statistics of that layer, so all layers could be processed at the same time. On a single GPU, however, we process them one after another, so the measured time is the sum over all layers. The sequential schedule instead alternates between the two stages: each layer is rounded only after all previous layers are quantized, since its statistics depend on their quantized outputs. 

Single-GPU runtimes. Table 13 reports the mean per-run wall-clock time on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU, split into quantization (Quant) and perplexity evaluation (Eval), for each method row of the W3A16 tables. For our methods, Quant covers the full parallel pipeline (proxy quantization, mo-ment estimation, the weight pretransform, and rounding). Learned-rotation runs load a precomputed rotation, and we report the time for learning rotations separately in Appendix B. For Llama-2-13B, which is close to the memory of a single 40 GB A100, we use numerically exact CPU offloading of the residual activation streams for configurations that do not otherwise fit (the learned-rotation sequential GPTAQ), which adds a small overhead to the reported time. Table 14 reports the same breakdown for the QuIP# W2A16 sweeps, on a single A100 for Llama-3.2-1B/3B and Llama-3-8B and on a single H200 for Llama-2-13B, so the 13 B column is not directly comparable to the others. 

Overhead of denoising depends on the solver and the noise model. Gaussian-only denoising needs no preliminary quantization pass, but its noisy forward pass, moment estimation, and pre-transform add cost on a single GPU. How much this matters depends on the rounding solver. With GPTQ, rounding is cheap, so this overhead dominates: on Llama-3-8B, our denoising takes 29 .4

minutes, compared to 11 .6 for parallel GPTQ and 24 .1 for sequential GPTAQ. With QuIP#, round-ing is roughly an order of magnitude more expensive (e.g. ≈24 vs. ≈2.5 minutes on Llama-3.2-1B), so the same overhead is small in relative terms (e.g. 183 .2 vs. 173 .3 minutes for the sequential base-line on Llama-3-8B). The proxy-based variants (W3 and W3+Gauss rows) additionally require a preliminary parallel quantization pass, unless an existing quantized model is reused (Appendix A.3). In all cases, the per-layer processing remains independent. 

Projected multi-GPU time. We do not run multi-GPU quantization experiments. Instead, Figure 2 and Figure 25 report a projection computed from measured single-GPU QuIP# times. For these figures, we re-measure the Llama-2-13B times on a single A100, so that all models are timed on the same GPU. We split the measured quantization time into the time of the forward passes, Tfwd , and the time of the rounding, Tround , and approximate the time of a parallel method on N GPUs as 

Tpar (N ) = Tfwd + Tround /N. 

For the sequential schedule, we keep the single-GPU time, Tseq (N ) = Tseq (1) , since its layers cannot be rounded concurrently. Note that the projection assumes that the layers are split evenly across GPUs and ignores communication overhead, so it is an upper bound on the achievable speed-up. Since the rounding of different layers is independent given the collected statistics, distributing it across GPUs does not change the quantized model. Figure 25 shows the projection for Llama-2-13B (W2A16 QuIP#). On a single GPU, all three meth-ods take a comparable ≈290 –316 minutes. The projected time of the parallel methods decreases roughly as 1/N , so that on 8 GPUs our denoising takes ≈41 minutes compared to ≈306 for the sequential baseline (a ≈7.5× reduction), at a small overhead over parallel QuIP#. Across the mod-els in Figure 2, the projected reduction on 8 GPUs ranges from ≈7.1× (Llama-3.2-1B) to ≈7.5×

(Llama-2-13B).

C GPTAQ AND QEP AS SPECIAL CASES 

In this section, we relate our denoising objective to the two recent works that aim to correct for upstream quantization noise using sequential quantization: GPTAQ (Li et al., 2025) and QEP (Arai & Ichikawa, 2025). We show that both methods can be expressed as special cases of our denoising objective when passing the sequential upstream error, and we discuss the differences in how they motivate the correction strength β. We show the closed forms of both methods in Proposition 1, and then show that they are both special cases of our denoising objective in Corollary 1. C.1 BACKGROUND ON GPTAQ AND QEP 

GPTAQ (Li et al., 2025) is a sequential method that separates the two roles the input plays in the objective, min ˆW E∥W x − ˆW ˜x∥2: the target W x uses the clean activations x of the original model, while the quantized weights ˆW act on the perturbed activations ˜x propagated from the previously quantized layers. Each layer thus corrects for the upstream quantization noise it will actually receive, at the price of a sequential pass to measure ˜x.

QEP (Arai & Ichikawa, 2025) shares this goal but realizes the correction differently: rather than folding it into GPTQ’s column sweep, it first applies a closed-form (ordinary least-squares) pre-transform to the weights and then runs a standard GPTQ round on the transformed weights. GPTAQ and QEP solve the same problem. Both keep GPTQ’s damping ridge and additionally introduce a “correction strength” β ∈ [0 , 1] scaling the asymmetric correction: GPTAQ’s alpha ,fixed at 0.25 in its released code, and QEP’s perccorr , 0.5 by default.
