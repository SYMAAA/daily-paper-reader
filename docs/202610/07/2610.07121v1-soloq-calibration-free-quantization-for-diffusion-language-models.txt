ABSTRACT 

Diffusion large language models (dLLMs) have emerged as a promising alter-native to autoregressive language models through bidirectional diffusion-based token generation. However, their growing model sizes and high inference costs make efficient deployment challenging: full-sequence denoising repeatedly in-vokes compute-intensive forward passes, while block-diffusion models addi-tionally introduce a memory-intensive KV-cache. Low-bit weight–activation quantization is therefore attractive, yet existing dLLM post-training quantiza-tion methods rely on calibration data despite activation distributions shifting across masking states and denoising steps. We present SOLO Q, a calibration-free quantization framework that maps weights and activations into a nor-malized rotated basis with a predictable marginal distribution, enabling data-independent quantization. SOLO Q combines a structured K-RPBH rotation with a lightweight rescaling correction for calibration-free quantization. Its predictable post-rotation distribution supports both distribution-matched code-books and hardware-native NVFP4. For block-diffusion models, S OLO Q fur-ther applies commit-time KV-cache quantization to compress persistent states without perturbing the actively denoised block. Across full-sequence dLLMs (LLaDA and Dream) and block-diffusion dLLMs (Fast-dLLM v2 and Nemotron-Labs-Diffusion), S OLO Q retains accuracy under 4-bit quantization and outper-forms calibration-based baselines on knowledge- and reasoning-intensive bench-marks. With NVFP4, SOLO Q reduces peak memory by up to 2.61 × and accelerates end-to-end inference by up to 2.24 ×. The code is available at 

https://github.com/Intelligent-Computing-Lab-Panda/SoloQ .

1 INTRODUCTION 

Large language models (LLMs) have achieved remarkable progress across a wide range of lan-guage understanding, reasoning, and generation tasks (Brown et al., 2020; Achiam et al., 2023; Touvron et al., 2023; Team et al., 2023), with autoregressive (AR) modeling remaining the domi-nant paradigm for text generation. In parallel, diffusion models (Bao et al., 2023; Peebles & Xie, 2023) have become a powerful generative framework for continuous domains such as image and video generation (Esser et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2025; Kong et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2025), where iterative denoising enables high-quality and flexible generation. Motivated by these advances, re-cent diffusion large language models (dLLMs) extend diffusion-based generation to discrete text, replacing strictly left-to-right decoding with iterative masked denoising and bidirectional context. Representative models such as LLaDA (Nie et al., 2026) and Dream (Ye et al., 2025) have demon-strated competitive language understanding, reasoning, and generation capabilities, suggesting that strong language modeling is not inherently tied to AR decoding. More recently, block-diffusion models (Wu et al., 2026a; Fu et al., 2026; Team et al., 2026) further bridge diffusion-based gener-ation and practical inference by generating multiple tokens within a block and reusing previously generated context through caching. Together, these developments establish diffusion-based language modeling as a promising alternative to conventional AR generation. Despite this progress, efficient dLLM inference remains challenging as model size and context length grow. Unlike AR decoding, dLLMs iteratively refine multiple tokens in parallel across de-noising steps, while long-context inference increases the memory and computation associated with activations and KV-cache. At short contexts, weights dominate the memory footprint, but the relative contribution of activations and the KV-cache grows with context length. In terms of latency, attention becomes expensive as the sequence length grows. In dLLMs, these costs are repeatedly incurred across denoising steps, making weight-only compression insufficient for efficient inference. Instead, low-bit quantization of weights, activations, and KV-cache can reduce both computation and memory across context regimes. Post-training quantization (PTQ) has been widely studied for AR LLMs to reduce memory and com-putation without retraining. Existing LLM PTQ methods span several complementary paradigms, including second-order methods that exploit Hessian information for low-bit weight quantiza-tion (Frantar et al., 2022; Li et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2026b), scaling- and salience-based methods that mitigate activation outliers through scaling or reparameterization (Xiao et al., 2023; Wei et al., 2023; Shao et al., 2024), and rotation-based methods that transform weights and activations into more quantization-friendly representations (Chee et al., 2023; Ashkboos et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2025). These methods enable efficient low-bit inference while preserving accuracy. However, quantization on dLLMs is more challenging than AR LLMs. During diffusion decoding, the model repeatedly encounters different masking states and denoising steps, leading to substantial variation in activation distributions and quantization sensitivity throughout generation, as shown in Figure 1(b). Existing dLLM PTQ methods (Zhang et al., 2026; Xu & Yang, 2025; Yan et al., 2026; Huang et al., 2026) rely on calibration data to capture or compensate for these state- and time-dependent variations. Figure 1(c) illustrates this sensitivity for a representative calibration-based PTQ method (Ashkboos et al., 2024; Frantar et al., 2022): changing calibration data from WinoGrande to MMLU alters accuracy across the benchmarks, showing that calibration choices may not generalize across tasks. This motivates a different direction: rather than tracking a changing distribution, can we transform it into a predictable form that can be quantized without calibration? 

To realize this principle, we propose SOLO Q, a calibration-free dLLM quantization framework that avoids calibration-set dependence while maintaining strong performance, as shown in Figure 1(c). Rather than adapting to activation distributions that vary across masking states and denoising steps, SOLO Q transforms weights and activations into a normalized rotated space with a predictable dimension-dependent marginal distribution. This enables data-independent quantization that can be shared across diffusion states without calibration. To support arbitrary transformer dimensions, SOLO Q introduces a Kronecker-extended randomized permuted block-Hadamard (K-RPBH) rota-tion that combines efficient local mixing with global cross-block mixing. We further introduce a scale-only bias correction that restores the radial component of low-bit quantized representations with negligible overhead. For block-diffusion models, S OLO Q extends the same calibration-free design to persistent KV states through commit-time KV-cache quantization. Built on these com-ponents, S OLO Q supports both distribution-matched codebook quantization and hardware-native NVFP4 without chasing activation distributions. Our contributions are summarized as follows: • We introduce SOLO Q, a calibration-free dLLM quantization framework combining K-RPBH rotation with a lightweight scale-only bias correction. • The resulting single predictable distribution supports both distribution-matched codebooks and hardware-native NVFP4 within the same calibration-free framework. 2• We extend S OLO Q to block-diffusion models through commit-time KV-cache quantization, covering weights, activations, and persistent KV states. • Across full-sequence and block-diffusion dLLMs, S OLO Q achieves strong 4-bit accuracy with up to a 2.61 × reduction in peak memory and 2.24 × faster end-to-end inference. 

2 RELATED WORK 

2.1 DIFFUSION LARGE LANGUAGE MODELS 

Diffusion-based text generation has evolved from early discrete and masked diffusion mod-els (Austin et al., 2021a; Li et al., 2022; He et al., 2023) to large-scale diffusion language mod-els that perform iterative denoising with bidirectional context. Recent full-sequence models such as LLaDA (Nie et al., 2026), LLaDA-1.5 (Zhu et al., 2026), and Dream (Ye et al., 2025) demon-strate competitive language understanding, reasoning, and generation at billion-parameter scale. Beyond full-sequence diffusion, block-based and hybrid approaches improve decoding efficiency by generating multiple tokens per block and reusing previously generated context, including Block Diffusion (Arriola et al., 2025), Fast-dLLM (Wu et al., 2026b), Fast-dLLM v2 (Wu et al., 2026a), and LLaDA2.X (Bie et al., 2025; 2026). More recent systems further broaden this design space: Nemotron-Labs-Diffusion (Fu et al., 2026) unifies autoregressive, diffusion, and self-speculative decoding, Nemotron-TwoTower (Reda et al., 2026) separates autoregressive context modeling from diffusion denoising, and DiffusionGemma (Team et al., 2026) scales block-diffusion generation to a 26B mixture-of-experts model. These developments highlight the rapid progress of diffusion-based language modeling toward scalable and practical text generation. 2.2 CALIBRATION -F REE AND DATA -I NDEPENDENT QUANTIZATION 

A complementary line of research avoids calibration altogether by deriving quantizers from an-alytically known or data-independent distributions. TurboQuant (Zandieh et al., 2026) randomly rotates input vectors to induce a known coordinate distribution, enabling coordinate-wise optimal quantization. PolarQuant (Han et al., 2025) similarly exploits an analytically known distribution after random preconditioning and polar transformation for online vector and KV-cache quantiza-tion. OrbitQuant (Lee et al., 2026a) extends this principle to image and video diffusion transformers using randomized permuted block-Hadamard rotations for calibration-free weight–activation quan-tization. ConvRot (Huang et al., 2025) applies group-wise Hadamard rotations for W4A4 diffusion-transformer quantization, while DVD-Quant (Li et al., 2026) combines data-free grid refinement, rotation, and adaptive bit-width allocation for video diffusion transformers. S OLO Q builds on this distributional perspective for diffusion language models, extending calibration-free quantization to arbitrary transformer dimensions and persistent KV states in block-diffusion models. 2.3 POST -T RAINING QUANTIZATION FOR D LLM S

Recent work has begun to develop PTQ methods specifically for diffusion language models. Quant-dLLM (Zhang et al., 2026) introduces masked calibration simulation for timestep-dependent mask-ing and targets extreme low-bit weight quantization. DLLMQuant (Xu & Yang, 2025) accounts for temporal and masking variations for weight–activation quantization, while STaR-Quant (Yan et al., 2026) addresses state-dependent activation disparity and temporal error accumulation. FAIR-Calib (Huang et al., 2026) improves calibration by emphasizing fragile token-commitment states. Despite their designs, existing dLLM PTQ methods remain calibration-based and focus on full-sequence diffusion models. To our knowledge, S OLO Q is the first calibration-free framework to sup-port KV-cache quantization for block-diffusion models while also covering full-sequence dLLMs.

4 METHOD 

4.1 OVERVIEW 

Rather than tracking activation distributions that shift across masking states and denoising steps, SOLO Q transforms representations into a predictable rotated space for low-bit quantization. It applies K-RPBH for global mixing (Section 4.2), followed by distribution-matched codebooks (S OLO Q-C) or hardware-native NVFP4 (S OLO Q-N) (Section 4.3) and a lightweight scale-only cor-rection (Section 4.4). For block-diffusion models, S OLO Q further extends this design to persistent KV states through commit-time quantization (Section 4.5). Figure 2 illustrates the overall pipeline. 4.2 K-RPBH R OTATION 

Rotation plays a key role in calibration-free quantization by mapping normalized representations to a predictable, approximately Gaussian coordinate distribution, as shown in Eq. 3. RPBH, introduced 4in OrbitQuant (Lee et al., 2026a), achieves this efficiently, but has two limitations for heavy-tailed dLLM representations. (1) Outlier-sensitive block balancing: RPBH relies on the initial permuta-tion to balance the energy assigned to each block, and its variance-concentration guarantee weakens as the largest input coordinate grows. (2) No cross-block correction: Once the permutation is fixed, each Hadamard transform operates independently within its block, so any residual block-energy im-balance cannot be redistributed across blocks. This effect is particularly relevant for dimensions composed of many Hadamard blocks, such as Dream’s feed-forward network (FFN) dimension 

d = 18944 = 512 × 37 .To address these limitations, we introduce the Kronecker-extended randomized permuted block-Hadamard (K-RPBH) rotation,

Low-bit quantization can distort both the direction and magnitude of a representation, particu-larly with centroid-based quantizers such as Lloyd–Max and, to a lesser extent, finite-precision formats such as NVFP4. Related scalar corrections have been used for unbiased compression in DRIVE (Vargaftik et al., 2021) and EDEN (Vargaftik et al., 2022), and for unbiased inner-product estimation in RaBitQ (Gao & Long, 2024). 4.5 BLOCK -D IFFUSION KV-C ACHE QUANTIZATION 

Full-sequence dLLMs such as LLaDA (Nie et al., 2026; Zhu et al., 2026) and Dream (Ye et al., 2025) recompute KV states throughout denoising and therefore do not maintain a persistent KV-cache. In contrast, block-diffusion models (Bie et al., 2026; Fu et al., 2026; Wu et al., 2026a) reuse the KV-cache of previously committed blocks, causing the persistent cache to grow with context length. As shown in Figure 1(a), this growth creates a significant memory bottleneck at long context lengths. SOLO Q quantizes the KV-cache only when a block is committed, as illustrated in Figure 2(c). The active denoising block remains in full precision, avoiding repeated quantization across denoising steps. Keys use KIVI-style per-channel asymmetric quantization (Liu et al., 2024), while values follow the S OLO Q activation path with K-RPBH and either Lloyd–Max or NVFP4 quantization. This commit-time design avoids repeatedly quantizing the KV states of the actively denoised block while compressing only persistent states. The overall algorithm of S OLO Q is shown in Appendix B.

6 ANALYSIS 

6.1 ROTATION STRUCTURE COMPARISON 

We first compare alternative rotations, QuIP (Chee et al., 2023), QuaRot (Ashkboos et al., 2024), Haar (Zandieh et al., 2026), and RPBH (Lee et al., 2026a), under the same S OLO Q-N NVFP4 setting, as shown in Table 3. Rotation latency is measured for a single activation-rotation invocation on an NVIDIA H200 under each model’s native evaluation workload. Dense Haar provides strong accuracy but incurs substantially higher cost. K-RPBH retains comparable accuracy while reducing rotation latency from 98.135 ms to 5.779 ms on Fast-dLLM v2, and from 0.559 ms to 0.048 ms on LLaDA-1.5. Compared with RPBH, K-RPBH consistently improves downstream accuracy, showing that cross-block mixing provides a better accuracy–efficiency trade-off. 6.2 K-RPBH C OMPONENT ABLATION 

We next isolate the contributions of the randomized permutation P and cross-block mixer Qk in K-RPBH (Eq. 4), as shown in Table 4. For a token representation x ∈ Rd partitioned into k blocks of size h = d/k , we define the energy of block j after permutation as Ej (x) = ∥(P x )(j)∥22, whose uniform target is ∥x∥22/k . We measure block-energy imbalance as the median absolute relative deviation from this target, Median x,j kE j (x)/∥x∥22 − 1 . For K-RPBH, the corresponding block energies are computed after cross-block mixing by Qk. Lower imbalance indicates more uniform energy across blocks, while lower KL divergence indicates a closer match to the target post-rotation marginal. Starting from Block-RHT, adding P reduces KL from 0.114 to 0.0468 and energy im-balance from 67.89% to 45.64%. Adding only Qk is more effective at redistribution, reducing KL to 0.0068 and imbalance to 36.24%. Combining both further improves the metrics to 0.0041 and 22.12%, respectively, confirming their complementary benefits. 6.3 EFFECT OF SCALE -O NLY BIAS CORRECTION 

We further study the effect of the scale-only bias correction in Figure 5. Across all input-token mask-ing ratios on LLaDA-1.5, the correction reduces the KL divergence between the full-precision and quantized logit distributions, indicating improved distribution matching. This translates to higher downstream accuracy for both S OLO Q-C and S OLO Q-N: HellaSwag improves from 72.49/73.71 to 73.01/73.86 on LLaDA-1.5, while GPQA improves from 30.03/31.92 to 30.58/33.71 on Fast-dLLM v2. Overall, the correction improves quantization fidelity with negligible overhead. 6.4 KV-C ACHE QUANTIZATION COMPARISON 

Finally, we compare S OLO Q with existing KV-cache quantization methods using 2-bit KV quanti-zation at approximately 3 bits-per-entry (BPE), including scale and metadata overhead, in Table 5, 9while keeping weights and activations in FP16. SOLO Q-C and S OLO Q-N achieve average accu-racies of 53.31 and 53.53, outperforming KIVI (Liu et al., 2024), OScar (Su et al., 2026), and TurboQuant (Zandieh et al., 2026). S OLO Q-C performs best on GSM8K, while S OLO Q-N achieves the highest IFEval accuracy, highlighting their complementary strengths. These results show that SOLO Q effectively compresses the KV cache while preserving downstream accuracy. 

7 CONCLUSION 

We present S OLO Q, a calibration-free quantization framework for diffusion language models. By transforming weights and activations into a predictable rotated representation, S OLO Q enables data-independent low-bit quantization without calibration samples or pre-collected activation statistics. The framework supports both distribution-matched codebooks and hardware-native NVFP4, while commit-time KV-cache quantization extends the approach to block-diffusion models. Across full-sequence and block-diffusion dLLMs, S OLO Q achieves robust W4A4 quantization and remains competitive with or outperforms existing calibration-based baselines across a broad range of lan-guage benchmarks. These results establish predictable representation shaping as a general founda-tion for efficient low-bit dLLM inference. Limitations are discussed in Appendix G.

D.3 FOLDED ROTATION FOR EFFICIENT DEPLOYMENT 

Online activation rotation introduces additional inference overhead, motivating us to examine whether the residual-stream rotation can be folded into model weights offline. This is difficult in im-age or video diffusion transformers such as those targeted by OrbitQuant (Lee et al., 2026a), which commonly employ adaptive normalization such as AdaLN (Peebles & Xie, 2023). Let x ∈ Rd be a hidden state, W ∈ Rm×d a linear weight, and Π ∈ Rd×d an orthogonal K-RPBH rotation. We use 

N (·) to denote RMS normalization without its learned channel-wise gain. AdaLN can be written as 

AdaLN( x; c) = diag( γ(c)) N (x) + β(c), (21) where the scale γ(c) and shift β(c) depend on the diffusion condition c. Because these parameters vary at runtime, the residual-stream rotation cannot generally be absorbed into fixed adjacent weights and remains online. In contrast, the dLLMs considered in S OLO Q use a static RMSNorm gain γ.Since orthogonal rotations preserve the RMS norm, the full-precision operation can be equivalently written as 

W diag( γ)N (x) = W diag( γ)Π ⊤

QW (W Π⊤)QA(Π diag( γ)N (x)) ̸ = QW (Wfold )QA(N (Π x)) . (23) Tables 9 and 10 quantify this trade-off. Although folding reduces online rotation cost, it introduces model-dependent accuracy degradation, with a relatively modest drop on LLaDA and a substantially larger drop on Fast-dLLM v2. In contrast, under the more aggressive W4A4KV2 setting, preserving the sink becomes beneficial. This indicates that sink-aware protection is mainly important at ultra-low KV precision, while standard 4-bit KV quantization can generally quantize the sink together with the remaining cache without noticeable degradation. We implement a systolic-array accelerator with custom BF16 and NVFP4 datapaths on a Zynq Ul-traScale+ ZCU104 FPGA and evaluate LLaDA-Base-8B for edge dLLM inference. Fig. 7(a) shows the hardware setup. The Zynq MPSoC consists of a processing system (PS) and programmable logic 22 (PL), each with access to external DDR4 memory. The model weights are distributed across the PS and PL DDR4 memories, while activations are stored in the PL DDR4. As shown in Fig. 7(b), the systolic-array accelerator is implemented in the PL and receives ex-ecution instructions and a portion of the model weights from the PS. The systolic array comprises multiply-and-accumulate (MAC) units together with input (IP), weight (W), and output (OP) buffers to perform tiled matrix multiplication on data tiles fetched from the PL DDR4. Fig. 7(c) summarizes the design specifications of the hardware accelerator. For the NVFP4 acceler-ator, we double the compute capacity because NVFP4 enables higher compute density than BF16. Fig. 8 shows the power breakdown of the accelerators during LLaDA-Base-8B inference, where the compute logic, DDR memories, PS processor, and clock generation and routing are the major contributors. The NVFP4 accelerator consumes 15% less power while providing twice the compute throughput of the BF16 design. As shown in Table 12, S OLO Q’s NVFP4 accelerator achieves a

G LIMITATIONS 

SOLO Q introduces online activation rotation to transform representations into the K-RPBH basis. Although weight rotations are performed offline, activation-side rotations incur additional inference overhead. Folding these rotations into adjacent weights can reduce this cost, but, as shown in Ap-pendix D.3, the folded topology may degrade accuracy under low-bit quantization, particularly for block-diffusion models. In addition, S OLO Q-C uses non-uniform codebooks whose centroids are represented losslessly in INT8 for efficient computation. Consequently, its low-bit representation cannot directly exploit native 4-bit Tensor Core execution and instead relies on 8-bit kernels, result-ing in lower speedups than the hardware-native NVFP4 path of S OLO Q-N. Finally, the full latency benefit of S OLO Q-N depends on hardware support for native NVFP4 computation; on devices with-out such support, its practical acceleration may be reduced. 26
