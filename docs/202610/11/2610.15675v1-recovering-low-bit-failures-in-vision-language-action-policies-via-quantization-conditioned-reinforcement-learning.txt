Abstract — Quantization lowers deployment costs of vision– language–action (VLA) policies but can compromise task com-pletion. Across five VLA models and 4,050 real-world trials, we systematically reveal that quantization-aware training (QAT) generally improves on post-training quantization (PTQ) but leaves substantial gaps to full precision (FP) on long-horizon tasks and under visual and semantic shifts, while its imitation-based objective does not explicitly use rollout success or partial completion. To exploit these outcome signals, we introduce Quantization-Conditioned Reinforcement Learning (QCRL), an offline RL method coupling progress-based advantage estima-tion with quantization-aware source conditioning. FP rollouts supply reference experience beyond action-level distillation, progress-scaled terminal penalty distinguishes partial comple-tion, and quantization-aware conditioning selects advantageous reference behavior within the quantized policy. We evaluate weight-only INT4 W4A16 on π0.5 and GR00T N1.5 and joint weight–activation NVFP4 W4A4 on π0.5. Using only in-distribution data, QCRL reaches 99.3% of FP’s mean score across three visual-shift conditions for π0.5 with INT4 W4A16, alongside 42.9% model-memory savings. With NVFP4 W4A4, it reaches 100.7% of the same FP baseline, alongside a 1.73 ×

speedup using NVFP4/FP8 deployment. Hardware gains are relative to FP16 on Jetson Thor. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Vision–language–action (VLA) policies integrate visual observations, language instructions, and action generation, but their scale makes deployment on resource-constrained robots costly [1]–[4]. Quantization offers two complementary deployment benefits: lower memory use and faster inference. Weight-only quantization reduces model memory while re-taining high-precision activations [5], as in Core ML’s 4-bit weight compression with FP16 activations on the Apple Neural Engine [6]. Joint weight–activation quantization also accelerates inference through low-precision matrix multipli-cation [7]; NVFP4 W4A4 enables 4-bit weight and activation computation on Blackwell devices such as Jetson Thor [8]. These efficiency gains motivate quantized deployment, but preserving successful robot control remains a concern: action deviations can alter contact, subsequent observations, and task completion. To identify where quantization compro-mises execution and where QAT recovery falls short, we conduct a comprehensive comparative study of full-precision

(FP), post-training quantization (PTQ), and quantization-aware training (QAT) policies across 4,050 real-world tri-als and five VLA models [9]. Despite strong recovery on short tasks, QAT leaves severe long-horizon completion fail-ures and substantial visual and semantic out-of-distribution (OOD) gaps due to QAT’s shortsighted step-wise guidance. These findings motivate learning from long-term rollout success and partial completion, signals not explicitly used by the evaluated imitation-based QAT objective. To embody these findings for low-bit recovery, we propose Quantization-Conditioned Reinforcement Learning (QCRL), a new offline RL method coupling progress-based advan-tage estimation with source-conditioned behavior selection (Fig. 1). FP rollout augmentation collects reference trajec-tories beyond action-level distillation, without new expert demonstrations. A progress-scaled terminal penalty incor-porates partial completion into advantage estimates, dif-ferentiating failed rollouts. Quantization-aware conditioning 

distinguishes reference and quantized behavior, making ad-vantageous reference behavior separately selectable. We evaluate INT4 W4A16 on π0.5 and GR00T N1.5, and NVFP4 W4A4 on π0.5. Across appearance, illumination, and sensor-degradation shifts, in-distribution (ID)-only QCRL adaptation raises π0.5’s mean task score from QAT’s 77.5% to 99.3% of FP under W4A16, alongside 42.9% model-memory savings. Under NVFP4 W4A4, the corresponding score rises from 87.0% to 100.7% of FP, while NVFP4/FP8 deployment achieves a 1.73 × speedup. Hardware gains are measured against FP16 on Jetson Thor. Ablations and an FP adaptation control assess component contributions and recovery beyond general adaptation. II. B ACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK

Quantization maps a weight or activation x to a low-bit representation xQ, reconstructed as ex:

xQ = RF (x/s + z), ex = s(xQ − z),

where s > 0 is the scale, z the zero point, and RF rounds to the nearest value in format F, saturating at its limits. Weight-only quantization reduces parameter storage and memory traffic [5]; INT4 W4A16 uses uniform four-bit integer weights and 16-bit floating-point activations. ONNX Runtime defaults to round-to-nearest (RTN) for blockwise four-bit MatMul weight quantization [14]. Joint weight– activation quantization also enables low-precision matrix multiplication on supported hardware. NVFP4 W4A4 uses E2M1 weights and activations, with z = 0 , FP8 block scales, and an FP32 tensor scale [15], [16]. Blackwell-based Jetson Thor supports FP4 Tensor Core computation for efficient deployment [8]. Post-training quantization (PTQ) converts trained pa-rameters without retraining but can degrade accuracy. VLA-specific methods include QuantVLA’s scale calibra-tion and QVLA’s action-sensitive bit allocation [13], [17]. Quantization-aware training (QAT) can recover accuracy more effectively than PTQ by adapting parameters under quantized forward passes [7], [18]. Saliency-aware quantized imitation learning (SQIL) adds saliency-weighted FP distil-lation, improving robustness over PTQ in evaluated robotic settings [9]. However, systematic evaluation of PTQ and QAT across diverse VLA models, real-world task difficulties, and distribution shifts remains limited. In Sec. III, we evaluate SQIL and calibration-free RTN across five VLA models under a shared real-world protocol to characterize QAT’s recovery and its limits. 

B. Offline Policy Improvement 

Offline policy improvement reuses demonstrations and rollout outcomes without new robot interactions during op-timization, while accounting for data–policy mismatch [19]. Advantage-weighted regression (AWR) emphasizes actions with higher estimated advantage [20], while implicit Q-learning (IQL) avoids evaluating unseen actions and extracts policies through advantage-weighted behavioral cloning [21]. RECAP improves VLAs from demonstrations, autonomous rollouts, and human corrections through advantage condi-tioning [22]. D. Implications for Offline Policy Improvement 

QAT leaves severe deficits in long-horizon completion and substantial gaps under visual and semantic shifts. SQIL trains on recorded ID observations using action supervision and FP-output distillation, but does not explicitly use whether the quantized policy completes the task or completes only some stages. Offline RL can use these outcomes to estimate action advantages from successful, failed, and partially com-pleted trajectories. This motivates QCRL, which uses rollout feedback to improve task execution while retaining low-bit inference. IV. Q UANTIZATION -C ONDITIONED REINFORCEMENT 

LEARNING 

To address the residual failures in Section III, QCRL extends RECAP’s advantage-conditioned learning through three changes: FP rollouts augment the reference data, partial completion refines the terminal reward, and a source condi-tion makes reference behavior selectable within a quantized policy (Fig. 1). QCRL couples progress-based advantage labeling with source-conditioned policy extraction, enabling a quantized policy to learn from deployment failures while selecting reference behavior at inference.

(5) SQIL uses FP outputs as distillation targets, whereas QCRL collects FP rollouts as offline RL data, using an FP policy with the same backbone, action representation, and task training. This adds reference trajectories that grant high proximity without new expert demonstrations. Section IV-D validates the benefits of high proximity to rollout quality. 

B. Progress-Scaled Terminal Penalty 

RECAP assigns the same terminal penalty to every failed rollout (Eq. (1)). Yet low-bit failures can occur after useful execution: a folding policy may complete the first fold and fail on the second. A binary terminal outcome disregards this progress in the failure penalty. For each trajectory in Dmix ,let p(τ ) ∈ [0 , 1] be the fraction of required subgoals com-pleted. Different from RECAP, QCRL defines the terminal reward as: 

rT (τ ) = −λf (1 − p(τ )) . (6) For example, completing three of four subgoals incurs 

−λf /4 rather than −λf ; full completion receives zero termi-nal reward. Thus, failed rollouts can receive higher returns when they complete more subgoals. Progress adjusts the terminal penalty rather than supplying a dense reward at every subgoal. Using this progress-scaled terminal penalty, we fit Vψ to these return-to-go targets and compute N -step advantages and labels It as in Section II-B. The value targets now distinguish degrees of task completion even when full suc-cesses are scarce, allowing partial execution to inform policy improvement. Per-transition advantages still determine the labels, so a partially successful trajectory is not automatically labeled positive throughout.

A.1. Experimental Protocol and Implementation Details 

This appendix expands the evaluation protocol and pro-vides the task-level evidence behind the main paper. The advantage-conditioned objective is given in Section II-B; the robot setup, baseline schedules, and QCRL data and optimization settings are given in Sections III-A and V-A and Tables I and IV. We refer to those descriptions for settings already specified in the main text. 

a) Data collection and task grouping.: The same Trossen ViperX tabletop platform and base/wrist camera setup are used for supervised demonstration collection, FP and quantized rollout collection, and real-world evaluation. The three short-horizon tasks are combined into one adapted policy because they share reaching, grasping, moving, plac-ing, and releasing primitives. Cloth folding and clean desk use separate policies because their ordered subgoal struc-tures differ. The fixed mixture contains demonstrations and autonomous rollouts, including unsuccessful and partially completed episodes. No visual-shift or semantic-shift obser-vations are added during adaptation; those settings are used only to evaluate transfer from ID training. 

b) Visual shifts.: Appearance changes replace the cloth color in sweeping and the background in pick-and-place and stacking. Illumination changes reduce image bright-ness/contrast, and sensor degradation blurs observations. Dis-tractor settings introduce additional task-irrelevant objects. The first three shift families are evaluated on all three short tasks; distractors are evaluated on pick-and-place and stacking. These choices explain the three-task versus two-task averages in the main paper. Table A1 summarizes the additional evaluation conditions; representative observations appear in Fig. 2. 

c) Object and instruction shifts.: The pick-and-place evaluation separately replaces the manipulated object (corn with carrot) and the recipient (plate with pot). Role reversal exchanges the instructed roles of the pink and blue cups. Added-context prompts modify the language input while pre-serving the target manipulation: the pick-and-place prompt adds a preliminary action and the stacking prompt adds task-focus context. Their exact wording is given in Table A2; the five original ID prompts are already listed in Table V. 

d) Reading semantic metrics.: Primitive progress mea-sures execution of task-specific manipulation stages and need not imply correct selection of the instruction-specified object or role. Likewise, the added-context scores do not separately certify every language clause. Null-input scores record task-like manipulation despite the absence of a valid task in-struction; higher scores are not better instruction following, and lower scores alone do not establish a learned abstention policy. We retain these diagnostics separately from the valid-instruction results. 

e) Progress and offline learning.: Final partial comple-tion affects only the trajectory’s terminal penalty, rather than supplying a dense reward at each milestone. The value model is fitted before policy training, and its fixed estimates deter-mine the transition-level advantage labels. Consequently, par-tial trajectory success does not make every transition positive. The source condition identifies data provenance rather than arithmetic precision: demonstrations and FP rollouts form the reference group, and the deployed policy remains quantized when selecting reference behavior. All value learning, return computation, and source annotation take place offline. 

A.2. Low-Bit Quantization and Additional Training Config-uration 

The comparative tables in this appendix concern INT4 W4A16, with group-wise asymmetric four-bit language-model weights and group size 128. The vision and action modules retain their original precision. This scope differs from the NVFP4/FP8 experiments in the main paper; the latter are not represented by the additional INT4 tables below. The general quantizer and deployment-format definitions are given in Section II. PTQ applies weight quantization after task training. QAT simulates quantized weights during adaptation so that train-able parameters can accommodate the low-bit representation; the evaluated SQIL objective additionally uses saliency-guided FP-to-quantized distillation [9]. QCRL retains the quantized policy while using rollout returns and advantage labels for policy improvement. Thus, the extra adaptation changes how the policy is trained, while the runtime preci-sion remains shared across methods within each format.

B.4. Interpretation and Scope 

For the auxiliary source itself, the lower bound is positive when Ss > 2Amax T (T − 1) α2 

> s

: beneficial actions at PTQ states must outweigh the possible effect of changing the state distribution. High return under the source’s own occupancy does not by itself establish this condition. For the learned policy, the reference benefit must additionally exceed the ex-traction error in Eq. (11). The horizon dependence indicates why small deviations can become consequential in longer executions. The corresponding FP policy is a practical source can-didate because it shares the backbone, action representa-tion, and task training of the PTQ policy while providing better rollout outcomes in our evaluated settings. An ex-ternal expert can have high task performance yet produce substantially different actions. These observations motivate source selection, but do not establish the theorem’s uniform assumptions. Similarly, source conditioning makes selected reference behavior separately representable; the bound does not prove that conditioning minimizes approximation error or is optimal. The analysis uses exact PTQ advantages and a reference policy defined throughout the state space. Training instead uses fitted advantages and a finite offline dataset. Neither these fitted labels nor the action-distance diagnostics below certify Sr , αr , or ϵBC . The bound is a design guide, while Section V-D evaluates the contributions of FP rollout aug-mentation, progress-based rewards, and source conditioning experimentally.
