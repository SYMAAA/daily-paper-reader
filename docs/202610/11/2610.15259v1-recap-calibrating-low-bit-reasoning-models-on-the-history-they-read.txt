# ABSTRACT 

Low-bit quantization reduces the cost of serving reasoning models but can sub-stantially degrade long chains of thought. Each step reads cached keys and values spanning thousands of tokens, allowing quantization errors to accumu-late along the trajectory. Standard calibration operates on short segments that omit the earlier context needed by later reasoning steps. Even when this con-text is retained, cache reconstruction error alone does not capture how quanti-zation errors affect subsequent attention. We present RECAP, a post-training quantization method that calibrates reasoning models on the history they read. Dynamic-prefix calibration separates long historical context from short-block reconstruction. It regenerates prefix keys and values with the current quan-tizer and rotates the active block to supervise every position along the trajec-tory. Prefix read geometry further supervises historical cache errors according to their effects on attention outputs, weighting key and value errors asymmet-rically. With both components applied only during calibration, RECAP outper-forms the baseline across multiple reasoning models and quantization settings. Under W4A4KV4, RECAP improves accuracy over the baseline by 13.3 percent-age points on MATH500 with DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B and 15.8 points on AIME-120 with Qwen3-4B. These results highlight the importance of pre-serving historical dependencies during calibration for low-bit reasoning. Project repository: https://github.com/AI-Efficiency/RECAP .

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Reasoning language models solve complex problems by generating extended sequences of interme-diate steps (Wei et al., 2022; DeepSeek-AI, 2025; Qwen Team, 2025). These capabilities come with substantial inference costs, making low-bit quantization an attractive approach to deployment (Fran-tar et al., 2023; Lin et al., 2024; Xiao et al., 2023). However, low-bit quantization can substantially degrade reasoning performance (Liu et al., 2025a; Lv et al., 2026; Lee et al., 2026). Long-chain reasoning relies on repeated access to a growing history of intermediate states. Quantization er-rors in cached keys and values can distort attention outputs at later reasoning steps. As shown in Figure 1b,c, quantization can reduce answer accuracy while increasing generation length. This motivates calibration that preserves the historical dependencies underlying long-chain reasoning. Existing post-training quantization methods seek to preserve model accuracy by improving the fi-delity of quantized representations. QuaRot (Ashkboos et al., 2024) suppresses activation outliers through rotations, while FlatQuant (Sun et al., 2025) learns affine transformations through layer-output reconstruction. However, for reasoning models with low-bit weights, activations, and KV caches, calibration effectiveness also depends on how well it captures the role of earlier quantized states in later computation. Layer-output reconstruction captures these interactions within the sup-plied historical context and at the positions being reconstructed. Improving representation fidelity within this scope does not by itself ensure that longer historical dependencies are preserved across the trajectory. These considerations motivate examining which history calibration exposes, which reader positions it supervises, and how historical errors affect subsequent attention.

First, the cache-importance scores we tested transfer poorly across reasoning trajectories, limiting their usefulness for selecting which states to preserve. Calibration should therefore retain the historical context itself rather than a selected subset. Retaining this context raises a second challenge, which is to balance historical context, position coverage, and computational cost. Short windows omit relevant history, reconstructing only the final segment leaves earlier positions unsupervised, and reconstructing the full sequence at every update is costly. Third, even with adequate context and coverage, cache errors of similar magnitude can distort attention to different degrees, so raw cache reconstruction error remains an incomplete measure of their effect on later computation. To address these challenges, we introduce RECAP (REad-geometry CAlibration with dynamic Pre-fixes), a post-training quantization framework that calibrates reasoning models on the history they read. Dynamic-prefix calibration addresses history and position coverage by allowing each short reconstruction block to read a long prefix regenerated with the current quantizer. Rotating the active block along the trajectory extends supervision to every position without reconstructing the entire sequence at each update. With this coverage in place, prefix read geometry further accounts for how errors in the retained history affect subsequent attention. It penalizes historical key and value er-rors according to their effects on attention outputs, complementing block reconstruction with direct supervision of the history being read. Together, these components preserve historical dependencies during calibration without adding inference-time token selection or precision allocation. We evaluate RECAP across multiple rea-soning models and quantization settings on mathematical reasoning, scientific question answering, and code generation. RECAP consistently matches or improves on the baseline. Under W4A4KV4, accuracy gains reach up to 13.3 percentage points on MATH500 and 15.8 points on AIME-120. Our contributions are as follows: • Motivation analysis. We show that calibrating low-bit reasoning models is limited by historical dependencies: cache-importance scores transfer across trajectories at near-chance level, short-window and final-segment calibration each fail on the positions they leave uncovered, and cache errors of equal size affect later attention unequally. • Dynamic-prefix calibration. We decouple the context used for calibration from the positions being reconstructed. At each update, the historical keys and values are regenerated with the current quantizer, and only a short block is reconstructed; this block is shifted along the trajectory across updates. Calibration therefore observes the long quantized history encountered at inference, and every position is supervised at the cost of short-block reconstruction. • Prefix read geometry. We introduce an auxiliary cache objective derived from the local sensitivity of the attention output. Key errors are weighted by their projection onto query directions, and value errors by the attention they receive. Historical context, position coverage, and cost. Figure 2(b) illustrates a trade-off in calibrating long reasoning sequences. Short windows can omit earlier premises that later steps depend on, preventing calibration from capturing how those steps use the preceding history. Extending the window restores access to more of this context, but reconstructing every position requires more computation per update as the sequence grows longer. Long reasoning trajectories, therefore, make full-sequence reconstruction costly. Supplying a long prefix while reconstructing only the final segment restores history, but leaves earlier query positions outside the objective. In teacher-forced diagnostics over an 8K context, a model calibrated on 4K windows diverges from the FP model late in the sequence, with KL rising from 0.14 nats/token in the first 1K positions to 7.33 in the last 1K. A model that reconstructs only positions 4K–8K with a 4K prefix fails in the opposite region, reaching 6.58 in the first 1K and 0.17 in the last. The challenge is to preserve the historical information needed by later steps and supervise every position without reconstructing the entire sequence in every update. 

Unequal influence of cache errors. Figure 2(c) illustrates how quantization errors of the same size can have different effects on the current reasoning step. The current query reads the cached history with unequal attention weights. In the example, it relies more heavily on the step r2 = 9 than on the connecting word “so”, so errors at these positions can contribute differently to the attention output. Measuring cache error alone does not capture this difference (Zhou et al., 2026). Although layer-output reconstruction captures these effects at supervised positions, an additional cache loss should also reflect how later queries read the history. These observations suggest three requirements for calibration. It should retain the historical context that later steps read, extend supervision across the trajectory without reconstructing the full sequence at every update, and account for how cache errors affect subsequent attention outputs. 4Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2027

The block loss in Equation 6 measures errors at active outputs. Although this loss already reaches the prefix K/V through gradients, that signal is indirect: it is mixed with weight and activation errors at the active outputs and reflects only the queries of the current block. Its metric is collected once from the original FP target model and frozen before layerwise calibration.

# 4 EXPERIMENTS 

We first compare a range of models and quantization methods across diverse benchmarks, then examine paired generation behavior. We assess both task accuracy and generation length to charac-terize the effects of quantization on reasoning. Ablation studies assess historical-prefix length and the contribution of read geometry beyond MSE. 4.1 SETUP 

Models and benchmarks. We compare DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B/7B and Qwen3-4B, us-ing BF16 as the unquantized reference and QuaRot and FlatQuant as quantization baselines. We evaluate MATH500 (Hendrycks et al., 2021; Lightman et al., 2023), AIME-120, GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021), GPQA-Diamond (Rein et al., 2023), and LiveCodeBench (Jain et al., 2024), covering mathematical reasoning, scientific question answering, and code generation. We report task scores throughout and mean generation length in the MATH500 and AIME-120 ablations. 

Quantization and calibration. We evaluate W4A4KV4 and W4A8KV4 with symmetric per-channel weights, asymmetric per-token activations, asymmetric KV groups of 128, and 16-bit queries. Calibration uses target-model FP trajectories; the 1.5B ablation pool contains 1,000 NuminaMath-1.5 traces (Li et al., 2024). The default uses 64 fit trajectories, a 12K maximum prefix, a rotating 4K block (16K total; 1K = 1,024 tokens), and 32 epochs (512 updates/layer). AdamW uses base learning rate 5 × 10 −3, weight decay 0.01, and cosine decay. We fix αℓ at 0.1 times the lower median block-to-auxiliary gradient-norm ratio, computed at initialization over the first eight fit samples and all nonempty prefixes. The auxiliary multiplier is zero for the first 10% of updates, rises linearly to one over the next 10%, and then stays at one. 

Evaluation protocol. Local generation uses temperature 0.6, top-p 0.95, and a 32,768-token out-put limit. AIME-120 combines 90 problems from 2022–2024 and 30 from 2025. Scores are per-centages, averaged equally across the five benchmarks. In the ablations, MATH500 and AIME-120 scores are averaged over three evaluations per configuration. 4.2 MAIN RESULTS

Consistent gains over FlatQuant. RECAP matches or exceeds FlatQuant on every model, bit setting, and benchmark in Table 1; the only tie is R1-7B on GSM8K under W4A4KV4 (90.9%). Relative to BF16, RECAP retains 89.0–91.2% of the average score under W4A4KV4, compared with 78.0–82.6% for FlatQuant, closing 41–58% of FlatQuant’s remaining gap. Across all six model–precision combinations, RECAP achieves the highest average score among the quantized methods. 

Gains concentrate on long multi-step reasoning. The largest improvements appear on AIME-120, MATH500, and LiveCodeBench, which require long reasoning chains or code generation. Effect of prefix length. Table 2 compares 8K and 12K prefixes with a 4K active block on R1-1.5B under W4A4KV4. This increases total calibration coverage from 12K to 16K while retaining short-block reconstruction, so later query blocks can access a longer quantized history. For RECAP, the 12K configuration improves reported MATH500 and AIME-120 accuracy by 1.2 and 1.6 percentage points, respectively, while reducing mean generation length by 14.3% and 10.8%. Block MSE follows the same trend. These results favor the 12K configuration, although differences in calibration cohorts and update budgets limit attribution to prefix length alone. 

Effect of read geometry. Block MSE already retains the prefix; read geometry adds direct su-pervision of historical K/V according to their effects on subsequent attention outputs. Key errors alter attention weights, whereas value errors perturb the content being aggregated. The reported read-geometry runs use statistics collected from the original FP model. Across both prefix lengths, RECAP improves reported accuracy by 1.0–1.6 points on MATH500 and 2.3–3.3 points on AIME-120, with 4.8–11.8% fewer generated tokens. The gains therefore combine higher accuracy with shorter generation. These point estimates do not establish statistical superiority, and decoding dif-ferences further limit causal attribution at 12K.

Lengths are re-tokenized from saved outputs, with the dashed line marking the nominal 32K budget. Of the 101 questions that change from correct to incorrect, 96 produce longer quantized outputs, often approaching the generation limit. This association shows why gen-eration length must be read together with correctness: long outputs can still end in wrong answers. # 5 RELATED WORK 

Post-training quantization of LLMs. LLM PTQ combines sensitivity-aware quantization, outlier suppression, and reconstruction-based calibration. GPTQ (Frantar et al., 2023) uses approximate second-order information for weight quantization, while AWQ (Lin et al., 2024) protects salient channels through activation-aware scaling. SmoothQuant (Xiao et al., 2023) redistributes quantiza-tion difficulty from activations to weights, while QuaRot (Ashkboos et al., 2024) and SpinQuant (Liu et al., 2025b) suppress outliers through fixed and learned rotations, respectively. For reconstruction-based calibration, OmniQuant (Shao et al., 2024) learns equivalent transformations and weight clip-ping, and FlatQuant (Sun et al., 2025) learns affine transformations through layer-output reconstruc-tion. Building on FlatQuant, RECAP extends calibration along the sequence dimension, preserving long historical context while distributing reconstruction across short blocks. 

KV cache quantization. KV cache quantization balances memory savings, attention quality, and decoding overhead (Lin et al., 2025). KIVI (Liu et al., 2024) exploits key/value asymmetry with per-channel key and per-token value quantization, while KVQuant (Hooper et al., 2024) combines sensitivity-weighted quantization with outlier isolation. Outlier-aware rotations further improve low-bit cache representations (Su et al., 2025), and vector codebooks provide an alternative to scalar quantization (Li et al., 2025). Beyond cache distributions, OSCAR (Zhou et al., 2026) uses attention-aware covariance statistics to construct rotations and clipping thresholds. 

Quantization and calibration for reasoning models. For reasoning models, quantization sensi-tivity varies across model families and tasks (Liu et al., 2025a), motivating reasoning-aware train-ing (Okoshi et al., 2026; Lee et al., 2026). In post-training compression, calibration quality de-pends on both data selection (Williams & Aletras, 2024) and sequence length (Oh & Oh, 2025). Self-calibration generates calibration text with the model itself, avoiding dependence on external corpora (Williams et al., 2025). AYOT (Wang et al., 2026) uses reasoning traces and final answers from the high-precision target model alongside calibration questions. 

# 6 CONCLUSION 

We presented RECAP, a post-training quantization method that calibrates low-bit reasoning models on the history they read. Dynamic-prefix calibration brings the long quantized history into short-block reconstruction, and prefix read geometry supervises historical cache errors by their effect on attention, both only during calibration.
