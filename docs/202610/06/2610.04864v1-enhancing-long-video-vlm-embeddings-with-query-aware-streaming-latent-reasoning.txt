## ABSTRACT 

Long-video embedding requires capturing sparse query-relevant evidence under a limited visual-token budget. Uniform sampling can miss brief events in videos spanning minutes or hours, whereas encoding more frames in a single context increases memory and computation. We introduce Query-Aware Streaming La-tent Reasoning (QASLR), a post-training framework that accumulates evidence across clips while keeping the embedding size fixed. QASLR selects a bounded set of frames, restores their temporal order, and processes them clip by clip with a vision-language backbone. A compact set of persistent think tokens cross-attends to each clip’s features, while an embed token reads out a normalized representa-tion after every update. This design integrates evidence across multiple backbone calls without requiring all selected frames to share a single context. Training combines final contrastive learning, step-wise contrastive supervision, and final-embedding self-distillation. Intermediate supervision trains partial-video read-outs for retrieval, while self-distillation regularizes them toward the final rep-resentation. Query-aware selection produces query-conditioned representations for candidate-set scoring and reranking, whereas query-independent selection en-ables reusable corpus indexing. Under the full training recipe, HourVideo retrieval Hit@1 increases from 54.2 to 70.7 and from 57.4 to 72.8 for 2B and 8B Qwen3-VL-Embedding backbones, respectively. Gains extend to the evaluated moment-retrieval and video-QA tasks, and the streaming head transfers to a second Qwen-family embedding backbone. These results support streaming latent aggregation as an effective approach to integrating long-video evidence into fixed-dimensional representations. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Multimodal embedding models provide a common representation interface for retrieving, organiz-ing, and comparing text, images, and videos (Radford et al., 2021; Jia et al., 2021; Girdhar et al., 2023; Meng et al., 2026). Their fixed-dimensional outputs are attractive for large collections be-cause candidate representations can be indexed once and searched efficiently (Johnson et al., 2021; Karpukhin et al., 2020). Extending this interface to long videos is difficult, however, because the visual input grows with duration whereas the model’s visual-token budget remains bounded (Zhang et al., 2025a; Shen et al., 2025). A video may span tens of minutes, while the evidence that deter-mines its relevance to a query may occupy only a few seconds (Chandrasegaran et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2024). Consequently, the quality of a long-video embedding depends not only on seman-tic alignment, but also on whether the model observes the relevant evidence and retains it while processing the rest of the sequence. Existing pipelines usually address the input constraint by sampling a fixed number of frames. Uni-form sampling provides broad temporal coverage at low cost, but can miss brief relevant events when a fixed frame budget is spread across a longer video (Yu et al., 2023; Tang et al., 2025). Query-aware selection concentrates a limited budget on semantically relevant frames (Wang et al., 2025b; Tang et al., 2025), but it does not by itself solve the representation problem. Selected frames may be temporally fragmented, and encoding all of them in one context couples the frame budget to the backbone’s memory cost. Moreover, query-aware selection changes the retrieval setting: be-1arXiv:2610.04864v1 [cs.CV] 4 Oct 2026 cause the selected frames depend on the query, the resulting video representation cannot generally be reused as a single query-independent corpus embedding. A practical system must therefore dis-tinguish query-independent indexing from query-conditioned candidate scoring, as in embedding-and-reranking pipelines (Li et al., 2026). A second issue concerns how selected evidence is aggregated. Video-retrieval encoders have ex-plored joint spatiotemporal encoding and pooling of frame-level representations (Bain et al., 2021; Xu et al., 2021; Luo et al., 2022), while memory-augmented video models explicitly retain informa-tion across observations (Song et al., 2024; He et al., 2024). These developments motivate studying how a compact streaming state can be trained to produce useful retrieval embeddings throughout a video. This issue is particularly important when relevant observations are distributed across time or when the number of selected frames still exceeds the context that can be processed economically in one forward pass. We therefore study long-video evidence accumulation through a bounded latent state, building on latent bottlenecks and recurrent memory architectures (Jaegle et al., 2021; Bulatov et al., 2022; Hutchins et al., 2022). We propose Query-Aware Streaming Latent Reasoning (QASLR), a post-training framework for long-video embeddings. As shown in Figure 1, QASLR processes selected frames as chronolog-ically ordered clips, using learnable think tokens to accumulate clip-level evidence in a persistent latent memory. An embed token reads out a fixed-dimensional representation after each step. Query-independent frame selection enables reusable video indexing, whereas query-aware selection pro-duces query-conditioned representations for a bounded candidate set, such as candidates returned by a first-stage retriever. Training combines a final contrastive loss with step-wise supervision (Lee et al., 2015) and self-distillation toward the final embedding (Zhang et al., 2019), aligning both intermediate and complete representations with their targets. Following MMEB and MMEB-V2 (Jiang et al., 2025; Meng et al., 2026), a unified query–positive interface supports retrieval, moment retrieval, classification, and multiple-choice video question answering (QA). Interleaved sub-batching groups compatible input formats during mixed-task training and is evaluated separately from streaming aggregation. The full QASLR training recipe improves both 2B and 8B Qwen3-VL-Embedding backbones (Li et al., 2026), increasing HourVideo retrieval Hit@1 from 54.2 to 70.7 and from 57.4 to 72.8, re-spectively, relative to the original checkpoints. Gains extend to the evaluated moment-retrieval and video-QA tasks, while experiments on RzenEmbed (Jian et al., 2025) demonstrate applicability to a second Qwen-family embedding backbone. Our contributions are threefold: • We introduce QASLR, a streaming framework that decouples temporal coverage from rep-resentation size for long-video embedding. A persistent latent memory incrementally inte-grates temporally ordered clips and emits a fixed-dimensional embedding without placing all selected frames in one backbone context. • We design a streaming post-training objective that combines final contrastive learning, step-wise contrastive supervision, and final-embedding self-distillation. This objective di-rectly supervises intermediate representations through the same query–positive interface used across retrieval, classification, and video QA. • We evaluate QASLR on nine long-video tasks using 2B and 8B Qwen3-VL-Embedding models and demonstrate within-family transfer to RzenEmbed 7B. The full QASLR recipe improves every reported task over the original checkpoints.

## 3 METHOD 

3.1 OVERVIEW AND RETRIEVAL SETTINGS 

Figure 2 summarizes QASLR. A fixed set of think tokens accumulates clip evidence, and an embed token reads the latent state after each update. Streaming refers to aggregation over preselected clips, not necessarily online frame selection from an unseen video. Following unified multimodal embedding frameworks (Wei et al., 2024; Jiang et al., 2025; Meng et al., 2026), we formulate all tasks as query–positive pairs (Ai, B i). The query Ai may contain text, an image–text input, or a video–question input, while Bi may be a video, temporal window, class label, or answer. Non-video and short-video inputs retain the original embedding pathway. Long videos use the proposed streaming pathway. QASLR supports two retrieval settings, distinguishing reusable dense representations (Karpukhin et al., 2020) from pair-conditioned scoring (Li et al., 2026). In the indexable setting , frame selection is query-independent, and each candidate video produces one reusable embedding vj . Table 2 shows that the sub-batching configuration, including a larger effective batch, improved QASLR’s mean retrieval Hit@1 from 54.5 to 61.1 at 2B and from 55.7 to 63.0 at 8B. HourVideo retrieval gains were 15.3 and 13.6 points, respectively. LVBench retrieval improved by 2.4 and 4.9 Hit@1 points, while LongVideoBench moment retrieval improved by 2.2 and 3.2 points. At 2B, MovieChat and LVBench QA fell below the original checkpoint without sub-batching, reaching 47.0 and 36.8. Sub-batching recovered them to 55.2 and 42.3. At 8B, QA gains ranged from 2.3 points on EgoSchema to 7.1 on MovieChat. Sub-batching therefore benefited both retrieval and QA within the QASLR configuration. However, sub-batching also improved single-round post-training: mean retrieval Hit@1 increased from 52.2 to 56.8 at 2B and from 53.8 to 59.9 at 8B. With sub-batching, QASLR retained higher Hit@1 on all three retrieval tasks at both scales. Mean scores were 61.1 versus 56.8 at 2B and 63.0 versus 59.9 at 8B. At the task level, HourVideo Hit@1 gains over this baseline were 4.6 points at 2B and 5.5 at 8B. The corresponding margins were 8.2 and 1.5 points on LVBench, and 0.3 and 2.1 on LongVideoBench, showing task- and scale-dependent differences. QA results were mixed: single-round post-training scored higher on three of four tasks at 2B and on MovieChat at 8B. These comparisons show substantial gains from the batching configuration, while QASLR’s advantage over the sub-batched baseline is consistent for retrieval but task-dependent for QA. 

## 5 CONCLUSION 

We introduced QASLR, a post-training framework that represents long videos through a fixed-size latent memory updated over temporally ordered clips. The full training recipe improved retrieval, moment retrieval, classification, and multiple-choice QA relative to the original checkpoints at both the 2B and 8B scales. The streaming head also transferred to a second Qwen-family embedding model. QASLR stores frame indices rather than duplicated images and preserves the downstream embedding interface. Its query-aware path produces query-conditioned representations and is there-fore intended for bounded candidate sets or second-stage reranking. Query-independent selec-tion remains necessary when each video must be indexed once and reused across queries. Sub-batching benefits both single-round and streaming models, although improvements in QA remain task-dependent. Trainable modules. Only the streaming latent reasoning head is updated in QASLR, while pre-trained backbone parameters remain frozen. Post-training uses AdamW with a learning rate of 10 −4 and approximately three to five epochs, depending on the configuration. Interleaved sub-batching. Compatible examples are grouped by task and input format. Frame storage. Selected frames are recorded as indices with optional timestamps and decoded from the original videos when needed. A.7 FRAME BUDGET AND STREAMING DEPTH 

The full-budget configuration uses K = 128 frames, L = 16 frames per clip, and eight memory updates. For inputs with fewer selected frames, QASLR reduces the number of frames per clip. Frame budget and clip size determine different aspects of processing: K controls the observations supplied to the backbone, while L controls per-call context length. For fixed K, changing L also changes the number of updates T = ⌈K/L ⌉. At fixed L, increasing K

changes both temporal coverage and total computation. These dependencies distinguish the storage occupied by the persistent memory from the cost of obtaining and encoding its evidence. A.8 OBJECTIVE ABLATIONS AND INTERMEDIATE EMBEDDING QUALITY 

We isolate the effects of step-wise contrastive supervision and final-embedding self-distillation using a 2 × 2 objective ablation. The final contrastive loss is active in every configuration, and all other settings are identical across the four loss combinations. For text-to-video retrieval, the text query embedding is shared across steps and contrasted with each intermediate video embedding. For examples with unequal numbers of steps, padded steps are excluded from both the contrastive and distillation losses. Table 6 reports Hit@1 after 25%, 50%, 75%, and all selected clips. Mean checkpoint Hit@1 is the unweighted average of these four scores, summarizing intermediate and final retrieval quality without treating the checkpoints as an integrated curve. 

Negative construction. Consistent with the main objective, each query retains its paired target as the positive. Other valid co-positives from the same source video are excluded from the contrastive denominator, rather than added to a multi-positive target. The mask removes valid co-positives from the denominator while preserving the paired target. 18 Final and intermediate effects. Adding step-wise supervision alone increases final HourVideo Hit@1 from 67.9 to 69.8; distillation alone increases it to 70.1; using both reaches 70.7. The corre-sponding changes at the 25% checkpoint are +5 .9, +7 .4, and +11 .8 points. Relative to final-only supervision, the mean over the four checkpoints increases from 57.3 to 65.1 with both objectives. The combined final gain is 2.8 points, with larger improvements at the early checkpoint. Both ob-jectives therefore improve partial-video readouts as well as the final embedding in this comparison. 

Partial evidence and distillation. An early clip may not yet contain the evidence needed for the paired target. Step-wise supervision therefore encourages a useful partial-video representation, rather than certifying that the answer is already observable. The final teacher also sees evidence unavailable at earlier steps. Alignment to that teacher is a training regularizer, not access to un-seen frames at inference. Neither a high checkpoint accuracy nor a low average distillation loss guarantees correctness on every query or every candidate. A.9 FRAME SELECTION AND EVIDENCE COVERAGE 

Q-Frame selects text-relevant frames within the input budget, and QASLR restores their chrono-logical order before aggregation. The persistent memory occupies O(N D ) values per example. Excluding model parameters and backbone activations, a conventional single-step attention implementation requires working space of order 

O(( N + M )D + N M + d) . (18) These are inference-state bounds, not bounds on training peak memory. Backpropagation through the streaming head may retain activations across steps even when the backbone is frozen; check-pointing or recomputation changes that trade-off. If the model exits after bT ≤ T steps, then, for fixed per-step cost and negligible gate overhead, 

E[Cexit ] = Cfixed + E[ bT ] [ Cvlm (L) + Chead ] . (19) When fixed costs are negligible, the remaining cost ratio is approximately E[ bT ]/T , and the saved fraction is 1−E[ bT ]/T . QASLR depends on frame selection: its latent memory cannot recover evidence that is absent from the selected observations. The query-aware pathway produces query-conditioned representations for bounded candidate scoring. Reusable corpus indexing instead requires query-independent selection and encoding. The comparisons with sub-batched single-round training show consistent retrieval gains but task-dependent QA gains. Test-time order and memory perturbations measure sensitivity to altered inputs and state updates, rather than identifying the internal reasoning process. Transfer is evaluated within the Qwen family. Warm-cache latency depends on selection, decoding, candidate count, and hardware. Fixed memory size bounds the persistent state, but does not imply a proportional reduction in total computation. The seed-averaged scores describe empirical differences without establishing statistical significance. Early-exit bounds concern agreement with full-step predictions under the stated assumptions, rather than unconditional ground-truth accuracy. 22
