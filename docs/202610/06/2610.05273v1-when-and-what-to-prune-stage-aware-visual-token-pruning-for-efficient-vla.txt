Abstract 

Visual token pruning is an effective way to accelerate vision-language models and is especially useful for vision-language-action (VLA) inference, where many visual tokens must be processed before predicting robot actions. Existing pruning methods usually estimate which tokens can be pruned based on attention scores or feature diversity, retaining tokens that are either highly attended or visually different from others. However, most of them use fixed pruning schedules, such as pruning once at a preset layer or pruning at uniformly spaced layers. Such schedules can be risky for VLA models, because the model may not know which visual regions matter for the action in early layers. Tokens that look unimportant at first may become useful after the model combines visual observations with the language instruction. In this work, we propose SAPrune , a training-free visual token pruning framework for efficient VLA inference. Instead of pruning at fixed layers, SAPrune uses a small calibration set to observe how action-to-visual attention changes across layers, and chooses pruning layers only after the attention pattern becomes more reliable. At each selected layer, SAPrune applies a dual-path pruning rule: one path protects strongly attended visual tokens from pruning, while the other prevents useful surrounding context from being discarded. Experiments on LIBERO, SIMPLER, and real-world robotic tasks show that SAPrune prunes 87.5% of visual tokens and achieves up to 1.718 × inference speedup while maintaining competitive task success rates. 

1 Introduction 

Vision-language-action (VLA) models have recently shown strong potential for embodied decision making by mapping visual observations and language instructions directly to robot actions [1–6]. Despite their effectiveness, VLA inference remains computationally expensive because a large number of visual tokens must be propagated through deep Transformer layers [5, 7–9]. Visual token pruning is therefore a natural direction for reducing latency and computation, as shown in prior studies on efficient Vision Transformers and large vision-language models [10–17]. Although recent studies have shown substantial redundancy in VLA inference, most pruning-oriented acceleration methods still decide token removal from local importance cues, visual saliency, feature-space diversity, or their fixed combinations [8, 9, 13–18]. This treats pruning mainly as a static token-selection problem. Such a formulation is insufficient for action generation, where visual evidence does not remain fixed across depth: it gradually emerges, redistributes, and stabilizes as the model forms an action decision. Moreover, visual distinctiveness alone does not imply decision usefulness, since tokens that are geometrically diverse may still be irrelevant to the current action. These limitations motivate us to reconsider VLA pruning from two questions: when pruning becomes reliable, and what action-relevant evidence should be preserved. 2 Preliminary 

2.1 Related Works Efficient inference for VLA models. Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models extend large vision-language models by introducing an action modality, enabling robots to predict executable actions from 2visual observations and language instructions. Representative generalist robot policies include RT-1 [1], RT-2 [2], Open X-Embodiment / RT-X [3], Octo [4], OpenVLA [5], π0 [6]. Although effective for general-purpose manipulation, these models repeatedly process dense visual tokens through deep Transformer layers, making inference costly for real-time robot deployment [7–9, 21, 22]. 

Visual token pruning for VLMs. Visual token pruning reduces visual redundancy by retaining a compact subset of informative visual tokens. Early efficient Vision Transformer methods, such as DynamicViT [10], EViT [11], and ToMe [12], reduce computation through token sparsification or merging. For large vision-language models, attention-based methods such as FastV [13], Sparse-VLM [15], FoPru [23], HiPrune [24], LVPruning [16], IVTP [14], and ZSPA-Prune [25] rank tokens by prefill attention, text-to-vision attention, or prompt-conditioned relevance. Other methods preserve diversity or global representativeness [17, 18, 26, 27], while calibration or layer-adaptive methods determine pruning layers, ratios, or progressive schedules from calibration statistics or cross-layer information flow [28–32]. However, these methods are mainly designed for VLM understanding and do not explicitly model action-dependent visual evidence in VLA inference. 

Training-free acceleration for VLA models. Training-free VLA acceleration mainly follows temporal reuse, adaptive computation, and pruning-based acceleration. VLA-Cache [7] reuses visual features across adjacent timesteps, while VLA-ADP [22] dynamically reduces computation during manipulation. EfficientVLA [8], SP-VLA [21], SpecPrune-VLA [33], and VLA-Pruner [9] reduce visual-token computation during VLA inference. These methods reveal substantial redundancy in VLA models, but often rely on fixed pruning locations, semantic relevance, temporal reuse, or additional acceleration modules. They leave underexplored how to choose pruning layers and preserve action-relevant evidence under aggressive token reduction.

3 Method 

3.1 Overview 

Figure 3 gives an overview of SAPrune . SAPrune uses layer-wise action-to-visual attention statistics to decide where pruning is applied. It first selects pruning layers from rollout-level action-attention dynamics, and then applies a layer-local dual-path pruning rule.

4 Experiment 

We evaluate SAPrune on two simulation benchmarks, LIBERO [19] and SIMPLER [20], as well as on four real-world manipulation tasks. We compare our method with several representative efficient inference baselines, including FastV [13], DivPrune [17], SP-VLA [21], VLA-ADP [22], 7and EfficientVLA [8], and instantiate them on three VLA backbones: OpenVLA [5], OpenVLA-OFT [34], and π0 [6]. For real-world evaluation, we deploy the system on an AgileX Piper. All latency results are measured on an NVIDIA RTX 5090 GPU. Unless stated otherwise, latency is measured with PyTorch SDPA for both the full model and SAPrune, without model compilation. FlashAttention-2 results are reported in Appendix B.4.3. 

4.1 Experimental Setup Compared Methods. We compare SAPrune with representative token-pruning baselines for efficient VLA inference, including FastV [13], DivPrune [17], SP-VLA [21], VLA-ADP [22], and Effi-cientVLA [8]. These methods cover the major pruning paradigms relevant to our setting: FastV [13] performs attention-based top-token selection, DivPrune [17] preserves visually diverse tokens to re-duce redundancy, SP-VLA [21] applies structured pruning tailored to VLA inference, VLA-ADP [22] adaptively adjusts token retention during inference, and EfficientVLA [8] combines token relevance with feature-space diversity in a progressive pruning framework. For the main comparison, we follow the reported or default token settings of these methods rather than enforcing a unified token budget for all baselines. Specifically, the original models use 256 visual tokens, while different baselines adopt their own retained-token settings; our method keeps only 32 final visual tokens. To eliminate the effect of unequal token budgets, we further provide controlled comparisons under the same 32-token setting. 5 Conclusion 

We present SAPrune, a training-free visual token pruning framework for efficient VLA inference under aggressive token reduction. Instead of treating pruning as a static token-ranking problem, SAPrune addresses two practical questions for VLA deployment: when pruning should be applied, and which visual tokens can be safely removed. The proposed pruning-layer selection avoids arbitrary layer choices by using rollout-derived action-to-visual attention statistics to identify more reliable pruning stages. At each selected layer, the dual-path pruning rule removes redundant visual tokens while reducing the risk of pruning strongly attended action cues or useful surrounding context. This design requires no retraining or architectural modification, making SAPrune compatible with different VLA backbones. 10 References

B.1 VLA Model Details 

We evaluate SAPrune on representative VLA backbones, including OpenVLA, OpenVLA-OFT, π0,and π0.5. These models cover both autoregressive action-token policies and flow-based continuous-action policies, allowing us to examine whether the proposed pruning strategy generalizes across different action-generation mechanisms. 

OpenVLA. OpenVLA [5] is a 7B open-source vision-language-action model adapted from a pretrained vision-language model for robotic control. It encodes visual observations and language instructions, and predicts discretized robot actions through autoregressive token generation. We use OpenVLA as the representative token-based VLA backbone. 

OpenVLA-OFT. OpenVLA-OFT [34] is an optimized fine-tuning variant of OpenVLA. It improves the original action-generation recipe with parallel decoding, action chunking, continuous action representation, and regression-based training objectives. Compared with the original OpenVLA, it provides stronger control performance and higher inference efficiency while preserving the same general VLA formulation. We use OpenVLA-OFT as a stronger OpenVLA-family backbone. 

π0 Model. π0 [6] is a flow-based vision-language-action model for general robot control. Instead of decoding discretized action tokens autoregressively, it augments a pretrained vision-language model with a continuous-action flow-matching module. This makes π0 representative of continuous-action VLA policies and complements the OpenVLA-family models in our evaluation. 

π0.5 Model. π0.5 [35] is a newer VLA model designed for open-world generalization. We evaluate it on LIBERO and LIBERO-Plus using the same calibration and pruning procedure, without manual pruning-layer tuning. 

B.2 Acceleration Baseline Details 

We compare SAPrune with representative training-free or plug-and-play acceleration baselines, including FastV, DivPrune, SP-VLA, VLA-ADP, and EfficientVLA. These methods cover attention-based pruning, diversity-based pruning, structured VLA pruning, adaptive pruning, and progressive VLA acceleration. 

FastV. FastV [13] is a training-free attention-based pruning method for large vision-language models. It ranks visual tokens by attention-derived importance and removes low-score tokens after a selected layer. Since it relies mainly on attention relevance, we use it as an attention-only pruning baseline. 

DivPrune. DivPrune [17] selects visual tokens by encouraging feature-space diversity among the retained set. It reduces redundancy by preserving visually dissimilar tokens, but its diversity criterion is not explicitly conditioned on action generation. We use it as a representative diversity-based pruning baseline. 

SP-VLA. SP-VLA [21] is a VLA-oriented acceleration method that combines model scheduling with token pruning. Compared with generic VLM pruning methods, it is more tailored to VLA inference. We include it as a structured VLA pruning baseline. 

VLA-ADP. VLA-ADP [22] performs action-aware dynamic pruning for efficient VLA manipu-lation. It adaptively adjusts token retention during inference rather than using a completely fixed pruning rule. We use it as a representative adaptive VLA pruning baseline. 

EfficientVLA. EfficientVLA [8] is a recent training-free acceleration framework for VLA models. Its pruning component combines token relevance with feature-space diversity in a progressive pruning pipeline. We include EfficientVLA, especially its text-guided variant EfficientVLA(T-only), as a strong relevance-plus-diversity baseline. 16 SIMPLER Environment

B.3 Benchmarks and Tasks 

We evaluate SAPrune on three groups of benchmarks: LIBERO [19], SIMPLER [20], and a set of real-world robotic manipulation tasks, as shown in Figure 6. These benchmarks cover both simulation and real-robot settings, and include tasks with different horizons, scene complexity, and semantic requirements. 

LIBERO. LIBERO [19] is a language-conditioned manipulation benchmark built on Robosuite. We follow the standard four-suite protocol and evaluate on Spatial , Object , Goal , and Long , with 10 tasks in each suite. Spatial focuses on spatial relations under varying object configurations. Object changes the target object while keeping the task template relatively fixed. Goal evaluates goal-conditioned manipulation with different final states. Long contains multi-stage long-horizon tasks and is more sensitive to accumulated execution errors. 

SIMPLER. SIMPLER [20] is a simulation benchmark designed to better reflect real-robot ma-nipulation performance. We evaluate on four Google-Robot-style tasks: Pick Coke , Move Near ,

Open Drawer , and Close Drawer . These tasks cover common short-horizon manipulation primi-tives, including grasping, local object repositioning, and articulated-object interaction. SIMPLER complements LIBERO by providing visually grounded tabletop tasks with a closer connection to real-robot evaluation. 

Real-world tasks. We further conduct real-world experiments on an AgileX Piper robot. The real-world suite includes Cube Grasping , Pick&Place , Select-Pick&Place , and Multi-Cube Stacking .Cube Grasping evaluates basic target localization and grasp execution. Pick&Place tests object transport and placement. Select-Pick&Place introduces distractor objects and requires target selection before manipulation. Multi-Cube Stacking requires repeated grasping and placement, and is the most sequential task in this suite. These real-world tasks test whether aggressive visual-token pruning remains reliable under practical sensing and execution conditions.

B.5.1 More analysis 

Figure 10a shows the effect of the candidate-pool expansion coefficient γ on the average LIBERO success rate. When γ is too small, the candidate pool is overly restrictive and may exclude potentially useful visual tokens, leading to clear performance degradation. As γ increases, the performance improves steadily, since a larger candidate pool provides more room for complementary evidence selection. The curve reaches its peak around γ ≈ 1.75 and then remains relatively stable, suggesting that once the candidate pool is sufficiently expressive, further expansion brings only marginal benefit. Based on this observation, we use γ = 1 .75 in our experiments. 

B.5.2 Sensitivity and allocation analysis. 

Figure 10 shows two overall trends. First, the average LIBERO success rate increases as the candidate-pool expansion coefficient γ grows, and then gradually enters a stable region when γ

becomes moderately large. Second, the allocation coefficient λ shows a clear depth-wise increasing pattern across different VLA backbones: it remains small in shallow layers, becomes larger in middle layers, and reaches the highest values in deep layers. 

C Limitation 

SAPrune is built on the empirically supported premise that action-to-visual attention can serve as a useful proxy for action-relevant visual evidence. Our experiments suggest that this premise holds well for sufficiently trained VLA backbones whose action-related states are grounded in task-relevant visual regions. However, this premise may become weaker when the backbone attention is poorly grounded, remains diffuse, or is dominated by spurious background regions. In such cases, the attention signal may not accurately reflect the causal contribution of visual tokens, and SAPrune may still introduce performance degradation, especially in scenarios involving out-of-distribution objects, fine-grained contact regions, heavy occlusion, or long-horizon manipulation. SAPrune also requires visual and action-related representations to interact within the Transformer being pruned. This holds for the implementations evaluated here. In separated vision-language model (VLM) and diffusion Transformer (DiT) systems, action-conditioned queries may be available only in the downstream action module. Direct use in the VLM then requires an adaptation across modules, which we have not evaluated. Pruning the VLM conditioning sequence would not remove the remaining DiT denoising computation, limiting the possible end-to-end speedup. 

D Impact Statement 

This work aims to improve the efficiency of vision-language-action models by reducing visual-token computation during inference. More efficient VLA inference can lower latency and deployment cost, potentially making robotic manipulation systems more practical on resource-constrained hardware. At the same time, efficiency improvements may also make robotic automation easier to deploy at larger scale, which raises broader concerns about safety, accountability, and labor displacement. Since token pruning may remove visual evidence needed for reliable control in rare or out-of-distribution situations, SAPrune should be used with appropriate validation, monitoring, and task-specific safety constraints before real-world deployment.
