## ABSTRACT 

Large language models (LLMs) are typically pretrained in high precision but in-creasingly deployed with low-precision post-training quantization (PTQ). Recent studies have shown that using weight averaging during pretraining can improve PTQ performance compared with learning-rate decay, suggesting that it might provide a simple way to improve the pretraining-to-quantization transition. But the mechanism behind weight averaging remains insufficiently explained. This leads to inconsistent and fragile performance gains, thereby preventing practition-ers from applying such a technique confidently. As a response, we formulate weight averaging as a trade-off between retaining training progress and improving robustness under perturbation. We further derive a continuous family of averag-ing kernels that unifies conventional strategies and achieves the Pareto frontier between the two competing goals. Critically, a theoretical framework for per-forming weight averaging under PTQ is developed. It can be shown that coarser quantization is more susceptible to perturbations, whereas finer quantization could be less affected. Thus, our results could provide unified theoretical guidance for performing weight averaging under different PTQ conditions. Experiments validate both the predicted behavior and the proposed averaging strategy. Code is available at https://github.com/MOFA-LAB/weight-averaging-for-ptq. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Large language models (LLMs) are typically pretrained at relatively high numerical precision (Work-shop et al., 2022; Team et al., 2025a;b). As model scale continues to grow, post-training quantization (PTQ) has become increasingly important for reducing deployment costs by converting pretrained models to lower precision (Xiao et al., 2023; Shao et al., 2026; Li et al., 2025). This mismatch be-tween training and deployment raises a key challenge at the training-to-quantization transition: better performance before quantization does not necessarily translate into better performance afterward (Ouyang et al., 2025; Kumar et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2026; Catalan-Tatjer et al., 2026). Prior work has shown that training dynamics affect both training performance and sensitivity to quantization (Ahmadian et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025; Catalan-Tatjer et al., 2026; Luo et al., 2026). Late-stage learning-rate (LR) decay can further reduce training loss, but may move the model into sharper regions that are more sensitive (Watts et al., 2026; Microsoft AI Team, 2026; Yano et al., 2026). These results provide principled guidance for performing weight averaging across PTQ settings. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Learning-rate decay. Late-stage LR decay is widely used in LLM pretraining to further improve final performance (Hu et al., 2024; Hägele et al., 2024). Prior work has mainly focused on understanding why LR decay benefits pretraining (Wen et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026a;b; Liu et al., 2026). However, recent studies show that LR decay can also affect how well the resulting model transfers to subsequent stages (Chen et al., 2026). Similar effects have been observed across various downstream tasks (Wang et al., 2025; Yano et al., 2026; Microsoft AI Team, 2026; Catalan-Tatjer et al., 2026), suggesting that lower final pretraining loss does not necessarily imply a better transition to downstream adaptation. 

Post-training quantization. PTQ converts a model to lower precision, reducing storage and infer-ence costs (Chee et al., 2023). Various PTQ methods have been proposed (Nagel et al., 2020; Lin et al., 2024; Ashkboos et al., 2024; Dettmers et al., 2022; Egiazarian et al., 2024), and error compensation is one of the most well-established approaches (Yao et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025), with GPTQ as a widely used representative (Frantar et al., 2022). GPTQ reduces layerwise reconstruction error through second-order updates to the remaining unquantized weights (Frantar et al., 2022). Recent work has further improved PTQ through a range of transformation, clipping, and reconstruction techniques (Liu et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2024; Shao et al., 2024; Tseng et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024). 

Weight averaging. Classical Polyak–Ruppert averaging established the variance-reduction benefit of averaging iterates (Polyak & Juditsky, 1992). Motivated by it, stochastic weight averaging (SWA) showed that averaging weights along the training trajectory can lead to wider optima and better generalization (Izmailov et al., 2018). Moreover, LAWA (Sanyal et al., 2023) uniformly averages recent weights sparsely, while EMA (Morales-Brotons et al., 2024) and WMA (Li et al., 2026c) assign larger coefficients to more recent weights through exponential and linear weighting, respectively. Recent work has applied LAWA to the pretraining-to-quantization transition, showing that the averaged models can match or outperform LR decay after PTQ (Catalan-Tatjer et al., 2026).

The main limitation of our work is that the theoretical guarantees are established under an idealized setting. However, bridging tractable theory and practical quantization pipelines is a broader challenge for the community rather than our uniqueness. In particular, our training analysis makes assumptions about the latent mean and spatial noise. We provide an empirical assessment of the key assumptions in Section B.1, which offers support for the theoretical setting. At small λ, NLL remains close to or below the LAWA baseline; at larger λ, it rises above the baseline. Reducing temporal lag can initially offset or outweigh the cost of retaining additional spatial noise. Further movement along the frontier retains more noise and degrades performance. These results across model scales and quantization methods further support the tolerance-to-degradation transition predicted by Theorem 4.2.

Since smaller coefficients retain less spatial noise and larger coefficients reduce temporal lag, the reversal indicates a preference for less noise at lower precision and less lag at higher precision. This further supports the precision-dependent preference predicted by Theorem 4.3.
