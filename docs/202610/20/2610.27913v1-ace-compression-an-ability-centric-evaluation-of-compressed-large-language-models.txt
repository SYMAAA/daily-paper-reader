Abstract 

Model compression makes large language models deployable, but today’s evalua-tion tells an incomplete story. Standard practice measures retained task performance and stops there. This misses a critical fact: compression perturbs the distributed knowledge embedded across billions of weights, and task scores alone cannot reveal which underlying abilities survive and which quietly collapse. To solve this question, we introduce ACE-Compression, an ability-centric evaluation frame-work. Instead of asking only “does the compressed model still answer correctly?”, we ask “which of its core abilities remain intact?” Our framework probes four deployment-facing dimensions: procedural reasoning and constraint execution, knowledge grounding and truthfulness, social cognition and affective understand-ing, and persona fidelity and stylistic consistency. Across more than 35 compressed checkpoints from six open-weight LLMs—covering quantization and pruning—we find that compression redistributes ability profiles. Coarse, local, and surface-level signals often persist, while fine-grained, long-tail, and context-dependent abilities become fragile. Quantization consistently outperforms pruning in retention, but the practical meaning of that gap depends entirely on which ability an application requires. Based on our findings, deployers can choose compression methods by the ability profile their application needs, not just by a leaderboard average. And algorithm designers gain diagnostic signals to build compression methods that preserve what matters. We believe that ACE-Compression turns compression from a black-box accuracy drop into an interpretable intervention—one we can measure, understand, and ultimately improve. 

1 Introduction 

Model compression has become increasingly important for serving LLMs under memory, latency, and cost constraints [Frantar et al., 2022, Lin et al., 2024, Frantar and Alistarh, 2023, Sun et al., 2024, Zhu et al., 2024]. The general goal of model compression is to preserve the original model’s behavior, which raises the question of how to evaluate compressed LLMs at multiple levels [Zhu et al., 2024]. Current LLM evaluation can be viewed through two complementary lenses: task evaluation and ability evaluation. Task evaluation asks whether a model can complete concrete tasks under standardized benchmarks and metrics, providing a practical criterion for comparing model quality [Liang et al., 2023, Srivastava et al., 2023]. Ability evaluation, in contrast, seeks to identify the underlying capabilities that support task performance, such as reasoning, knowledge grounding, instruction following, truthfulness, social understanding, and persona consistency [Ye et al., 2024, Cui et al., 2026]. These two levels are not equivalent: a task score may reflect a mixture of abilities, and the same ability may contribute to multiple tasks. LLMs store learned information distributively across their weights, while model compression intro-duces local or global perturbations to the weights. Compression can therefore change not only how the

model performs on an individual task, but also how ability is shaped in a model. Modern post-training compression methods perturb the original parameters only mildly. We therefore conjecture that the compressed model might retain decent accuracy on a benchmark task while losing a core ability (e.g., multi-step reasoning or counterfactual understanding) that the task implicitly relied on. Thus, evaluating compressed LLMs should assess not only how much task performance is retained but also which underlying abilities remain intact after compression. Moreover, such joint diagnostics are useful for algorithmic improvement, since observed failure patterns can guide where compression methods should preserve sensitive weights, adjust sparsity patterns, use more targeted calibration data, or apply post-compression repair. Recent compression evaluation studies have compared efficiency, task performance, and reliability across different model families and different compression methods such as quantization, pruning, and distillation [Li et al., 2024, Yang et al., 2024, von Rad et al., 2026]. In this work, we propose 

ACE-Compression (Ability-Centric Evaluation for Large Model Compression) that instantiates this ability-centric view with four deployment-facing dimensions: (i) Procedural reasoning and con-straint execution captures whether a model can recognize requirements, coordinate constraints, and realize them in answerable or executable form. (ii) Knowledge grounding and truthfulness captures whether a model can preserve factual knowledge, ground responses in the relevant context, and avoid truth-sensitive failures. (iii) Social cognition and affective understanding captures whether a model can interpret beliefs, intentions, emotional cues, ambiguity, and socially situated meanings. (iv) Per-sona fidelity and stylistic consistency captures whether a model can maintain role-grounded behavior, character consistency, and stable stylistic or persona traits. These dimensions are not intended as a complete ontology of language-model abilities; rather, they provide a diagnostic lens for capabilities

This paper makes three contributions. 1) By interpreting benchmark outcomes as evidence about underlying capability profiles, we propose a comprehensive and systematic ability-centric protocol for evaluating pre- and post-compression changes in LLMs, organized around four deployment-facing dimensions. 2) In ACE-Compression, we evaluate more than 35 model checkpoints across 10 diagnostic benchmarks, covering over 25,000 questions, 1,785 multi-turn role-playing dialogues, and 13 psychometric scales. 3) Our findings shall provide actionable signals for compressed-model deployment and compression algorithm refinement for developers and practitioners. 

2 Related Work 

Holistic and fine-grained LLM evaluation. In recent years, a plethora of work motivates the evaluation of LLMs at the scenario, skill, and ability level. BIG-bench shows that aggregate task performance can conceal qualitatively different scaling behavior across diverse tasks [Srivastava et al., 2023]. HELM argues for broad scenario coverage, multi-metric measurement, and standardized reporting when studying capabilities, limitations, and risks [Liang et al., 2023]. FLASK decomposes coarse instruction-following judgments into alignment skill sets, making model behavior easier to interpret [Ye et al., 2024]. Task-specific benchmarks provide native ability readouts that are useful for this purpose: IFEval probes formal instruction following, ToMBench exposes theory-of-mind tasks and subabilities, CharacterEval measures role-playing quality, and PsychoBench measures psychological profile expression [Zhou et al., 2023, Chen et al., 2024, Tu et al., 2024, Huang et al., 2024]. To the best of our knowledge, ACE-Compression is the first to apply this fine-grained perspective to model compression, analyzing how ability profiles move from FP16 parent models to compressed variants. 

LLM compression evaluation. Post-training compression evaluation work has gradually moved from isolated score reporting toward systematic evaluation of retained performance, efficiency, and reliability. Quantization studies show that compression tolerance varies across tensor types, model scales, and task families, with multi-step reasoning often more fragile than simpler instruction-following readouts [Li et al., 2024]. LLMCBench expands the empirical scope by benchmarking compressed LLMs with deployment-oriented performance and efficiency measurements [Yang et al., 2024]. UniComp is capability-aware in benchmark coverage, but method-centered in evaluation design: its goal is to compare pruning, quantization, and distillation across performance, reliability, and efficiency[von Rad et al., 2026]. Although ACE-Compression shares probes such as GSM8K and TruthfulQA with UniComp, this overlap reflects common reference points in LLM evaluation rather than the organizing principle of our study. To the best of our knowledge, ACE-Compression is the first LLM compression evaluation designed from the outset to be ability-centric. 33 ACE-Compression Setup 

3.1 Study Panel and Compression Settings 

We evaluate 6 chat- or instruction-tuned base models: Qwen2.5-7B, Qwen2.5-14B, Qwen2.5-32B, Llama-3.1-8B, Llama-3.1-70B, and DeepSeek-V2-Lite-16B. The core compression panel covers five mainstream methods: BitsAndBytes 4-bit quantization, AWQ-INT4, GPTQ-INT4, 2:4 structured sparsity, and unstructured sparsity. Overall, the study covers more than 35 model checkpoints; the appendix lists the exact model compression combinations. For quantization, BnB-4bit is applied on the fly at inference time from the dense checkpoint, while AWQ and GPTQ variants are loaded from officially released quantized checkpoints in community [Dettmers et al., 2023, Lin et al., 2024, Frantar et al., 2022]. For pruning, we create sparse models from the corresponding FP16 checkpoints with one-shot SparseGPT pruning [Frantar and Alistarh, 2023]. SparseGPT can be viewed as a layer-wise reconstruction method: given a dense weight matrix

3.2 Ability Taxonomy and Benchmark Mapping 

The ACE-Compression dimensions are designed to connect fine-grained LLM evaluation with the central question under compression: which abilities are preserved, weakened, or redistributed after compression. This follows holistic evaluation work showing that aggregate scores can hide heterogeneous behavior across scenarios, tasks, and metrics [Srivastava et al., 2023, Liang et al., 2023]. In the compression setting, this view is especially important because a compressed model may preserve some task outcomes while weakening the abilities that support them. Prior work such as FLASK decomposes open-ended instruction following into multiple skill components, including logical thinking, background knowledge, problem handling, and user alignment, which is consistent with the ability-oriented view that LLM evaluation should look beyond aggregate task outcomes [Ye et al., 2024]. Moreover, recent LLM evaluation work has increasingly examined abilities such as social cognition, role-playing behavior, and psychometric profiling [Chen et al., 2024, Paech, 2023, Tu et al., 2024, Huang et al., 2024]. We therefore organize ACE-Compression around four diagnostic ability dimensions: Procedural reasoning and constraint execution , Knowledge grounding and truthfulness , Social cognition and affective understanding , and Persona fidelity and stylistic consistency .Table 1 specifies how ACE-Compression groups benchmarks into four ability dimensions and which benchmark-native readouts are retained for analysis. Procedural evaluation combines benchmarks that stress different stages of execution: IFEval probes explicit constraint satisfaction, GSM8K probes multi-step answer realization, and HumanEval probes executable output generation. The knowledge 

dimension is designed to separate knowledge retention from knowledge-grounded output behavior. C-Eval represents broad disciplinary knowledge, while RoleEval provides a representative probe of long-tail knowledge. TruthfulQA tests whether retained knowledge is expressed as trustworthy output. For social cognition, ToMBench provides the primary diagnostic signal, while EQ-Bench supplies auxiliary evidence about affective understanding under socially grounded dialogue contexts. 4 Results and Findings 

4.1 Procedural Reasoning and Constraint Execution 

The procedural dimension separates three related questions: whether compressed models could follow local constraints, whether multi-step reasoning still reaches the intended final answer, and whether the model can realize procedural competence as executable outputs. There is a cascade from local requirement recognition to multi-step answer realization and executable output generation. 

IFEval separates local instruction recognition from full-response constraint coordination. In Fig-ure 3(a), instruction-level strict retention usually exceeds prompt-level strict retention across methods. This indicates that local requirements tend to survive longer than their joint coordination in a full response. Compression, therefore, often breaks global constraint satisfaction before it erases recogni-tion of individual instructions. The size of this gap also varies across model families: under the same compression setting, local compliance can remain strong for one base model, whereas full-response control can weaken more clearly for another. As shown in Figure 3(b,c), we check the flexible-match retention and the gap between strict-match and flexible-match. The flexible-match view shows that GSM8K retention is large under quantization but becomes more fragile under pruning. The flexible–strict gap-shift view measures whether compression changes answer correctness and answer realization at the same time: under pruning, strict-match directly extracts the number following a specific answer marker, while flexible extraction searches more broadly over generated numeric strings and may select a later or irrelevant number instead. This reversal therefore suggests reduced consistency between answer recovery and canonical final-answer realization under pruning. 5Figure 3: Procedural reasoning and constraint execution. HumanEval evaluates the executable-output side of procedural ability. Pass@1 reflects first-sample executable consistency, while pass@100 indicates whether valid programs remain accessible under re-peated sampling. In Figure 3(d), quantization largely preserves first-sample success, whereas pruning causes larger drops. Figure 3(e) further shows how much additional executable success remains under sampling beyond the first decoded program. These two panels indicate that compression changes the first decoded solution while still preserving part of the valid program space under sampling. 

4.2 Knowledge Grounding and Truthfulness 

The knowledge dimension separates three related questions: whether broad knowledge is retained, whether long-tail knowledge remains undisrupted, and whether retained knowledge translates into truthful output behavior. Comparing C-Eval with RoleEval shows that broad disciplinary knowledge is usually retained better than role-specific long-tail knowledge. In Figure 4(c), C-Eval retention stays above RoleEval-Zh retention across most models and compression settings. The gap is modest under quantization but widens under sparse compression, where RoleEval declines more steeply. This suggests that role-specific long-tail knowledge is more fragile than broad disciplinary knowledge. 

TruthfulQA asks whether retained knowledge is expressed as truthful behavior. BLEURT is a learned reference-based metric for text-generation evaluation [Sellam et al., 2020]. We read BLEURT retention as generation-side reference compatibility, and MC1 retention as constrained truthful selection. Figure 4(a) shows that generation-side BLEURT retention remains relatively high across compression settings, while Figure 4(b) shows that the generation–selection gap is small under quantization but becomes positive under sparse compression. This phenomenon should not be read as improved truthfulness. Lower MC1 retention shows that constrained truthful selection has weakened. Thus the positive generation–selection gap suggests a shift toward reference-style, BLEURT-friendly generation after compression, rather than a genuine improvement in truthfulness.

EQ-Bench manifests a different failure signal. Its overall benchmark score often stays close to the FP16 baseline, but parseability declines: some compressed models stop answering within the emotion label space expected by the benchmark. DeepSeek-16B under unstructured sparsity is the most visible case in our results: parseable outputs drop from an FP16 baseline of 169.7 to 108.2, and the benchmark score becomes unavailable. This pattern suggests that compression weakens emotional judgment: the issue is not only whether the model understands emotion, but also whether it can express the intended emotion precisely. 

4.4 Persona Fidelity and Stylistic Consistency 

The persona dimension first examines whether compressed models still maintain visible role enact-ment, character consistency, and stylistic continuity. It then turns to a different question: whether questionnaire-elicited psychological profiles and identity-style readouts shift after compression. 

CharacterEval captures the visible role-play side of persona fidelity. Using CharacterEval’s native metrics, we construct within-dimension diagnostic contrasts to identify which visible persona signals are more compression-sensitive. Table 2 compares these sensitive signals with more stable reference signals in CharacterEval. It shows that more compression-sensitive signals are concentrated in expressive vividness, role enactment, and multi-turn continuity. Character-knowledge anchors and readability-oriented controls remain comparatively more stable, especially outside the sparse settings. This suggests that compression does not first erase the role; it flattens how the role is enacted. Under sparse settings, the loss extends further to persona behavior, persona utterance, and conversational consistency. 

PsychoBench groups its questionnaires into four psychological categories: personality traits, interper-sonal relationships, motivation, and emotional abilities. We use these categories to summarize how compression changes the model’s psychological portrayal. Figure 6(a) reports consistent drift from the FP16 profile. Across four PsychoBench categories, sparse settings produce visibly larger drift than quantization, indicating stronger movement in the psychological portrayal. Figure 6(b) makes this 8movement more interpretable by showing representative questionnaires: DTDD for dark-triad-related traits, BFI for broad personality, ECR-R for attachment, LMS for motivation, and Empathy for emotional-social orientation. The DTDD result is especially informative: in the raw questionnaire scores, compression tends to shift the model toward higher dark-triad-related traits. Figure 6(c) confirms this personality-type drift: 16P dominant-type stability drops after compression, especially under sparse settings. 

4.5 Findings

Figure 7 summarizes three key findings. First, compression redistributes abilities rather than uni-formly lowering scores. Knowledge-oriented signals are relatively stable, whereas procedural, social, and persona dimensions expose distinct weaknesses. Within these dimen-sions, local instruction compliance and broad knowledge survive better than full-response coordination, role-specific knowledge, implicit mental states, and persona stability. Sec-ond, coarse, short-range, and surface-facing signals are more robust than fine-grained, long-tail, and context-dependent abilities. This produces shallow retention: aggregate scores can remain acceptable after supporting abilities have weak-ened. Third, quantization generally retains abilities better than pruning, although the gap varies across dimensions and layers. Consequently, a checkpoint with high mean retention may still be unsuitable when an application depends on a specific ability. 

5 Broader Impact 

Our results support ability-aware deployment: practitioners should choose compression methods according to application-critical abilities rather than average retention alone. Quantization, especially GPTQ-INT4, preserves procedural reasoning and knowledge grounding more reliably, whereas sparsity—particularly 2:4 sparsity—more strongly damages fine-grained constraint execution, multi-step reasoning, and social cognition. Thus, conversational systems may prioritize persona fidelity, while code-generation services may prioritize HumanEval retention. Reporting ability profiles alongside aggregate scores reduces the risk of selecting a model that fails on its critical capability. The diagnostics also provide targets for compression research. Regularization or calibration objec-tives could explicitly preserve vulnerable dimensions, for example the activations associated with ToMBench’s belief-action-link subability. Occasional improvements in strict instruction scores under quantization further suggest that rounding noise may regularize some tasks, motivating carefully validated hybrid strategies. Finally, per-ability auditing is useful for edge, embodied, and regulated applications, where truthfulness or empathy may need to remain above deployment thresholds. The benchmark mapping and retention metrics provide a practical checklist for rejecting variants that pass average-score tests but fail a required ability. 

6 Conclusion and Future Work 

ACE-Compression provides an ability-centric diagnosis of how compression redistributes LLM capabilities. It characterizes behavioral changes rather than their internal causal mechanisms, and its model panel does not fully disentangle family, scale, and compression-method effects. The present scope covers post-training quantization and sparsity; future work should expand the model panel and examine distillation, quantization-aware training, and other retraining-based methods. 9References

This appendix follows the ACE-Compression design in the main paper and provides a detailed audit trail for its ability-centric claims. The main text interprets compression effects through four dimensions: procedural reasoning and constraint execution, knowledge grounding and truthfulness, social cognition and affective understanding, and persona fidelity and stylistic consistency. The appendix reports the detailed evaluation results behind these interpretations, preserving benchmark-native views and providing fuller tables where the main paper necessarily shows selected summaries. The evaluation suite contains ten diagnostic benchmarks. Its evaluation portion includes 541 instruction-following prompts, 1,319 grade-school math questions, 164 programming problems, 817 truthfulness questions, 13,948 multiple-choice exam questions, 6,000 bilingual role-grounded knowledge questions, 2,860 theory-of-mind testing samples, and 60 emotion-understanding questions. These item-level benchmarks are complemented by 1,785 multi-turn role-playing dialogues and 13 psychometric scales, which are reported in their native units. 

Procedural benchmarks. IFEval is kept in prompt- and instruction-level views, each with strict and loose scoring. This split is preserved because the paper’s procedural analysis relies on the difference between local instruction recognition and whole-response coordination. GSM8K is likewise kept in strict and flexible matching, since the strict–flexible gap is diagnostic of brittle answer realization. HumanEval is separated by decoding regime rather than summarized by a single pass@ k: the distinction between greedy execution, low-budget sampling, and broader recoverability under larger sample budgets is part of the substantive result. 

Knowledge benchmarks. TruthfulQA requires benchmark-native reporting because its selection-side and generation-side metrics support different diagnostic readings. The paper reasons specifically with MC1/MC2 and generation-side BLEURT-max, so the appendix surfaces those exact columns and states explicitly that this is a selected analytic table. C-Eval is compact enough to report directly with both validation and test accuracy. For RoleEval, the appendix reports grouped means that preserve the shot/language structure. 

Social benchmarks. ToMBench is supplied at three granularities: six official abilities, eight tasks, and 31 subabilities, each with EN/ZH variants. For the six-ability view, it reports the language-collapsed scores that correspond to the main paper’s base-model profile. For the task and subability views, the appendix provides grouped retention summaries with the averaging procedure stated in each caption. EQ-Bench is reported with both benchmark score and parseability. 

Persona benchmarks. CharacterEval is reported with the full grouped appendix table, exposing the detailed visible-persona scores. For PsychoBench, the appendix keeps the representative scales discussed in the paper as raw released outputs. The 16P file is available in aggregated form, with personality-type counts and axis percentages, so the appendix reports that aggregation directly and adds a clearly labeled axis-drift readout computed from it.

B.1 Procedural Abilities 

This subsection collects the fuller benchmark-native tables behind the procedural dimension. 

IFEval. Table 3 reports the full prompt-level and instruction-level IFEval outputs. The table keeps both strict and loose scoring because the two granularities support different diagnostic readings: prompt-level scores require all verifiable instructions in a prompt to be satisfied jointly, whereas instruction-level scores measure more local compliance with individual instructions. This makes IFEval the appendix-side evidence for the main paper’s local-to-global procedural analysis. 

Quantization. Across most model groups, 4-bit quantization stays close to the FP16 parent on both prompt-level and instruction-level views. Qwen2.5-7B under BnB-4bit slightly exceeds FP16 on prompt-level strict (0.7209 vs. 0.7172) and instruction-level loose (0.8177 vs. 0.8106). Qwen2.5-14B is similarly stable: GPTQ-INT4 slightly improves instruction-level strict (0.8501 vs. 0.8441) and 12 instruction-level loose (0.8657 vs. 0.8621). The larger Qwen2.5-32B and Llama-70B groups also show strong quantization robustness. Qwen2.5-32B matches FP16 under BnB-4bit on prompt-level strict (0.8004) and improves slightly under AWQ (0.8059), while Llama-70B improves under AWQ and GPTQ-INT4 on prompt-level strict (0.8447 vs. 0.8336). These cases support the main text’s observation that quantization often preserves local instruction compliance and full-prompt control better than pruning. 

Model-family variation. The stability of quantization is not uniform across model families. DeepSeek-V2-Lite-16B has a lower FP16 IFEval baseline than the Qwen and Llama groups, and its compressed variants show clearer losses. For example, AWQ reduces DeepSeek-V2-Lite-16B prompt-level strict from 0.4307 to 0.3549 and instruction-level strict from 0.5372 to 0.4712. This contrast is useful for ACE-Compression because it shows that the same compression method can be nearly lossless for one base model while producing visible compliance loss for another. 

Sparsity. Sparse variants show much larger degradation than quantized variants, especially under 2:4 structured sparsity. Llama3.1-8B prompt-level strict drops from 0.7468 to 0.3937 under 2:4 sparsity, and Qwen2.5-7B drops from 0.7172 to 0.4547. The same pattern appears in the instruction-level view: Llama3.1-8B instruction-level strict falls from 0.8237 to 0.5252, and Qwen2.5-7B falls from 0.7926 to 0.5863. Unstructured sparsity is generally less damaging than 2:4 sparsity. For example, Qwen2.5-7B prompt-level strict is 0.6359 under unstructured sparsity versus 0.4547 under 2:4 sparsity, and Llama-70B is 0.8078 versus 0.7135. However, unstructured sparsity still remains below the FP16 parent in most rows, indicating that sparsity weakens instruction-following behavior more strongly than post-training quantization. 

Granularity and strictness. Instruction-level scores are consistently higher than prompt-level scores because they evaluate individual verifiable instructions rather than joint satisfaction of the entire prompt. Loose scores are also higher than strict scores under the same granularity. These gaps are not merely reporting artifacts: they are the benchmark-native evidence for the procedural distinction used in the main text. A compressed model may still satisfy many individual requirements while failing to coordinate all requirements in a complete response. The IFEval table therefore supports the ACE-Compression reading that compression first exposes weaknesses in global constraint coordination before fully erasing local instruction recognition. 

GSM8K. Table 4 keeps the strict-match and flexible-match views together. This is necessary because the two scores diagnose different parts of multi-step answer realization: flexible matching asks whether the final numeric answer can be recovered somewhere in the output, whereas strict matching is more sensitive to whether the model presents the answer in the expected canonical form. The appendix table therefore provides the detailed evidence behind the main paper’s flexible-match retention and flexible–strict gap analysis. 

Quantization. Across most model groups, flexible-match accuracy remains close to the FP16 parent under 4-bit quantization. Qwen2.5-7B retains most of its flexible-match performance under BnB-4bit, AWQ, and GPTQ-INT4 (0.7377, 0.7202, and 0.7225 versus 0.7597 for FP16). The larger models are even more stable in this view: Qwen2.5-32B reaches 0.8370 under AWQ and 0.8317 under GPTQ-INT4, both slightly above its FP16 flexible-match score of 0.8211, while Llama-70B nearly matches FP16 under AWQ and GPTQ-INT4 (0.9462 and 0.9439 versus 0.9469). These results support the main text’s observation that quantization usually preserves recoverable answer correctness on GSM8K.

HumanEval. Tables 5 and 6 keep the decoding regimes separate so that first-pass reliability and sampling-based recoverability can be audited directly. Greedy pass@ 1 measures whether the first decoded program is already executable and correct, while the sampling columns show whether correct programs remain accessible under repeated decoding. 

Greedy execution. Quantized variants generally stay close to their FP16 parents under greedy pass@ 1,although the amount of preservation varies by model family. Qwen2.5-7B remains near its FP16 score of 0.7927 under BnB-4bit, AWQ, and GPTQ-INT4 (0.7683, 0.7744, and 0.7683). Qwen2.5-32B is even more stable, with AWQ and GPTQ-INT4 slightly exceeding the FP16 greedy score (0.8902 and 0.8963 vs. 0.8780). Llama-70B is similarly robust: BnB-4bit exactly matches FP16 at 0.8171, while GPTQ-INT4 and GPTQ-INT8 reach 0.8293. The main exception is DeepSeek-V2-Lite-16B, where AWQ reduces greedy pass@ 1 from 0.5061 to 0.2256. These results support the main text’s finding that quantization often preserves first-sample executable consistency, but not uniformly across model families. 

Sparse degradation. Sparse variants show much larger drops in first-sample execution, especially under 2:4 structured sparsity. Qwen2.5-7B drops from 0.7927 to 0.3354 under 2:4 sparsity, Llama3.1-8B drops from 0.6951 to 0.1280, and DeepSeek-V2-Lite-16B drops from 0.5061 to 0.0549. Larger models remain more robust in absolute terms but still degrade substantially: Qwen2.5-32B falls from 15 0.8780 to 0.6341, and Llama-70B falls from 0.8171 to 0.5244. Unstructured sparsity is usually less damaging than 2:4 sparsity, as seen for Qwen2.5-7B (0.6890 vs. 0.3354), Llama3.1-8B (0.4634 vs. 0.1280), and Llama-70B (0.7622 vs. 0.5244), but it still remains below the FP16 parent in most cases. 

Low-budget sampling. The n = 20 sampling columns show that compression can affect first-sample execution and short-budget recoverability differently. Some quantized models preserve or even slightly improve pass@ 10 under sampling: Llama3.1-8B under AWQ reaches 0.7816 compared with the FP16 value of 0.7586, and Qwen2.5-7B under BnB-4bit exactly matches FP16 on both sampling pass@ 1 and pass@ 10 (0.7034 and 0.8172). In contrast, sparse variants lose much of their low-budget recoverability. For example, Llama3.1-8B under 2:4 sparsity has sampling pass@ 10 of only 0.1518, compared with 0.7586 for FP16, and DeepSeek-V2-Lite-16B drops from 0.6535 to 0.1021. This shows that pruning not only weakens the first decoded solution but also reduces the chance that a correct program appears within a small sample budget. 

Higher-budget recoverability. The n = 100 table shows that some valid programs remain accessi-ble even after compression. Quantized variants often preserve high-budget recoverability almost completely. Qwen2.5-7B under BnB-4bit matches FP16 pass@ 100 at 0.8598, Llama3.1-8B under AWQ slightly exceeds FP16 (0.8415 vs. 0.8354), and Qwen2.5-14B under GPTQ-INT4 improves pass@ 100 from 0.8415 to 0.8598. Sparse models remain weaker, but the gap between pass@ 1

and pass@ 100 reveals residual recoverability. For instance, Qwen2.5-7B under 2:4 sparsity rises from sampling pass@ 1 of 0.1745 to pass@ 100 of 0.3780, and Llama-70B under 2:4 sparsity rises from 0.4334 to 0.6646. Unstructured sparsity preserves more of this sampled program space, reaching pass@ 100 values of 0.7622 for Qwen2.5-7B, 0.7866 for Qwen2.5-14B, 0.8232 for Qwen2.5-32B, and 0.8659 for Llama-70B. 

Executable-output reading. Together, the two HumanEval tables support the procedural interpretation in the main text. Quantization usually preserves both first-sample executable consistency and broader sampling-based recoverability. Sparsity, especially 2:4 sparsity, sharply reduces greedy and low-budget success, but pass@ 100 shows that some correct programs may still remain in the model’s output distribution. This separation justifies reporting decoding-specific pass@ k views rather than collapsing HumanEval into a single scalar score: compression can damage the first decoded program while leaving part of the valid program space recoverable under sampling.

Role-grounded knowledge reading. Together, the grouped RoleEval table supports the knowledge-dimension interpretation in the main text. Quantization often preserves much of the role-grounded signal, but sparse compression, especially 2:4 sparsity, weakens contextual knowledge deployment more sharply. B.3 Social cognition and Affective understanding 

This subsection follows the same hierarchy used in the social dimension of the paper: benchmark-level evidence from EQ-Bench, ability-level ToMBench profiles, and progressively finer ToMBench task and subability summaries. 

EQ-Bench. Table 10 reports EQ-Bench benchmark score and parseable-answer count for the social cognition and affective understanding dimension. EQ-Bench asks models to infer relative emotional intensities in dialogue contexts, so the benchmark score reflects affective judgment quality, while the parseable count indicates whether the model stays within the expected emotion-label output format. Broad personality and attachment shifts. BFI and ECR-R show a different kind of persona movement: compression can change the model’s broad interpersonal stance rather than only increasing or decreasing a single trait family. This illustrates why the appendix keeps raw scale outputs: the same compression family can shift different persona axes in different directions, and those directions are lost in a single drift magnitude.

31 C Additional Notes 

A few source-side facts matter for how this appendix should be read. First, the main manuscript analyzes the ACE-Compression panel formed by six base models and the primary post-training compression settings available for each model. DeepSeek-V2-Lite-16B does not include a GPTQ-INT4 checkpoint. Some files also contain auxiliary settings such as Trim or 

GPTQ-INT8 ; the appendix retains these rows for completeness when available, but they do not define the main cross-benchmark comparison. Second, the PsychoBench section is representative rather than exhaustive. The raw PsychoBench file contains more inventories and subscales than can be included without turning the appendix into a benchmark archive. The appendix therefore reports the representative scales used in the paper: DTDD, BFI, ECR-R, LMS, and Empathy. The released file does not provide DTDD Narcissism or LMS Overall values, so DTDD is reported through the available subscales and LMS is reported through its released factor-level outputs rather than through an imputed aggregate. Third, all evaluations were run with a unified vLLM serving backend on an evaluation server equipped with eight NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell Server Edition GPUs, each with 96GB GDDR7 memory. Benchmark runs used single- or multi-GPU serving depending on model size and checkpoint availability, with Llama3.1-70B served using 2-way tensor parallelism. Exact wall-clock runtime, vLLM server startup time, and total GPU-hours were not logged for every benchmark run; based on the evaluation matrix and typical throughput on this setup, reproducing the full reported evaluation is estimated to require approximately 750 RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell GPU-hours. CharacterEval additionally uses a Baichuan-based evaluator-side scoring model, whose overhead is included in the estimate. 

D Reproducibility and Evaluation Details 

This section records the evaluation details supported by the manuscript, the supplied result files, and the anonymized supplementary code package. 

Shared setup. The manuscript specifies six base models, the primary post-training compression settings evaluated for those models, the FP16-relative retention definition, a unified vLLM serving backend, and the split between quantized checkpoints and SparseGPT-based pruning. It also states that pruning is applied at 50% sparsity on attention and MLP projection matrices, with calibration samples drawn from yahma/alpaca-cleaned. The supplementary package provides configuration summaries with source links for the evaluated model checkpoints, compression methods, benchmark mappings, and third-party assets. 

Supplementary code and processed results. The anonymized supplementary package contains the ACE-Compression benchmark evaluation code, compression and pruning scripts, vLLM serv-ing templates, configuration files, processed benchmark result files, report-synchronization utili-ties, and validation scripts used to support the reported results. The package organizes the ten evaluated benchmarks under paper_benchmarks/ , the compression and pruning workflow under 

compression/ , model-serving launch scripts under serving/ , and canonical processed result files under results/processed/ . It also documents the external model checkpoints, benchmark sources, software tools, and license or terms-of-use information needed for rerunning the evaluation workflow. The package does not redistribute model weights, tokenizer caches, or original benchmark datasets. 

Benchmark-side details. The supplied result files expose several benchmark-specific details. IFEval, GSM8K, C-Eval, and TruthfulQA include point estimates together with released standard errors or seed-level variation where applicable. HumanEval distinguishes greedy decoding from sampled decoding at n=20 and n=100. TruthfulQA exposes 0-shot and 3-shot selection metrics, a generation-side metric block, and the 3-shot seed list (1234|5678|9012) in the supplied CSV. EQ-Bench includes both benchmark score and parseable-answer count as well as a run date. CharacterEval is reported through the processed grouped output used for the appendix table. ToMBench is supplied separately at the ability, task, and subability levels, each with EN/ZH columns. PsychoBench provides questionnaire-scale outputs and a separate released 16P aggregation file. 32 Remaining details. The manuscript and supplementary package document the main evaluation setup, code organization, serving templates, compression scripts, benchmark mappings, processed result files, and asset sources. Rerunning the full workflow requires users to obtain model checkpoints and benchmark datasets from their official sources listed in the supplementary asset table. The supplement should be read as an anonymized code-and-result package for checking and rerunning the ACE-Compression workflow subject to external model and benchmark availability, rather than as a self-contained redistribution of all models and benchmark inputs. 33
