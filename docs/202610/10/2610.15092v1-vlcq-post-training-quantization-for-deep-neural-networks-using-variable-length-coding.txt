# ABSTRACT 

Quantization plays a crucial role in efficiently deploying deep learning models on resources constraint devices. Post-training quantization does not require either access to the original dataset or retraining the full model. Current methods that achieve high performance (near baseline results) require INT8 fixed-point integers. However, to achieve high model compression by achieving lower bit-width, significant degradation to the performance becomes the challenge. In this paper, we propose VLCQ, which relaxes the constraint of fixed-point encoding which limits the quantization techniques from better quantizing the weights. Therefore, this work utilizes variable-length encoding which allows for exploring the whole space of quantization techniques. Thus, achieving much better results (close to or even better than the baseline results) while achieving lower bit-widths without the need to access any training data or to fine-tune the model. Extensive experiments were carried out on various deep-learning models for the image classification and segmentation, and object detection tasks. When compared to state-of-the-art post-training quantization approaches, experimental results reveal that our suggested method offers improved performance with better model compression (lower bit-rate). For per-channel quantization, our method surpassed the FP32 accuracy and Piece-Wise Linear Quantization (PWLQ) method in most models while achieving up-to 6X model compression ratio compared to the FP32 and up-to 1.7X compared to PWLQ. If the model compression is the concern with little effect on performance, our method achieves up-to 12.25X compression ratio compared to FP32 within 4% performance loss. For per-tensor, our method is competitive with Data-Free Quantization scheme (DFQ) in achieving the best performance. However, our method is more flexible in getting lower bit rates than DFQ across the different tasks and models.

Other methods assume access to calibration datasets. In addition to that to have a small memory footprint and take 2VLCQ advantage of the underlying distribution. The aim of quantization is to decrease the model size and computation cost by performing operations in the integer space, thus decreasing the number of multiplication and add operations, while maintaining high performance. Hence, it could be deployed on mobile, edge devices which are battery, power, and memory constrained. In order to optimize the model furthermore, compression techniques could be applied on quantized weights to further decrease the model size and to allow for more compact representation. There are multiple ways to carry out compression fixed-length and variable-length coding. In a fixed-length coding, each value is represented as the same length, in terms of number of bits. In a variable-length coding (VLC), values may have different lengths. VLC is beneficial when the distribution of the possible values is non-uniform, thus representing the more common values with a lower bit rate, thus having an overall better compression, when compared with fixed-length code. For variable-length coding, there are different widely used algorithms such as: Huffman coding, Lempel–Ziv coding, arithmetic coding, and context-adaptive variable-length coding. Thus by leveraging VLC, deep learning models can be represented in a compact format, therefore reducing the storage required, without affecting accuracy or performance. This paper focuses on post-training quantization for CNN models, without accessing the data or fine-tuning the models. In the absence of a dataset, the goal is to minimize quantization error, by taking advantage of the weights asymmetry, and scale selection to reduce rounding-off errors. We demonstrate significant improvements in quantization performance across a wide range of computer vision models that were previously thought to be difficult to quantify without fine-tuning. We utilize variable-length encoding as well as it helps in two aspects. First and foremost, it removes the fixed-length encoding constraint that limits all quantization techniques to be designed freely in order to reduce the quantization error. Without this constraint, our techniques in this paper can work in the general case where the distribution of the weights can be asymmetric and the number of bins can be as variable as needed to minimize the quantization error. These degrees of freedom allow better quantization of weights, thus achieving better performance. Second, in terms of bit-rate, it reduces the overall bit-rate since the weights distribution is not uniform, but rather close to Gaussian or Laplace distributions. VLCQ improves on existing work by having an assumption of having no access to the training datasets. Thus having much more generality. In addition, VLCQ takes advantage of the underlying asymmetry in distribution, thus dividing space into two regions only. This effectively decreases the number of information stored for the model and improves weights and activation quantization . In addition, conducting scale-selection or min-max search, is implicitly similar to the weight equalization step done in DFQ, which makes the model robust to tail-based distribution or outlier-based distribution. In addition it helps overcome rounding errors and improve performance for low-bit-rat quantization. This scheme is more general that makes the model have good performance in both per-tensor and per-channel quantization. In addition applying a mixed precision scheme on different granularities per-region, per-channel, and per-layer improves model performance while keeping the overall bit-rate at the desired level. Finally encoding model improves memory footprint compared to other approaches, and decreases bit-rate requirement of the model while having a large number of levels. Thus achieving good performance with low bit-rate. The main contributions of our work are as follows: 1. # 2 Related Work 

There has been a great effort in accelerating efficient deployment (in terms of computational power, memory, or energy consumption) of deep learning models on low computational devices. This attracted research into the area of model compression. There are a variety of approaches to achieve that. The first group relies on designing networks with efficient layers. Examples include depth/point-wise layers [25, 26]. The second group relies on optimizing the network architecture either through network pruning or activation compression, knowledge distillation, or quantization. 3VLCQ The work in [27] introduces the EL-YOLO algorithm, an optimized version of YOLOv8, designed for efficient maritime object detection using RGB cameras. It features novel enhancements such as AWIoU, SMFN, and GDFP, which improve convergence speed, detection accuracy, and network efficiency, respectively. AWIoU improves object localization by assigning different weights to training examples based on quality, reducing the impact of low-quality samples. SMFN enhances the neck component by adding scales and shortcut connections to better analyze features, especially for small objects in maritime settings. GDFP employs greedy filter pruning to reduce the network size by removing less important filters while maintaining accuracy. Together, these improvements significantly boost EL-YOLO’s performance in challenging maritime object detection scenarios. Model quantization refers to mapping model weights and/or activation from float space to integer space. Model parameters can be stored in fewer bits, and computations can be carried out on integer units rather than power-hungry floating-point ones. For model quantization, the literature can also be divided into two categories based on the expense and complexity of the quantization procedures: post-training quantization, and quantization-aware training. Quantization-aware training: This method works on re-training the model from scratch by simulating low-precision behavior (integer space) in the forward pass, while the backward pass remains the same. This makes model parameters more robust to quantization making the whole process almost lossless. The authors in [28] proposed a quantization scheme DEGREE-QUANT (DQ) to overcome quantization degradation in graph neural networks. The authors identified the main cause of errors which is rounding error, especially at nodes with high in-degree. In order to overcome this, DQ works by protecting high in-degree nodes from quantization thus encouraging more accurate gradients to flow back to the weights. Post-training quantization (PTQ): These methods work on quantizing a pre-trained model without the need to re-train it. PTQ can be further divided into subcategories: data-free quantization, mixed-precision quantization, or quantization with a small calibration dataset. Data-free quantization: refers to the quantization of a network without any knowledge of the training dataset used for training the model. The work in [20] proposed the first practical 4-bit post-training quantization approach. The proposed methodology relies on three steps: Analytical Clipping for Integer Quantization (ACIQ), per-channel bit allocation, and bias correction. In Analytical Clipping, the best minimum and maximum are chosen in order to clip activation values to mitigate errors. In per-bit allocation, each channel in the convolution layer has a different b-bit-width allocated, but the average bits are equal to require bit-width d. As a result of quantization, the new distribution of weights is much different than the original one, to overcome this bias correction is used which shifts the mean of the quantized weights near the original full precision weights and rescales the quantized distribution in order to have a variance similar to the original one. Piece-Wise Linear Quantization (PWLQ) [18] is a scheme to quantize networks that have a significant model degradation with low bit-widths. PWLQ works on dividing model weights and activation into three non-overlapping regions: a dense region (center around zero) and two sparse regions. The dense region is quantized with b-bits, while the two sparse regions are quantized with b-1 bits. Therefore the total number of levels is doubled (if b=8, the dense region will have 256 levels, while each of the sparse regions will have 127 levels which results in a total of 512 levels). In addition to that, they need an extra bit for storing the region in order to determine which region the weight value belongs to, so that re-quantization (dequantization) could work. The research work in [19] proposed a Data-Free Quantization scheme (DFQ) for per-tensor weight/activation quantization. DFQ relies on cross-layer equalization, which re-scales each channel in the convolution layer weights/activations so that the overall minimum and maximum in each channel are similar, hence when choosing a scale, all the values would have a corresponding value in the integer space other than zero. In addition to cross-layer equalization, DFQ relies on absorbing bias introduced by the quantization procedure. However, such a scheme shows its effectiveness only in per-tensor quantization only (In per-tensor quantization is applied to the whole tensor irrespective of its shape.). However, DFQ does not work well in per-channel (In per-channel quantization the weights of a convolution layer tensor are quantized for each output channel independently.) quantization as per-tensor quantization. Furthermore, it cannot be integrated with other methods such as PWLQ, or others. Generative low-bit-width Data Free Quantization [29] is a data-free quantization scheme, which relies on similar concepts as [30], where three models are used: full precision model, generator model to generate synthetic data, and the quantized model. GDFQ relies on generating synthetic data from the generator model by learning knowledge from the pre-trained full-precision about classification boundary knowledge and data distribution. Then this data is used to fine-tune the weights of the quantized model. This scheme shows its effectiveness for low bit-width for weights and activation. Post-training quantization with a small calibration dataset refers to those methods which have access to a 4VLCQ small subset of the training data. These methods use data to either fine-tune the quantized model or search for a better scale. Quantization with a small calibration data set: refers to the quantization of a network while having access to a small subset of the dataset. This could help in fine-tuning the model, or adjust some hyperparameters as minimum and maximum when doing quantization for activation. The authors in [23] proposed Adaptive Rounding technique for post-training quantization (AdaRound). Their motivation is based on the fact that rounding to the nearest integer is not always optimal. It may be optimal from the perspective of Mean Squared Error (MSE), but generally, it might not be good from the point of view of activation error. AdaRound performs per-layer fine-tuning with a small calibration dataset, in order to find the best rounding option for each weight value, by minimizing activation error. Similar to AdaRound, the authors in [22] proposed AdaQuant. AdaQuant is quite similar to AdaRound, but the authors introduced three other modifications: 1- per-layer bit-allocation using integer programming, 2- batch-norm tuning after quantization, and 3- bias tuning. The research work in [24] proposed a PTQ scheme using block reconstruction (BRECQ). BRECQ is quite similar to AdaRound, but the authors introduced two modifications: 1- initial scale selection by adjusting the minimum and maximum of distribution and choosing the one with the lowest Lp-norm loss. 2- Instead of calculating activation error per-layer and adjusting rounding based on it, which neglects interaction between layers, BRECQ divides the model into blocks, and minimizes activation error per-block, thus taking into account layer interactions in the same block. Mixed precision quantization: refers to those methods that allocate different numbers of bits for each layer or each channel. However, a major problem is how to determine the sensitivity and importance of each layer to allocate the corresponding bit-width for quantization. The research work in [31] formulated the problem of allocating/assigning bit-width for each layer as a discrete constrained optimization problem. However, using the original training objective (loss function between predicted using the quantized model and original labels) makes the problem intractable. In order to solve it, the authors used Taylor expansion to approximate the objective and provided an efficient approach to calculate the Hessian matrix. Finally, they showed that the problem could be reformulated as a Knapsack problem called Multiple Choice Knapsack Problem (MCKP) and proposed a greedy search algorithm to solve it efficiently. The authors in [32] introduced a new network compression method called PNMQ. It does not rely on expensive calculations like gradient descent training or clustering. The method involves using a parametric family of non-uniform quantization grids that can be easily optimized. These grids improve the quality of quantization approximation compared to uniform quantization. The paper also presents an optimization pipeline for tuning the non-uniform grids for each model layer, with or without using data. Additionally, the authors demonstrate that different layers of models require different bit-widths for quantization approximation and propose a data-free algorithm for selecting the appropriate bit-widths based on comparing quantization errors. This algorithm automatically selects optimal bit-widths to achieve a user-specified compression ratio. PNMQ can be applied to any model without changing the network structure and is compatible with existing weight compression techniques. 

# 3 Motivation 

The goal of PTQ is to successfully quantize a deep learning model, with small amounts of performance degradation, by transferring floating point values to integer values and carrying out operations in integer space. The quantization process could be carried out while accessing a small amount of dataset, or not accessing any amount of it. Many trained FP32 deep learning models could be quantized to INT8 without much loss in performance using per-channel or per-tenor quantization. Some models as MobileNet family incur loss in performance varies up to 5% performance degradation due to the nature of its layer decomposition. MobileNet family are mainly decomposed of a variant from convolution layers which are pointwise and depthwise separable layers. The performance keeps degrading for most of the CNN families such as ResNet, MobileNet, Inception, etc when we quantize using lower bit-rate, i.e, apply INT5, INT4, INT3, and INT2 quantization to these models. The degradation arises due to multiple factors. First, there is a distribution shift between the quantized model and the original FP32 model. Second, the nature of the rounding operation in the quantization operation induces noise. Third, neglecting the per-layer or per-block interaction while quantization. A typical CNN structure (adapted from [33]) consists of a stem layer (initial convolution on input images), a body layer, and a head layer (average pooling with a fully linked layer). A body is made up of numerous stages, and each stage is made up of several blocks, each block is composed of basic layers as convolution, maxpool, etc. Error in one layer mitigates and affects subsequent layers. Finally, the error metric to measure noise is based on weights not original task loss or activation. To overcome such degradation and problems, per-channel quantization with fine-tuning is usually applied. However, this might not be practical in some cases where a small portion of the dataset might not be accessible due to privacy concerns or license issues. 3.1 Weight ranges and distribution 

Most of the literature focuses on per-channel quantization for one main reason. It yields higher performance for quantized models when compared to per-tensor quantization at the expense of more storage will be needed to store the extra scale for each channel. The reason for better performance lies in the nature of the weight distribution for each channel in the model. According to the work in [34], all deep learning model weights and activation distribution follow a bell-shaped distribution similar to the Gaussian or Laplacian distribution. However, distribution shape varies according to model architecture or task. Some models have exact Gaussian or Laplacian distributions. Others might have a long-tail Gaussian distribution or have outliers. Some models have high variations in the ranges of weights for each channel in a given layer. As shown in figure 1, weight ranges vary drastically from channel to another even in the same layer as in MobileNetV2. Some channels have a symmetric range of [-1.5, 1.5], while others have asymmetric and skewed range [-0.1, 0.6]. If per-tensor quantization is applied on the weights, the weights which have small ranges will be clustered in very few bins and many of them might get mapped to 0, thus degrading the performance. However, this gain in using per-channel quantization is not applicable in some models as DeepLab-v3+ with a backbone of MobileNetV2, where several layers of weight ranges become very large (e.g., [-54.4, 64.4]). Thus while applying per-channel quantization, the model still suffers from severe performance issues. In addition to per-channel quantization, uniform quantization is applied, which assumes symmetry and uniformity of levels in the underlying distribution. In practice, the weight and activation distributions of pre-trained DNNs are similar to bell-shaped Gaussian or Laplacian distributions. As a result, uniform quantization may not always produce a small enough approximation error to preserve model accuracy, particularly in low-bit cases.

# 4 Methodology 

In this section, we start by discussing the proposed quantization method which relies on utilizing 2-scales quantization 4.1. This method leverages from the asymmetry of the weights distribution by dividing the distribution weights into two regions and applying a quantization operator in each region. This is the core method that we expand to have more variant methods. One variant relies on utilizing Mixed-Precision quantization 4.2 with the 2-scales core method by applying it at multiple levels such as per-channel or per-region. The motivation behind it is that not all channels have the same importance, thus by allowing some channels to have more bits/levels, the quantization performance might improve. In addition, some channels might have large values of positive numbers while small values of negative values or vice versa. Thus allocating a fixed number of bits (8-bits, 4 bits..etc) for the channel might not be a good choice. Therefore we allow more bits/levels to represent a certain region than the other. A second variant is through studying the impact of non-uniform quantization by allowing multiple step sizes. In a third variant, we move forward toward studying the impact of scale choice in the quantizer 4.3 operator by allowing exploration of different scales and how it affects activation and weight error. In the last variant, we explore further impact of minimum and maximum distribution in quantizer operator and how they affect quantization noise. Finally, variable length Golomb code 4.4 is discussed to allow compact representation for the quantized model across all variants we propose.

4.2 Mixed precision Quantization 

In mixed-precision quantization, each layer or channel in the model is assigned a different number of bins, thus utilizing the asymmetry of the distribution of the weights to redistribute the bins across. However, mixed-precision quantization is difficult for many reasons. First, it’s difficult to estimate the sensitivity of each layer or channel without accessing the full dataset. Therefore choosing random bit-width b for each channel or layer might result in performance degradation. Second, the search space for choosing the number of bins for each channel or layer is large, and an exhaustive search within each layer as well as across all layers is infeasible. In our approach, we focus on per-channel mixed-precision quantization. Instead of limiting all channels to have the same number of bins/bits, some channels are allowed to have higher or lower numbers. The only requirement we have is that the total bit-rate remains the same or less than the required. We further use mixed-precision per region. For a specific channel, symmetry does not often exist (if we divide the distribution into two regions around zero positive space and negative space). Therefore some regions may require more levels than other regions. For example, we could find a channel where the total unique negative values are 16, and the total unique positive values are 500. Hence, to better approximate the distribution, the number of levels allocated in positive space should be larger compared to negative space. The techniques for mixed precision quantization that are applied: • Non-uniform quantization through multiple break-points. Further breaking each region into two parts dense part and sparse part using a breakpoint (the point at which divide sparse/ tail of the distribution and dense part ) and applying non-uniform quantization at each subpart. • Per-region bit-allocation. • Per channel per-region bit-allocation. 9VLCQ

4.3 Scale selection 

In PTQ, usually weights are quantized by mapping FP32 values to the nearest integer values, Wq = round (w/s ) ∗ s,which can be formulated as Wq = dequant (quant (W )) = W + nw, where nw is the noise introduced and can be modeled as a shift in weights. Often, this is known as rounding to the nearest integer strategy. However, as demonstrated by [23], it’s not always optimal to round to the nearest. Rounding to the nearest may produce a small mean square error (MSE) with respect to original weights, However, it does not mean that it will produce a small error with respect to output activation or task loss. Table 2, shows the performance of the model when the whole network is quantized using different rounding techniques. It can be concluded that the best scheme was flooring. Rounding to the nearest introduces bias and variance shifts in the quantized weights. In order to overcome this issue, bias and variance correction can be used as described by [19 ? ] to decrease the rounding error effect. This problem is partially solved for high bit-width as 8,7,6 bit quantization, however, for low bit-width quantization as in 5,4,3 bits quantization, this problem still exists. In order to mitigate this problem, we tackle this problem from another perspective. Instead of finding an optimal rounding strategy as described by [23, 24], We employ three techniques: • Exploring search space of possible scales (Scale search / Fraction bit-width). • Finding the best scale by changing the minimum and maximum of the weights (Min-Max adjustment). • Exploring best minimum and maximum space with scale search for each combination. In the first approach, we try to find the scale that minimizes the Lp norm with respect to weights. Scale, in essence, plays a crucial part in quantization as it determines the new centers, in which the values need to be mapped. When the weights are divided by a scale to obtain integer values, the results are not purely integer values, but rather still in the floating space. Thus, these values need to be mapped to an integer level whether up, or down if not exactly an integer value. Each decision shifts the distribution of weights thus model performance changes. Therefore choosing appropriate scales helps in reducing rounding errors. In order to do so, different ranges of scales need to be investigated for a specific bit-rate. Scale search strategy partially solves the problem without knowledge of the data. Finding the best scale that minimizes the Lp norm is similar to the rounding solution. The space of possible scales to achieve a certain bit-width is infinite. If we consider what each scale does, it remaps weights to new centers (re-bin weights to specific centers based on a given scale). Therefore each scale in the infinite space differs in the new centers of the weights. In other words, each scale performs different rounding on the weight, therefore our goal to partially solve rounding errors can be defined as finding the best scale that minimizes rounding errors. However, it’s not feasible to search this infinite space. To overcome this problem, The search space is restricted to be the interval of possible scales that lies between the scale of the first higher bit-width and the scale of the lower bit-width. This interval is divided equally based on specified steps, chosen according to the type of quantization and bit-widths. This criterion holds as we decrease 11 VLCQ bit-width, the search space is double the size of the previous one. For a fixed minimum and maximum we compute different scales by adjusting a number of levels (having fraction levels example: 128.5 levels or 129 levels or 127.5 levels) or by directly exploring the space between the first higher bit-width and the scale of the lower bit-width. In the previous approach, the minimum and maximum values of the distribution are fixed when calculating the scale. However, changing these values might help in improving quantization. Changing minimum and maximum may introduce new levels, and change mapping centers thus might allow better approximation for tail values which is crucial. In the second approach, we modify the technique used by [24], to be robust enough for low and high bit widths. The approach used in [24] improves results for asymmetric quantization. However, it fails for symmetric quantization. Therefore, We propose modifications in order to work with asymmetric quantization and symmetric quantization. In the asymmetric quantization case, the idea is based on changing the minimum and maximum values of the distribution, and then calculating the scale and choosing the best values that minimize the Lp norm of weights. Since optimal p values in the Lp norm are unknown, we try to find the best scale for different values in p space. In the case of symmetric quantization, the minimum and maximum adjustment calculated is based on the weights distribution which lies entirely in the positive space or negative space as shown in figure1. In case of the weight lies entirely in positive space, the maximum of the distribution is fixed and a search is carried out to find the best minimum values that minimize the Lp norm of weights to be used in calculating the scale, the search interval lies between zero and original minimum value in the distribution. In case the weights lie entirely in the negative space, the minimum of the distribution is fixed and a search is carried out to find the best maximum values that minimize the Lp norm of weights to be used in calculating the scale, the search interval lies between zero and original maximum values in the distribution. Finally, we integrate both approaches min-max adjustment and scale search to push the limits of model performance. For each candidate’s minimum and maximum, we search for the best scales that minimize the Lp norm of weights. 

4.4 Variable length encoding 

Most of the model’s weights distribution is bell-shaped/ logarithmic shape, we take advantage of such distribution and use a variable-length encoding technique to compress weights, thus decreasing the model’s memory footprint further. Variable-length encoding leverages from bell shape/ normal distribution, as frequent weights will have a small representation, while infrequent weight values will have a large representation. Therefore by calculating the total bit-rate needed it will be much smaller than the original bit-width (bin number) of the quantized weights. Such techniques could allow mixed-precision quantization, or increase the number of bits for specific layers, while still having a low bit-rate. This could result in a low memory footprint while having a good quantized model. One of the variable encoding techniques that takes advantage of such distribution is Golomb coding. That results in allocating 2 ∗ 2B levels for each layer and an extra bit to determine which region it belongs to. Thus the total number of levels in each layer is 2 ∗ 2B and bit-rate is B + 1 . VLCQ outperform PWLQ using 256, 64, and 16 bins for the MobileNetV2 model. For ResNet50 and ResNet18, VLCQ achieves good performance (3-4% accuracy loss) with sub 3 bit-width making it the first data-free PTQ method to achieve good performance with small bit-width. For 256 bins there is a margin of 0.02% increase with lower bit-width, VLCQ Min-Max, and VLCQ Min-Max SS could achieve a near-perfect accuracy of 76.108% and 76.120% with 7 sub-bit-width while PWLQ achieves 76.100% with full 9 bits, with 512 bins. VLCQ achieves similar results compared to PWLQ but with smaller bit-rates. For ResNet18, PWLQ achieves 69.622% accuracy while VLCQ Min-Max SS achieves 69.790% accuracy with 5.3 bits which is better than the FP32 accuracy. The reason for why quantization can accidentally achieve better results, is because when the weights get quantized, the new values can luckily achieve better accuracy. Furthermore, VLCQ MP achieves around 1% error loss, which is similar to BRECQ (BRECQ uses calibration dataset to find optimal rounding strategy) which achieves 1% error loss on MobileNetV2. However, VLCQ SS achieves around 1.6% error with a sub 5 bit-rate in MobileNetV2. Thus, VLCQ is the first DFQ-PTQ technique to achieve around 1-2% error with a low bit-rate for MobileNetV2, ResNet50, and ResNet18. For the memory footprint, VLCQ occupies less memory than PWLQ and uniform quantization for two reasons. First reason is that Golomb code encoding is applied which compresses weights representation. Secondly, PWLQ and uniform quantization apply asymmetric affine quantizers which need to store offset FP32 values for each channel. Unlike VLCQ which uses symmetric quantizers. In terms of FLOPs, VLCQ has a similar number of FLOPS compared to uniform quantizers, as in VLCQ after the MAC is applied in each region its descaled then the addition operation takes place in FP space to get the final activation where this operation depends on the tensor size. In uniform quantizers, since they use affine asymmetric quantizers after the MAC operation takes place and tensor is dequantized to original space, the offset of the quantizers is added to the dequantized weights, therefore this operation depends on size of tensor. Hence, VLCQ does not suffer from an increase in FLOPs compared to normal methods. Unlike PWLQ which divides space into three regions, three additions need to be performed in FP space to construct output activation, in addition to 2 FP addition to add offset, since the quantizer operator is affine quantizer. Although the current proposed methodology, VLCQ and its variants, show impressive results in various tasks, it suffers from some limitations. For instance, in scale selection and min-max adjustment, the search space is fixed. The search space should be dynamic according to bit-rate, and model architecture. During our experiments for higher bit-rate it requires a small search space for low bit-rate. So, to achieve good results, the search space needs to be increased. The metric to choose the best scale or minimum and maximum values needs to be adjusted to investigate which p-value in lp-norm to choose, currently we are fixing it to 2 (MSE). Another limitation is the choice of break-point currently we are limiting to zero, even for the distribution which lies entirely to the left or right. This might need more investigation to find the best break point to start quantization from. Another limitation is performance degradation in case of outlier presence. Although Scale selection and Min-Max could mitigate this case in per-channel quantization as in the case of semantic segmentation in table 10, it wasn’t able to achieve relatively good performance in case of per-tensor quantization. However, if search space in per-tensor quantization was to be large and the presence of calibration data was to be used to choose the best min-max or scale. Performance would drastically improve.
