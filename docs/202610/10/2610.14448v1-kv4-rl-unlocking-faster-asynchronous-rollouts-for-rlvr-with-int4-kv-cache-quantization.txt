# KV4-RL: Unlocking Faster Asynchronous Rollouts for RLVR with INT4 KV Cache Quantization 

Sihwan Park 1, Sung-Yub Kim 2, Doohyuk Jang 1 and Eunho Yang 1,3, †

Abstract. Asynchronous rollout improves the efficiency of reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) by reducing under-utilization during long-tail generation. Sustained concurrency also concentrates context processing into fewer decode steps, increasing the KV workload per step. We characterize this KV concentration and find that KV cache quantization delivers substantially greater acceleration than weight-only and weight-activation quantization in our matched-workload comparisons. However, INT4 KV rollout can degrade learning even with token-level truncated importance sampling (TIS). We propose KV4-RL , which combines calibration-free INT4 KV quantization with selective INT8 protection of attention sinks and recent context. Protecting a bounded number of positions reduces sampler mismatch while retaining most of the speedup from INT4. Across 400 iterations of Qwen3-8B-Base GRPO training, KV4-RL achieves 1.80 × cumulative rollout speedup and 1.27 × cumulative full-iteration speedup over BF16 rollout, reaching 1.97 × rollout speedup over the final 50 iterations. Under a common BF16 evaluator, its final six-benchmark mean accuracy is within 0.5 percentage points of BF16-rollout training for both Qwen3-4B-Base and Qwen3-8B-Base. 

# 1. Introduction 

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) has become a central approach for training reasoning models. Methods such as Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) learn from multiple responses per prompt (Shao et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2026), making rollout generation a major training cost. Reasoning responses can span thousands of tokens and vary substantially in length (Fu et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2026). In conventional wave-based collection, shorter responses finish early while a few long responses continue decoding, causing the active batch to collapse. Asynchronous systems mitigate this long-tail inefficiency by admitting new requests (Fu et al., 2025; Lu et al., 2025), while partial-rollout schemes carry unfinished responses across policy updates (Kimi Team et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2026). We study quota-based partial collection, which combines these mechanisms to sustain decode concurrency. Sustaining concurrency improves utilization, but also changes the workload processed at each decode step. For a fixed set of responses, asynchronous scheduling preserves the total generated tokens and logical attention work, but advances more responses together and concentrates the same KV processing into fewer sequential steps. We call this increase in the average KV workload processed per decode step KV concentration .Response-length variability strengthens this effect because longer responses remain active for more steps and are therefore overrepresented in the active batch. In a matched Qwen3-4B-Base trace, asynchronous collection increases mean nominal resident KV volume by 7.82 × and mean decode-step latency by 4.52 ×. The larger active batch still yields 1.83 × higher decode-service throughput, but its per-step cost offsets a substantial part of the concurrency gain. At longer contexts, KV capacity can also restrict request admission. Fully realizing the benefit of asynchronous rollout therefore requires processing its concentrated workload efficiently. This workload shift changes which quantization target provides the greatest rollout acceleration. Prior work has primarily quantized model weights or activations (Liu et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026), with recent work also incorporating FP8 KV caches into broader low-precision stacks (Qiu et al., 2026). Under sustained concurrency, weight reads are increasingly amortized across the active batch, whereas attention continues to process the context of every response. Our roofline analysis identifies KV reads as the largest idealized service demand in the studied workloads, and matched-workload measurements show substantially greater decode acceleration from KV quantization than from the evaluated weight-only and weight-activation configurations. Cache compression can additionally sustain higher concurrency by fitting more resident context within the same HBM budget. Together, these results identify KV cache quantization as an effective target for asynchronous rollout. Aggressive KV quantization nevertheless creates a distinct challenge for RL training since rollouts generate the samples used for subsequent policy updates. # 2. Related Work 

Asynchronous and partial rollout. RL training systems mitigate rollout bottlenecks through flexible placement and scheduling of generation and policy optimization (Sheng et al., 2025; Fu et al., 2025; Lu et al., 2025). One line of work overlaps rollout collection with learner execution, increasing hardware utilization while controlling the policy staleness introduced by asynchronous updates (Fu et al., 2025; Li et al., 2027). Another line addresses long-tail generation through partial rollout, allowing completed responses to enter training while unfinished responses continue across subsequent updates (Kimi Team et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2026). These approaches primarily study how scheduling reduces idle time or exposes more parallelism. We examine a complementary system consequence. By keeping more responses active, asynchronous collection concentrates context processing into fewer and larger decode steps, changing both the execution bottleneck and the relative value of different quantization targets. Our experiments focus on colocated, quota-based partial collection, where generation and optimization alternate on the same GPUs and unfinished prefixes are carried across policy updates. 

Quantized reinforcement learning. Quantized RL methods reduce rollout cost by lowering the precision of model execution. Existing approaches predominantly target weights or activations, using weight-only or weight–activation quantization with either low-rank adaptation or full-parameter policy updates (Huang et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026). Since the resulting sampler may differ numerically from the learner policy, these methods combine quantized rollout with importance correction, modified policy objectives, or other mechanisms that improve robustness to rollout–training mismatch (Yao et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026). A related direction instead aligns the numerical execution paths used during generation and training, or introduces compensation on both sides of the update (Xi et al., 2026; Zhuge et al., 2026). FP8-RL extends low-precision execution to a broader training stack that also includes FP8 KV caches (Qiu et al., 2026). Our work investigates a different numerical target and operating regime: sub-8-bit KV cache quantization under the sustained concurrency and long contexts produced by asynchronous rollout. We examine both the execution efficiency of rollout KV quantization and its effect on learned-policy quality, while keeping learner computation and evaluation in BF16 to isolate the rollout precision. 

KV cache compression and selective precision. KV cache compression reduces the storage and memory traffic associated with attention during autoregressive generation. Distribution-aware quantizers exploit the distinct statistical properties of keys and values (Liu et al., 2024; Hooper et al., 2024), while rotation-based methods mix channels to reduce sensitivity to outliers before quantization (Ashkboos et al., 2024; Jia et al., 2026). To translate storage reduction into practical speedup, efficient attention implementations combine packed KV representations with low-bit dot products and fused reconstruction (Lin et al., 2025; Du et al., 2026; Jia et al., 2026). Selective-precision methods complement these techniques by retaining influential initial positions (Xiao et al., 2024; Su and Yuan, 2025), recent context (Liu et al., 2024; Duanmu et al., 2024), or dynamically selected regions at higher precision (Liu et al., 2026). Most of these methods are designed and evaluated for inference, where numerical perturbations affect the generated output of an individual request. RL rollout introduces an additional constraint because those outputs become training data and can influence subsequent policy updates. KV4-RL builds on these compression and selective-precision principles, but utilizes them to balance execution efficiency against sampler fidelity during repeated RLVR training. 

# 3. Preliminaries 

Quota-based partial rollout. Each training iteration requires 𝑇 completed prompt groups, with 𝐺 responses per group. As shown in Fig. 1, the synchronous baseline launches the groups assigned to the current wave and waits until all 𝑇 𝐺 responses have completed. Its active batch therefore contracts as shorter responses finish, leaving decode capacity idle until the longest response terminates and the policy update begins. Our asynchronous collector instead maintains an oversubscribed request pool and admits new responses whenever decode capacity becomes available. Responses can begin in one collection interval and continue across a policy update, while work that may contribute to a later update can start before the current quota is reached. The scheduler does not assign a fixed set of prompt groups to each update in advance. Collection can stop once 

𝑇 prompt groups have each completed all 𝐺 responses, provided that no unfinished group has reached the configured age limit. The oldest 𝑇 completed groups are selected for training, additional completed groups remain in the pool, and unfinished groups retain their token prefixes for a later interval. After a policy update, the KV caches of carried prefixes cannot be reused since they were computed under the staled policy. The retained token sequences are therefore re-prefilled under the updated policy to reconstruct their KV caches before decoding resumes. Re-prefill does not resample or modify the retained tokens, and the probability originally recorded for each sampled token remains unchanged. A carried response can consequently

# 4. KV Concentration in Asynchronous Rollout 

Quota-based partial rollout avoids the low-utilization tail of synchronous generation by continuously filling available decode slots. This improvement, however, does not eliminate the underlying generation work. Instead, it changes how that work is distributed across decode steps, raising a central question for accelerating asynchronous rollout: Which component of the decode workload becomes the dominant cost when high concurrency is sustained across long responses? 

We answer this question by first characterizing how asynchronous scheduling concentrates KV processing into fewer decode steps and validating this behavior in practical rollout traces. We then compare candidate quantization targets and show that KV cache quantization most directly addresses the resulting bottleneck.

4.2. Why Quantize the KV Cache? Sustained concurrency shifts the workload toward KV processing. Under a wide active batch, model-weight traffic is amortized across more responses, while attention must still process the retained context of every response. For a fixed model and numerical precision, the logical memory demand per generated token scales approximately as 𝑊 bytes /𝐵 + 𝜅 ¯ℓ, separating amortized weight reads from context-dependent KV accesses. Increasing concurrency reduces the relative contribution of weight traffic, whereas longer responses continue to increase KV traffic. KV processing therefore becomes increasingly important precisely in the high-concurrency, long-generation regime created by asynchronous rollout. Among the weight-only, weight-activation, and KV cache configurations evaluated here, KV quantization provides the largest speedups. The W4A16 and W8A8 implementations remain close to or below BF16 speed on the same workloads. The 8B experiments exhibit the same pattern, with KV4 providing 1.44 − 1.97 × speedup across checkpoints (Appendix D.2). These measurements depend on both storage precision and the corresponding numerical and kernel implementation, but consistently identify KV compression as the most effective target in the asynchronous rollout regime. Fixed-workload replay isolates the reduction in execution cost for a given decode state. During end-to-end rollout, a smaller cache provides an additional benefit by allowing more contexts to fit within the same HBM budget, preserving a wider active batch when KV allocation would otherwise limit request admission. KV cache quantization can therefore accelerate asynchronous rollout through two reinforcing effects: faster processing of the concentrated workload and higher feasible concurrency. The remaining question is whether an aggressive precision such as INT4 can provide these benefits without introducing enough sampling error to alter policy learning. 

# 5. KV4-RL: Faster Asynchronous Rollout with INT4 KV Caches 

Section 4.2 identifies KV cache quantization as the most effective acceleration target for asynchronous rollout and shows that INT4 provides the largest execution gains among the evaluated configurations. This finding motivates a seemingly direct approach: storing and processing the rollout KV cache in INT4. In RL rollout, however, the generated responses are not merely inference outputs. They constitute the samples from which policy updates are constructed, so numerical errors that appear modest at an individual prefix can accumulate along trajectories and ultimately alter the learned policy. The resulting design challenge is to retain the execution and capacity benefits of INT4 while keeping the rollout sampler sufficiently close to the BF16 policy. KV4-RL addresses this tension through three coordinated components. It uses calibration-free online quantization to follow the evolving policy, selectively protects a bounded number of sensitive context positions, and executes the INT4 and protected regions without

5.1. A Practical INT4 KV Rollout Path 

A quantizer calibrated at a fixed checkpoint can become stale as policy updates shift activation and KV statistics. Repeating offline calibration throughout training would introduce additional synchronization and data-collection overhead. At the same time, KV compression reduces decode cost by lowering cache traffic and increases feasible concurrency by reducing cache storage. Materializing the cache in higher precision before attention would add memory traffic and computation, substantially weakening the decode-cost benefit of compression. We therefore use a calibration-free representation whose quantization parameters are computed online for each newly produced KV vector and whose compressed values are consumed directly by the attention kernel. We quantize K and V independently using one affine INT4 group for each 128-dimensional token-head vector. The scale ∆ and offset 𝑚 are stored in FP16 alongside the packed 4-bit codes. Bounded selective protection. We refer to the combination of KV4 storage for all positions and bounded INT8 protection as KV4-RL . These results show that sink and recent protection play complementary roles rather than serving as interchangeable uses of the same precision budget. With the sink width fixed at 𝑠 = 16 , Fig. 4(c) shows that increasing the recent width reduces sampler mismatch but progressively decreases decode speedup. We select 𝑤 = 128 as a practical balance, sharply reducing conditional KL while retaining most of KV4’s execution benefit. Fidelity–efficiency trade-off. The default configuration achieves 1.90 × decode-forward speedup over BF16 and retains approximately 94% of KV4 throughput on the same 4B states. Selective protection therefore recovers most of the observed policy-quality loss while retaining most of KV4’s execution benefit. Appendix D.3 reports the complete fidelity and timing results, and Section 6 evaluates whether this trade-off carries through to end-to-end acceleration and learned-policy quality across model sizes. 

5.3. Efficient Attention Execution 

Beyond improving sampler fidelity, the bounded structure of selective protection admits an efficient imple-mentation that preserves the scaling benefits of INT4 KV compression. Unlike finer quantization groups or error-compensation schemes, which introduce additional work for every cached token, its higher-fidelity overhead is restricted to at most 𝑠 + 𝑤 positions per response. KV4-RL also maintains the INT4 and protected representations as independent cache structures. The paged INT4 cache stores every position, while a fixed-capacity INT8 side buffer stores additional copies of the sink and recent positions. Both representations are produced directly from the source K/V vectors when each token is generated. Consequently, a position leaving the recent window requires no transfer or requantization into the main cache since its INT4 entry already exists. The evicted side-buffer slot can simply be reused. Timing protocol. We measure execution time at three nested scopes. Fixed-workload replay measures captured decode forwards at matched states and therefore isolates the execution benefit of KV compression. We exclude initialization, restart downtime, evaluation, and checkpoint saving. Cumulative speedup is computed as the ratio of summed BF16 time to summed method time over the same update interval, rather than as the mean of per-iteration speedup ratios. 6.2. End-to-End Acceleration KV4-RL substantially accelerates rollout and end-to-end training. Across 400 Qwen3-8B-Base updates, KV4-RL achieves 1.80 × cumulative rollout speedup and 1.27 × cumulative full-iteration speedup over BF16, as shown in Fig. 6. It reaches comparable periodic evaluation accuracy in less cumulative training time, while its run-wide mean response length remains within 1% of BF16 (Fig. 7(b)). The timing improvement therefore accompanies a closely matched generation workload. Unlike the fixed-workload replay in Section 4.2, this end-to-end gain includes both faster processing of the resident KV workload and the additional decode concurrency enabled by fitting more context within the same memory budget. The smaller full-iteration gain reflects the remaining learner-side cost: rollout collection accounts for approximately 47% of cumulative BF16 iteration time, while KV4-RL leaves the BF16 learner computation unchanged. After rollout is accelerated, learner computation accounts for 60 .5% of KV4-RL iteration time over the final 50 updates (Appendix D.7), limiting the fraction of total iteration time that further rollout optimization can reduce. 

Rollout acceleration grows with response length. KV4-RL provides smaller but consistent gains on Qwen3-4B-Base, where responses remain shorter. Across 600 updates, it achieves 1.57 × cumulative rollout speedup and 1.17 × cumulative full-iteration speedup (Appendix D.6). The run-wide mean response lengths are closely matched at 1.45k tokens for BF16 and 1.46k for KV4-RL, indicating that the timing difference is not explained by a smaller generated workload. The contrast becomes more pronounced late in training: over the final 50 iterations, both configurations average approximately 2.0k tokens per response at 4B, compared with 5.5k at 8B. Consistently, within the 8B experiment, rollout speedup rises from 1.45 × over iterations 1–100 to 

1.97 × over iterations 351–400 as responses become longer. These trends align with the workload analysis in Section 4.2. Longer contexts increase both the KV traffic processed at each decode step and pressure on the finite cache pool, strengthening the execution and capacity benefits of KV compression.

# 7. Discussion 

7.1. KV Quantization as a Scaling Primitive for Long-Horizon RLVR From sustained concurrency to KV-aware scaling. Asynchronous and partial rollout improve utilization by sustaining generation across long and heterogeneous responses (Fu et al., 2025; Lu et al., 2025; Kimi Team et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2026). Our central finding is that this concurrency also concentrates context processing into fewer and more expensive decode steps, shifting the rollout bottleneck toward the KV cache. KV quantization directly addresses this workload by reducing both the execution cost of each decode state and the memory required to sustain a large active batch. KV4-RL further shows that these gains can translate into faster policy optimization when aggressive compression is paired with sufficient sampler fidelity. KV quantization therefore provides a systems primitive for converting sustained rollout concurrency into end-to-end RLVR acceleration. This mechanism depends on the workload maintained by the rollout engine and is also relevant to disaggregated systems whose rollout workers retain many long prefixes (Li et al., 2027). 

Expanding the scaling frontier. Recent frontier architectures substantially reduce context-dependent memory and computation through compressed, local, or hybrid attention designs (Gemma Team, 2025; MiniMax, 2025; Kimi Team, 2025b; Zhao et al., 2025a). These advances expand the feasible context and concurrency range, but the released capacity is increasingly used to support longer reasoning, larger active batches, and more persistent generation state. The same pressure therefore reappears as systems approach the new operating frontier. MoE architectures alter the balance of FFN computation and communication, but their KV workload remains governed by the attention structure (Yang et al., 2025; Kimi Team, 2025a; Zhao et al., 2025b). This consideration becomes particularly important for frontier reasoning and agentic RL, where rollouts extend into long multi-turn trajectories with tool outputs, environment observations, and persistent histories (Guo et al., 2025; Kimi Team et al., 2025; Kimi Team, 2025a; Tan et al., 2026; Lu et al., 2026). KV quantization complements these architectural advances by pushing the feasible context and concurrency frontier further, positioning it as an increasingly important foundation for scaling long-horizon RLVR. 

7.2. Limitations 

While encouraging, our evaluation focuses on two dense Qwen3 models and mathematical reasoning workloads under a colocated asynchronous GRPO configuration. This setting directly captures the long-generation, high-concurrency regime in which KV concentration becomes important, but it does not cover the broader diversity of modern RLVR systems. Although the underlying mechanism applies whenever rollout workers sustain many long resident contexts, its quantitative impact may vary with model architecture, attention structure, scheduler design, and system placement. Further validation on MoE and hybrid-attention models, disaggregated rollout systems, and broader long-horizon workloads such as agentic and multi-turn RLVR remains an important direction. 

# 8. Conclusion 

In this work, we identified KV concentration as a defining systems consequence of asynchronous RLVR rollout: sustained concurrency reduces low-utilization tail generation but concentrates context processing into fewer and more expensive decode steps. Our analysis and matched-workload measurements showed that this workload shift makes KV cache quantization the most effective acceleration target among the tested quantization configurations. We further found that INT4 KV rollout can degrade learning despite token-level importance correction, demonstrating that aggressive cache compression must be accompanied by sufficient sampler fidelity. Guided by these findings, we proposed KV4-RL, which combines calibration-free INT4 KV storage with bounded INT8 protection of attention sinks and recent context. Across full-parameter GRPO experiments, KV4-RL preserves learned-policy quality close to BF16-rollout training while substantially accelerating rollout and end-to-end training, with larger gains as responses become longer. Together, these results establish KV-aware precision allocation as a practical path for converting sustained rollout concurrency into training acceleration and position KV quantization as an important scaling primitive for long-horizon RLVR.

# A. Implementation Details 

All experiments use an in-house RLVR framework with a tensor-parallel rollout engine and an FSDP2 learner. The implementation incorporates the core inference and training optimizations used in vLLM and verl (Kwon et al., 2023; Sheng et al., 2025), including direct reuse of vLLM’s communication primitives and FlashInfer attention (Ye et al., 2025). These optimizations are enabled in the BF16 baseline as well as the quantized configurations. Precision changes are therefore evaluated within a common optimized execution stack. 

Why a separate framework. Combining quota-based partial rollout with our paged INT4 attention requires coordinated control over response lifetime and cache execution. Prefixes carried across a policy update must be resumed with priority and used to rebuild both the packed KV history and its protected side cache. The custom cache representation also affects memory-budget accounting and attention dispatch within captured model forwards. Supporting this combination through verl and vLLM would require changes across both the collection layer and the engine’s cache interfaces. We implement these components together to make their interaction explicit while retaining the serving and learning optimizations detailed below. 

A.1. Collection and Policy Updates 

Fig. 1 in Section 3 illustrates how collection proceeds across policy updates. Synchronous collection finishes every response assigned to the current iteration. Its request queue eventually empties and the remaining long responses continue with progressively fewer companions. Partial collection instead maintains an oversubscribed pool and stops after enough complete prompt groups have become available. The collector selects the oldest 

𝑇 completed groups for training and retains any additional completed groups for later selection. Unfinished responses preserve their token prefixes and resume with priority when the next collection begins. In the evaluated configuration, the global quota is 𝑇 = 128 groups with 𝐺 = 8 responses per group. An oversubscription factor of 𝛽 = 1 maintains up to 𝑇 (1 + 𝛽 ) groups across the rollout replicas. A group that reaches the age limit 𝜂 = 12 must complete before collection can stop. This requirement can extend collection beyond the completion quota. Here asynchronous rollout refers to the lifetime of responses across updates. Generation and optimization alternate on the same four GPUs. Each response retains the behavior log-probability recorded when each token was sampled. These probabili-ties remain attached to the trajectory when it crosses an update or waits in the completed-group pool. The learner evaluates the current pre-update BF16 policy on the collected tokens and forms the TIS correction in Eq. 3.5. The rollout configuration uses temperature one without top-𝑘 or nucleus truncation, so the recorded softmax probabilities correspond to its sampling distribution. After optimization, the updated weights are copied into the rollout engine. Re-prefill then rebuilds the carried prefixes under those weights while preserving the sampled tokens and their historical behavior probabilities. 

A.2. Common Execution Optimizations Scheduling and attention. The rollout engine stores KV entries in 16-token pages and admits requests according to available pages and the concurrency limit. Continuous batching reuses slots released by completed responses while queued requests remain. Chunked prefill shares a token budget with decode requests, and active-row compaction keeps completed requests out of subsequent model forwards. The BF16 baseline uses FlashInfer paged attention with its Tensor Core decode path (Ye et al., 2025). These choices provide the paged storage and dynamic batching mechanisms used in modern serving engines such as vLLM (Kwon et al., 2023). 

Model execution and communication. The engine fuses QKV projections and the MLP gate/up projections. Compiled model execution reduces dispatch overhead and intermediate tensor allocations around attention. Multiple CUDA graphs cover different decode batch sizes, allowing the scheduler to select a captured forward as concurrency changes. Tensor-parallel communication reuses vLLM’s custom all-reduce implementation with PyNCCL as a fallback. BF16 and quantized samplers share these scheduling and execution mechanisms. Precision-specific conversion work remains inside the timed forward when it is required during decoding. 

Learner execution. The learner updates all model parameters with FSDP2. BF16 forward and backward computation use FP32 master parameters and optimizer states. Padding is removed before variable-length attention, and token-budget microbatches balance the work of responses with different lengths. Fused projection and log-probability computation avoid retaining full-vocabulary logits for every training token. Activation checkpointing reduces the update’s peak memory. The FSDP2 state-management and offloading utilities follow

Phase memory management. Before rollout, optimizer-only state and the frozen reference model are offloaded to make memory available for the KV pool. The updated learner parameters are streamed into the engine’s tensor-parallel weight buffers. Before optimization, rollout allocations are released and the learner state is restored. Virtual-memory-backed allocations preserve addresses used by captured graphs across these transitions. The memory profiler reserves model state and execution workspace before sizing the KV pool, including precision-dependent metadata and protected side-cache storage. If 𝑀 𝑞 denotes the memory remaining for cache format 𝑞 and 𝑏 𝑞 denotes its bytes per 16-token page, the allocator provisions ⌊𝑀 𝑞 /𝑏 𝑞 ⌋ pages. The scheduler admits a request only when both a request slot and its required pages are available. KV compression can therefore improve rollout through two distinct mechanisms: reducing the cost of processing the current decode state and delaying the point at which page capacity restricts active concurrency. The bounded side-cache allocation reduces the available page pool for KV4-RL but does not grow with the retained context length. 

A.3. Paged INT4 Attention with Selective Protection 

Section 5.3 and Fig. 5 summarize the numerical paths and their integration with partial rollout. The following paragraphs detail the encoding, allocation, and regional merge. 

Encoding and allocation. Following the K-only rotation design of SAW-INT4 (Jia et al., 2026), Q and K use a normalized Hadamard transform after RoPE, while V remains in its original basis. Each 128-element group uses affine INT4 codes with FP16 scale and offset. Codes occupy half a byte per scalar and metadata adds 4/128 

bytes. INT8 side entries use symmetric per-token/head scaling and are computed from source vectors before INT4 rounding. The INT4 history covers every position, so a token leaving the recent ring needs no historical requantization. Sink size 16 aligns the beginning of the unprotected INT4 region with a page boundary. Side storage is reserved per request slot and included in the complete cache allocation. 

Integer decode path. Query rotation and INT8 quantization are fused into query preparation. The attention kernel loads packed pages into shared memory and widens four-bit codes using mask and shift operations. Integer QK MMA accumulates into INT32. Scales and affine offsets convert these accumulators to floating-point scores. After FP32 softmax, the main PV path forms an eight-bit probability operand with the required value-scale factors. A second integer MMA and affine correction produce the regional output without materializing the full history in BF16. 

Protected path and reconstruction. The protected path reuses the prepared query for integer QK and dequantizes only the INT8 side values for BF16 PV with FP32 accumulation. It avoids the main path’s probability quantization. Prefill and carried-prefix reconstruction use BF16 attention and populate both cache representations from newly computed K/V. Historical behavior probabilities remain attached to the sampled tokens during this reconstruction, as described in Section A.1. 

Regional softmax merge. For each disjoint region 𝑟 ∈ { 4, 8}, let 𝑜 𝑟 be its locally normalized output and 𝑧 𝑟 its log-normalizer. The outputs are merged as 

𝑧 = log( 𝑒 𝑧 4 + 𝑒 𝑧 8 ), 𝑜 = 𝑒 𝑧 4−𝑧 𝑜 4 + 𝑒 𝑧 8−𝑧 𝑜 8. (A1) A stable log-sum-exp reduction evaluates this identity, with zero weight for empty regions. The merge is exact for the regional scores and outputs, which already reflect their numerical paths. The same identity merges context splits within each region.

B.6. Rollout and Training Time

The proposition isolates the scheduling mechanism for a fixed workload, while the frozen-checkpoint traces in Fig. 2 measure the resulting KV concentration under finite request pools, page constraints, and the actual quota-based scheduler.
