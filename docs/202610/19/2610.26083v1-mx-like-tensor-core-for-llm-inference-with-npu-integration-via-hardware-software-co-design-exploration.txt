## Abstract 

Block-scaled quantization formats such as OCP Microscaling (MX) and NVFP4 have been widely adopted as efficient data formats for LLM inference. However, the design space of these formats spanned by shared scale, weight, and activation format combinations has not been systematically explored, and the joint accuracy–silicon tradeoff across this space remains uncharacterized, leaving the true Pareto-optimal configurations for NPU-based edge LLM deploy-ment an open question. To bridge this gap, we conduct a joint hardware–software (HW–SW) co-design exploration of the above underexplored MX-like design space with three innovations. 1) We define and systematically characterize the full 8-bit scale format family within the MX-like design space, jointly with asymmetric weight-activation precision, identifying configurations that out-perform the MX and NVFP4-like (without per-tensor scaling, PTS) baselines. 2) We build a per-format specialized MAC datapath tem-plate that enables fair hardware cost evaluation across the space. 3) We develop an automated co-design flow, integrated into the open-source SNAX NPU platform, that enables joint HW-SW anal-ysis across the design space. Overall, our co-design exploration reveals that scale format matters only under aggressive FP4 quan-tization, yielding a case-oriented design guideline for both scale format and asymmetric weight-activation precision. We further identify UE5M3 as a favorable accuracy–hardware tradeoff for the FP4 regime, reducing perplexity degradation by up to 25.3% relative to the NVFP4-like baseline, at a cost of only +0.06% area and +0.49% power overhead.

## 1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable per-formance across a wide range of tasks, from natural language under-standing to code generation [13]. However, this growing capability comes with a corresponding growth in model size [3], demand-ing ever more memory and computational resources. This makes efficient LLM deployment a significant challenge, an issue that be-comes even more pressing when deployment targets shift from datacenter GPUs [19] toward edge NPUs [37]. Block-scaled quantization [9, 10, 23, 29] addresses this by quan-tizing small groups within the tensor and assigning a shared scale to each group to accommodate its local distribution. This allows high-precision values to be stored and computed in low-bitwidth datatypes, reducing memory footprint and power consumption while preserving accuracy. MX adopts an unsigned power-of-two block scale (UE8M0) for its prime hardware efficiency, while NVFP4 instead adopts a fractional UE4M3 block scale to improve quantization granularity. However, MX and NVFP4 each commit to a single point in the scale format space, UE8M0 and UE4M3, respectively. On the algo-rithmic side, prior works [11, 29] have only explored a small part of the MX-like design space in Fig. 1: [29] sweeps element formats across INT8, FP8, FP6, and FP4 but fixes the scale format to E8M0, while [11] explores fractional scale formats but restricts the analy-sis to FP4, leaving the joint interaction between scale format and element format not yet fully characterized. This uncertainty is com-pounded by a further consideration: activation and weight tensors exhibit different distributions [33, 34]. Activations are heavy-tailed with significant outliers, whereas weights follow a near-Gaussian profile, suggesting that asymmetric weight-activation configura-tions could be beneficial. Such asymmetry has mainly been studied in weight-only quantization at per-tensor and per-token granular-ity [33, 34], but with limited attention in the block-scaled setting. On the hardware side, the silicon cost surface over the MX-like format space remains unquantified. Prior MX-compatible MAC dat-apaths fix the scale to UE8M0 with symmetric (activation, weight) operands, either as a single FP8 implementation [15, 18] or as a runtime precision-scalable unit that reuses the hardware for all MX element formats [7, 8], making the intrinsic cost of one format combination unobservable. Besides, fractional scales introduce a scale-mantissa multiplier and the operand pairs change the datap-ath width throughout the whole datapath, which couples the cost of one format axis to the other two. Measuring this space fairly requires a per-format combination datapath under one uniform micro-architecture. Moreover, the above two gaps are not independent. Examining accuracy and hardware cost in isolation is insufficient, since a for-mat that excels on one may be suboptimal on the other. Algorithmic studies [11, 29] treat hardware cost with varying and often limited rigor rather than systematic silicon evaluation, while prior hard-ware designs [7, 15] characterize silicon cost for a fixed format. As a result, the joint accuracy-silicon Pareto frontier for edge NPU deployment remains unmapped. In this work, as shown in Fig. 1(left), we address these gaps with a joint hardware–software (HW–SW) co-design exploration of the MX-like design space, spanned by the scale, weight, and activation formats, that lies between MX and NVFP4. To fairly expose the cost of each format combination despite the coupling across format axes, we build a per-format specialized datapath under one uniform micro-architecture, and integrate it into an automated flow for joint accuracy-silicon analysis targeting edge NPU deployment. Our contributions are as follows: 

• MX-like design space characterization: We identify a pre-viously overlooked region within the MX-like design space, spanning intermediate scale formats and asymmetric weight-activation precision, and systematically characterize it to reveal configurations that outperform both the MX and the NVFP4-like baselines. 

• Parameterized MAC datapath template: We design a param-eterized template for an MX-compatible datapath to support MX-like format combinations and evaluate the variation of hardware cost across the design space. 

• Automated co-design flow: We provide an automated end-to-end flow encompassing hardware generation, system inte-gration, and numerical verification, enabling efficient design space exploration (DSE) and HW-SW co-analysis that identifies practical configurations under application-specific accuracy and hardware budget constraints. After constructing the Pareto front for hardware efficiency and software accuracy, we find that the UE8M0 scale is preferred above A6W6 while the UE5M3 scale dominates FP4 quantization, with asymmetric configurations and narrow-exponent element formats offering further gains. Together, these findings provide case-oriented guidelines for selecting block-scaled quantization configurations under application-specific accuracy and hardware constraints. 

## 2 BACKGROUND AND MOTIVATION 

Table 1 positions our work against the prior works along the five axes that reveal the gap. To the best of our knowledge, no prior art spans all five. We elaborate on the following aspects. 

Quantization algorithms for LLMs: Recent algorithmic stud-ies refine the block-scaled recipe in two directions. The first di-rection tunes the scale format itself. [5] sweeps over FP8-typed scales in FP4 training and identifies fractional E4M3 scales as the accuracy optimum. Fasoli et al. [11] propose UE5M3 scale for FP4 inference. The second direction diversifies the representation. Per-block schemes such as AMXFP4 [17] and BlockDialect [16] establish the value of asymmetric, block-specific formats. The strongest hard-ware evidence in this line consists of spot checks of one proposal against one baseline: a single-point area comparison of UE5M3 scale logic [11], a MAC synthesis placing AMXFP4 about 10% over MXFP4 [17], and a MAC-level match against FP4 [16]. None of these studies map how cost varies across the scale family, how it couples with the element formats, or whether the FP4-regime findings hold at higher precision, motivating our extension of the design space.

## 3 Design space exploration 3.1 MX-like Design Space Characterization 

We define the MX-like design space over the full 8-bit scale format family, paired with symmetric and asymmetric weight-activation precision. Unlike NVFP4’s UE4M3 scale, which leaves its sign bit permanently unused for FP8 alignment, this family reallocates every bit to exponent range or mantissa precision, and we characterize its accuracy landscape to identify practical configurations. The element formats evaluated, FP4 E2M1, FP6 E2M3/E3M2, FP8 E4M3/E5M2, and MXINT8, follow the OCP Microscaling (MX) specification [27], spanning precisions from 8 bits down to 4 bits, the range most relevant to edge deployment. First, the scale format’s exponent width has a hard floor. As Fig. 2(a) shows for Qwen2.5-3B, formats with fewer than 4 exponent bits (UE3M5 to UE0M8) degrade accuracy severely across most weight/activation combinations, because the scale cannot represent the activation tensor’s dynamic range. This restricts the practical scale formats from UE8M0 to UE4M4 and UE4M3, ruling out approximately half of the design space. Second, within this range, fractional scale formats such as UE5M3 and UE4M4 provide a measurable accuracy advantage over UE8M0 and NVFP4’s UE4M3, particularly in the FP4 regime. In contrast, NVFP4 additionally introduces a second-level FP32 per-tensor scale on top of its block scale [23]. As illustrated in Fig. 2(b), incorporating this PTS yields only marginal accuracy improvement within this range, at the cost of two additional whole-tensor memory traversals for tensor-level scaling. Since choosing a better scale format achieves a larger accuracy gain without this overhead, this work prioritizes scale format selection over per-tensor scaling. Third, asymmetric weight-activation configurations further improve accuracy over symmetric ones at the same weight precision, an effect that is most pronounced in the FP4 regime, as depicted in Fig. 2(b), motivating support for asymmetric operation in the datapath. The above findings narrow the practical design space to UE8M0 through UE4M4 without a PTS, and identify fractional scale formats such as UE5M3 and UE4M4 as the most promising candidates for the FP4 regime. ## 3.2 Datapath Template Implementation 

To investigate how activation, weight and scale precision affect the hardware implementation, we propose a MAC datapath tem-plate. Fig. 3a shows the datapath. The pre-processor forms four products of operands which a shared reduction tree then sums. Alignment before the tree is performed against a pre-computed anchor position, which avoids comparing exponents across prod-ucts [18]. A scale multiplier follows, and a bi-directional shifter aligns the partial result with the current scaled term through a carry-propagation adder (CPA). Then the accumulation result goes through a leading-zero detection (LZD) normalizer and round-ties-to-even (RNE) rounder [27]. The output after the LZD normalizer and rounder is written back to the accumulation register, which is read out again as the running sum for the next MAC cycle. Prior MX-compatible designs focus on FP8 operands with E8M0 scale [15, 18]. Instead, to enable our exploration into the MX-like design space, our proposed general hardware template is built on top of it with two innovations: ❶ When the scale factor is frac-tional and cannot be folded into the bi-directional shifter as a power-of-two, we implement an additional multiplier after the reduction tree. ❷ We parameterize the datapath on five format axes (e A, mA, eW, mW, mS), representing the exponent and mantissa widths of activations, weights, and shared scales 1 and generate a datapath specialized to each configuration. A Chisel [2] generator evaluates those parameters at elaboration time and emits one RTL instance per design point. This parameterization absorbs asymmet-ric operand pairs and fractional scale, enabling a fair comparison of the hardware cost across the MX-like design space. Table 2 gives each stage’s width as a function of these axes. These widths reveal two properties that drive the cost. First, the operand exponents set the tree width from both ends. ## 3.3 Automated Co-design Framework 

The joint HW-SW DSE requires evaluating hundreds of quantiza-tion configurations, each demanding a matched hardware instance and a consistent software test setup. Performing this manually means hand-deriving the run-time values that define memory lay-out and dataflow mapping for every configuration, and manually keeping the software reference and hardware RTL in sync, a pro-cess that is both labor-intensive and error-prone at this scale. We therefore introduce an automated co-design flow that derives every downstream parameter, dataflow mapping, and RTL instance from a single design-time input, without manual calculation. Our co-design flow builds on the SNAX platform [1], an open-source RISC-V compute cluster in which a lightweight Snitch [36] core offloads computation to attached accelerators through Control and Status Register (CSR) instructions. As shown in Fig. 3b, a shared scratchpad memory and parameterized data streamers absorb the varying data widths across our DSE, while within the tensor core a CSR manager, a two-dimensional PE array with design-time con-figurable spatial parallelism, and a re-quantization unit carry out the MAC and format conversion. Building on this platform, the flow is driven by a top-level or-chestrator, as illustrated in Fig. 3c. The orchestrator takes a unified set of design-time parameters including operand datatypes, scale datatypes, workload dimension and tensor core dimension, and translates them into the configurations that feed the downstream flow. It then drives two parallel paths from this shared configuration. On the software compilation path, the orchestrator invokes data generation to produce quantized test data and software reference outputs by our custom quantization function, along with derivation of run-time CSR values for the target quantization configuration and workload to define the memory layout and computation dataflow. Together with a run-time library, this path yields a software binary. In parallel, the selected LLM is quantized by our custom quantizer and evaluated on the target dataset to obtain the accuracy bench-mark. On the hardware generation path, the tensor core datapath is generated directly according to the orchestrator’s parameters, while the derived SNAX configuration, including the streamer width com-puted from the target bandwidth requirement, is fed into the SNAX toolchain to generate the surrounding components such as the streamers and CSR manager. Both steps use Chisel [2] to produce synthesizable HW RTL. The software binary and hardware RTL are then co-simulated to confirm that the generated hardware behaves consistently with the software reference. ## 4 Experimental results 4.1 Experiment Setup 

On the software side, we extend the open-source MX quantization library of microxcaling [21] and BitMoD [4] to produce identical numerical results matching the hardware datapath. We evaluate four language models: Qwen2.5-3B [24], Qwen3-1.7B [25], Llama-3.2-1B [13], and Gemma-3-1B [12] on the full test split of WikiText-2 [20] and 256 randomly sampled sequences of 2048 tokens from C4 [26] to generalize our findings across datasets. Accuracy is measured as the perplexity degradation ΔPPL % as defined in Eq. (2). On the hardware side, all generated designs are synthesized at 400 MHz with Synopsys Design Compiler on a TSMC 28 nm process, using standard cells at 0.8 V supply in the typical-typical corner. Power is estimated with Synopsys PrimeTime PX from the switch-ing activity of a gate-level simulation over 2048 MAC operations to stabilize the average activity. At the system level, we instantiate a 4 ×16 PE array and its re-quantization units in the SNAX clus-ter [35] to capture the full tensor-core cost, driven by a 32 ×1024 tile of real weight and activation tensors from a representative trans-former layer of Qwen2.5-3B [24], whose distribution we observe to be consistent across layers. The reported area and power include the on-chip storage and data-movement overheads associated with asymmetric operand widths and fractional-scale metadata. Every configuration maps to the same 64-PE array at an easily achiev-able timing constraint of 400 MHz to ensure the minimum area across all designs and therefore sustains an identical throughput of 204.8 GOPS. 

## 4.2 Accuracy Characterization 

Building on observations in Sec. 3.1, we first evaluate the UE5M3– UE4M4 divergence and the limits of asymmetric precision across four models. The effect of scale format on accuracy depends criti-cally on the target precision regime: it is negligible at high precision but becomes decisive once quantization is pushed aggressively. Tak-ing A6W6 as representative of the high-precision regime, all four scale formats yield ΔPPL % within a narrow 1.0–1.4% range across every model, as Fig. 4 shows, making the choice of scale format largely insensitive at this precision. This changes sharply in the FP4 quantization regime. In the extreme A4W4 case, the coarse UE8M0 scale causes severe degrada-tion, averaging 45.0%, while UE5M3, UE4M4 and NVFP4’s UE4M3 substantially outperform it, cutting average ΔPPL % roughly in half. UE5M3 and UE4M4 further outperform UE4M3 by approximately 5 percentage points on average, by reclaiming the unused bit in the format, a gap that stems from Eq. (1). Yet the two are not in-terchangeable, since UE5M3 widens dynamic range while UE4M4 improves precision. Fig. 4 also shows that this divergence surfaces sharply on Qwen3-1.7B. Its late-layer activation outliers, once quan-tized to MXINT8, overflow UE4M4’s and UE4M3’s four-bit exponent range, causing a severe degradation, whereas UE5M3’s extended exponent range absorbs the same outliers and stays the more robust choice. Further raising acti-vation precision to MXINT8 (A8W4) trims UE5M3’s degradation only slightly further, to 6.1%, while UE4M4 and UE4M3 instead rise to 16.0% and 19.2% due to their limited exponent range. 

## 4.3 Hardware Cost Evaluation 

In this subsection, we evaluate where an MX-like format spends its silicon cost, from the datapath stages up to the system level. We first break the datapath into stages across formats, as Fig. 5(a) shows. The accumulator is consistently the largest, holding 39–66% of the area. A fractional scale adds a scale-multiplier that reaches 36% and that a UE8M0 scale lacks, a cost traceable to Table 2, where the multiplier shares the accumulator’s Mant Term field. We then fix the scale at UE8M0 and sweep operand pairs, tracking area and power against the aligned sum-of-products (SoP) width growing with e and m of operands, as shown in Fig. 5(b). Over SoP from 11 to 67 bits the MAC area grows 4.9 × but power only 2.6 ×,since area follows the SoP width whereas power is set by actual switching in the multiplier and scatters against SoP. The fractional scale’s multiplier operates on this same SoP field, coupling its cost to the operand pair, as presented in Fig. 5(c). The heaviest scale, UE4M4, adds about 0 .5 × 10 3𝜇𝑚 2 on the narrow INT8 ×E2M1 but 1.9 × 10 3𝜇𝑚 2 on the wide E5M2 ×E5M2, a 3.6 × spread that no single design point reveals. At the system level, where deployment budgets bind, these mech-anisms pay off, as illustrated in Fig. 5(d). Of the 126 configurations, 60 draw both less area and power than the conventional symmetric-FP8 baseline, E4M3 ×E4M3 with UE8M0, which uses 0 .708 𝑚𝑚 2 and 64 .5𝑚𝑊 , and 30 of these are asymmetric with a fractional scale. A representative case, marked as a green star, is E2M3 ×E2M1 with UE5M3, using 0 .646 𝑚𝑚 2 and 51 .6𝑚𝑊 , 9% less area and 20% less power, while carrying a fractional scale the default lacks. However, the cost axis alone cannot decide which affordable format is worth adopting. The cost-only optimum, marked as a blue cross, is the FP4 pair at UE8M0, using 0 .606 𝑚𝑚 2 and 38 .3𝑚𝑊 , and is ruled out by the accuracy characterization in Sec. 4.2. 

## 4.4 Hardware–Software Co-analysis 

We now bring accuracy and hardware cost together to locate the true Pareto-optimal configurations, using the joint HW-SW co-analysis in Fig. 6. Each point is one configuration of the MX-like space, its vertical position the mean ΔPPL % across four models and its horizontal position the system-level cost, with every instance behind these points generated, verified, and synthesized by our automated co-design flow. We find that the two single-sided conclusions each hold in exactly one precision regime. Above A6W6 the choice is dominated by hardware cost, as UE8M0 is Pareto-optimal, while a fractional scale buys no accuracy but adds up to +22% system power and +17% area over the UE8M0 baseline. The FP4 regime inverts this: the same UE4M4 scale now buys 27 percentage points of ΔPPL from 45.0% under UE8M0 down to 17.9%, while at FP4 operands the same fractional overhead falls to +4.6% area and +20.8% power. Within the fractional-scale family, UE5M3 emerges as the sweet spot, recovering 25.3% of ΔPPL relative to the NVFP4-like UE4M3 at only +0.06% area and +0.49% power over that baseline, and showing more robustness than UE4M4, as discussed in Sec. 4.2. UE5M3 is therefore our recommended FP4 scale format. We further mark three deployment thresholds in Fig. 6: 1) a 20 % perplexity-degradation budget, guided by comparisons with smaller unquantized models of the same families in our experiments, 2) a 60 mW power budget, corresponding to 3413 GOPS/W at our con-stant throughput which is close to the prior precision-scalable MX clusters in FP4 mode [7], and 3) a 0.7 mm 2 area budget, in line with prior MX systems at 0.59–0.60 mm 2 on more advanced nodes [7, 15], with headroom at the 28 nm technology we use. Note that our con-clusions are not tied to these specific values, and they hold for any accuracy threshold in 15–25% and any power budget in 56–64 mW. Within them, neither traditional baseline is feasible. Symmetric MXFP4 fails at 45.0% and the per-tensor-scale-free NVFP4-like UE4M3 configuration at 22.9%, while most feasible points are asym-metric, fractional-scale configurations that neither standard has defined before, led by A6W4 (E2M3 ×E2M1) with UE5M3 scale. ## 5 Conclusion 

In this work, we characterize the previously unexplored joint accuracy-silicon Pareto frontier of the MX-like design space, spanned by scale format, weight format, and activation format. We define the full 8-bit scale format family jointly with asymmetric weight-activation precision, build a per-format specialized MAC datapath template for fair hardware cost evaluation across the space, and integrate both into an automated co-design flow on the open-source SNAX NPU platform. Our joint exploration shows that scale format is largely inconsequential above A6W6, where UE8M0 remains Pareto-optimal, but becomes decisive under FP4, where UE5M3 paired with asymmetric weight-activation preci-sion reduces perplexity degradation by up to 25.3% relative to the NVFP4-like baseline with only a negligible hardware overhead.
