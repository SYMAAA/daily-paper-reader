Abstract 

Large language models (LLMs) require sub-stantial memory, motivating low-bit weight quantization for efficient deployment. Exist-ing vector quantization (VQ) methods often improve accuracy by increasing the effective coding dimension, which can complicate inde-pendent lookup and local codeword updates. We propose C OS-VQ (Coordinated Shared-Scale Vector Quantization), which preserves low-dimensional codewords while jointly op-timizing hard indices and their shared scale across positions. The key challenge is scale-induced coupling: the optimal scale depends on the entire codeword combination and, in turn, changes candidate rankings across posi-tions, so pointwise updates can stall even af-ter scale re-optimization. COS-VQ collects candidates at multiple probe scales and con-structs a tangent separable upper bound for the residual-aware quadratic objective. By reduc-ing candidate comparisons to lower envelopes of lines over positive scales, it identifies finitely many representative intervals for joint index– scale updates without exhaustively enumerat-ing codeword combinations. On Qwen3.8-27B, COS-VQ outperforms QTIP and LLVQ on the evaluated language modeling, coding, and rea-soning benchmarks. Ablations from the same initialization further show larger gains from joint updates than from scale-only refinement, highlighting joint selection as an alternative to increasing codeword dimensionality. The code is available at https://github.com/xzktx 003/Cos_VQ .

Large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable capabilities across diverse tasks, in-cluding natural language understanding, complex reasoning, and code generation. Despite these

advances, the growing scale of LLMs poses sig-nificant challenges to their efficient deployment. Representative models such as LLaMA-3.1-70B (Grattafiori et al., 2024a) and Qwen3.8-27B (Qwen Team, 2026b) contain tens of billions of param-eters, with a 70B-parameter model alone requir-ing approximately 140 GB of memory to store its weights in 16-bit precision. Beyond the sub-stantial memory footprint, autoregressive inference involves repeated weight access, placing consider-able pressure on memory bandwidth and further limiting deployment efficiency. These challenges have driven growing interest in model compression techniques that reduce memory requirements and inference costs while preserving the capabilities of the original models. Post-training quantization (PTQ) has emerged as a practical paradigm for reducing the storage and inference costs of LLMs without full model retraining (Zhao et al., 2026a,c,b,d, 2025). Among existing approaches, vector quantization (VQ) rep-resents multiple weights using a single code in-dex, offering greater representational flexibility than conventional scalar quantization, particularly at extremely low bitrates. Recent methods such as QTIP and LLVQ further improve quantization quality by exploiting high-dimensional representa-tions through trellis and lattice structures (Tseng et al., 2024b; van der Ouderaa et al., 2026). Al-though these structures alleviate the exponential search and storage costs associated with unstruc-tured high-dimensional codebooks, they introduce structural dependencies that can restrict the inde-pendent lookup and local modification of small codewords. This raises a fundamental question: 

Can low-dimensional codewords retain their in-dependent lookup and local update capabilities while achieving the quantization quality of high-dimensional structured methods through a broader range of joint decisions?

In shared-scale VQ, multiple positions within a scale group are as-signed low-dimensional codewords while sharing a common scale parameter. Crucially, the op-timal scale depends on the entire codeword as-signment and, in turn, changes the relative pref-erence among candidate codewords at different positions. This bidirectional coupling creates a challenging optimization landscape: even when the shared scale is re-optimized after each single-index update, coordinate-wise refinement can stall because no individual index change reduces the reconstruction error, whereas coordinated changes across multiple positions can still yield improve-ments. As illustrated in Figure 1, the reconstruction error plateaus under pointwise refinement but de-creases again after switching to joint-index updates, with accepted updates frequently modifying mul-tiple indices. These observations motivate joint codeword selection under a shared scale, enabling a broader optimization scope without increasing the dimensionality of the underlying codewords. To address this challenge, we propose COS-VQ (Coordinated Shared-Scale Vector Quantiza-tion), which jointly optimizes hard codeword in-dices and their shared scale while preserving low-dimensional codewords. Directly optimizing these variables is computationally intractable because the layer-output reconstruction objective contains cross-position quadratic interactions, and exhaus-tive enumeration of codeword combinations incurs exponential complexity. We first construct candi-date pools by combining local shortlists obtained at multiple probe scales, preventing the search from being restricted to candidate rankings under a sin-gle scale. We then derive a residual-aware tan-gent separable upper bound that transforms the coupled quadratic objective into candidate-wise quadratic functions of the shared scale. By elim-inating candidate-independent terms, codeword comparisons over positive scales can be expressed as lower envelopes of lines. Merging their switch-ing points partitions the scale domain into finitely many representative intervals, within each of which the joint codeword assignment remains constant and the scale can be optimized through a con-strained one-dimensional quadratic problem. The resulting solver directly updates hard indices and the shared scale without probabilistic relaxation or gradient-based optimization, while verifying ac-cepted updates against the true layer-output error. The experiments further support this joint-selection design, as illustrated by the language-modeling and downstream-task results in Figure 2. 2 Related Work 

Vector quantization methods. Low-bit quantiza-tion methods span scalar quantization, explicit vec-tor codebooks, additive or residual codebooks, and structured lattice or trellis encodings. GPTVQ (van Baalen et al., 2024) combines vector representa-tions with layer reconstruction; VPTQ (Liu et al., 2024) constructs explicit codebooks using second-order objectives; AQLM (Egiazarian et al., 2024) uses additive codebooks and block-level refine-ment; and CRVQ (Xu et al., 2024) relaxes con-straints for critical channels. QuIP# (Tseng et al., 2024a) combines incoherence processing with lat-tice quantization, while QTIP (Tseng et al., 2024b) and LLVQ (van der Ouderaa et al., 2026) exploit trellis and lattice structures to increase effective coding dimensionality. OstQuant (Hu et al., 2025) learns orthogonal and scaling transforms for scalar grids. RSAVQ (Xu et al., 2025b), PCDVQ (Yue et al., 2025), RWKVQuant (Xu et al., 2025a), and KBVQ-MoE (Xu et al., 2026) leverage curva-ture, polar magnitude–direction decoupling, hybrid scalar–vector codes, and expert structure, respec-tively. Unstructured high-dimensional codebooks incur costly nearest-neighbor search and cache de-mands, whereas structured encodings depend on constraints or specialized decoding kernels. In con-trast, we study low-dimensional codebooks with a shared scale, retaining portability, independent lookup, and local rewriting while using coordinated cross-position selection to approach the quality of high-dimensional structured quantization. 

Quantization optimization methods. On inter-action search and parameter updates, GPTVQ (van Baalen et al., 2024) employs sequential compensa-tion, AQLM (Egiazarian et al., 2024) adopts beam search, VPTQ (Liu et al., 2024) leverages second-order refinement, CRVQ (Xu et al., 2024) mod-els critical channels, and KBVQ-MoE (Xu et al., 2026) accounts for expert-aggregation bias. PV-Tuning (Malinovskii et al., 2024), GPTVQ (van Baalen et al., 2024), GSQ (Dadgarnia et al., 2026), and LLVQ (van der Ouderaa et al., 2026) explore alternating updates or closed-form solutions for discrete assignments and continuous scales. In contrast, C OS-VQ jointly optimizes hard indices and their shared positive scale through scale elim-ination, a residual-aware tangent separable upper bound, and lower-envelope interval search, avoid-ing heuristic enumeration of the Cartesian product.

4 Method 

Overview. We propose C OS-VQ to improve low-bit VQ through coordinated index selection un-der an existing shared scale, without increasing the base-codeword dimension or introducing new stored parameters. As illustrated in Figure 3, the method alternates between two steps. First, a residual-aware quadratic majorizer controls cross-position interactions, while multiple scale probes provide codeword candidates beyond those pre-ferred at the current scale. Second, a lower-envelope solver jointly selects hard indices and their shared positive scale by searching finitely many scale intervals. Every proposed update is checked against the true calibration layer-output er-ror. The complete schedule and pseudocode appear in Appendix A.1.1, and a checkable coordinated-update example is given in Appendix A.2.

6 Conclusion 

We presented C OS-VQ, a low-bit VQ frame-work preserving independently accessible, locally rewritable low-dimensional codewords through coordinated shared-scale selection. To address coordinate-wise stalls after scale re-optimization, COS-VQ combines multi-scale candidate pools, a residual-aware tangent separable upper bound, and lower-envelope search to jointly optimize hard indices and scales without exhaustive enumera-tion. Same-start ablations show improvements over scale-only refinement on Qwen3-4B/8B. At approx-imately 2 bpw, C OS-VQ outperforms QTIP and 8LLVQ on language modeling, coding, and selected reasoning benchmarks for Qwen3.8-27B. These findings establish joint selection as a promising alternative to increasing codeword dimensionality. 

Limitations 

CoS-VQ optimizes hard indices and shared scales within finite candidate pools, so its theoretical optimality guarantee applies to the surrogate ob-jective rather than the original layer-output er-ror. Although true-error verification ensures non-increasing accepted updates, the surrogate may be too conservative to escape certain coordinate-wise stalls. Moreover, candidate quality depends on the multi-scale probe coverage and the chosen code-book. Our experiments focus on selected LLM families and benchmarks, leaving broader archi-tectures and extremely low-bit configurations for future evaluation. Finally, inference efficiency de-pends on hardware and codebook lookup imple-mentations, and performance across different de-ployment environments remains to be explored. 

Ethical Considerations 

CoS-VQ reduces the memory requirements of exist-ing LLMs through post-training quantization with-out modifying their underlying training objectives. Improved compression may facilitate more acces-sible and resource-efficient deployment, although actual energy savings depend on hardware and serv-ing conditions and have not been independently measured. Quantization can alter model outputs, potentially affecting factual reliability, fairness, and safety-related behavior. Our evaluations do not constitute dedicated safety, bias, or privacy assess-ments. Furthermore, reduced deployment costs may also facilitate harmful applications of LLMs. We therefore recommend retaining appropriate ac-cess controls and safety safeguards, together with task-specific evaluations, before practical deploy-ment.

When the domain consists of a single scale, directly select the lowest-cost codeword at every position. In all cases, every legal scale is considered through at least one interval closure or this singleton rule. Comparing all such minima therefore gives the global surrogate minimum over the specified pools and legal scales. □

What the theorem does not claim. The theorem neither certifies the full-codebook optimum (be-cause Pi may be truncated) nor the true-objective optimum (because Q replaces cross-position cur-vature by a majorizer). On envelope boundaries, different tied assignments may have different true 

errors even though their surrogate values coincide. The basic solver selects one deterministic surrogate-optimal assignment and verifies that proposal, with-out enumerating all tied true-objective alternatives.

A.4.2 Group Scope, Numerical Precision, and Verification 

Group updates include every position covered by the scale, even if only m indices can change. Sin-gleton positions contribute quadratic coefficients but no switching points. To study the effect of m,keep the existing scale-group partition, codebooks, and scale count fixed; otherwise changes in scale granularity confound the comparison. If weights are reconstructed in a rotated coordinate system, both W and X must be defined in the correspond-ing transformed coordinates and decoding must consistently apply any inverse transform. For discrete scales, record the stored dtype, pos-itive admissible range, and policy for subnormal and boundary values. Higher precision may be used to compute intersection points and coeffi-cients, but actual objective verification must use the reconstructed weights after converting the cho-sen scale to the stored dtype. When the nearest representable scale lies outside a candidate inter-val, use the inward predecessor or successor rather than associating that rounded value with an invalid interval representative. The stopping rule should state the exact absolute and relative thresholds used; when both are used, a defensible convention is 

ηt = max {ηabs , η rel F (z0)} with ηabs > 0. This specifies a reproducible policy but should match the actual implementation, rather than being presented as an experimental setting without verification. 

A.4.3 Reproducibility and Fair Evaluation 

Implementations can batch-evaluate candidate co-efficients before building position-wise envelopes. For memory efficiency, record the initial codeword representative, the sequence of switching events, and the best interval ID; replay the events once to reconstruct the best full index assignment instead of storing one M -length assignment per interval. Only the final surrogate-minimizing proposal is verified by the true error in the base algorithm. Op-tional reranking of every interval representative or gradient-based post-search scale refinement should be reported as a separate algorithmic step with its additional cost. Experimental reporting should fix configura-tions, seeds, model-checkpoint identifiers, code-book construction, calibration samples, and soft-ware versions. Accuracy improvements on calibra-tion data must not be substituted for unseen bench-mark results. Comparisons to numbers published elsewhere should identify their source and any dif-17 ferences in quantized layers, actual bit budgets, calibration data, or evaluation protocol. If a single-GPU sequential execution policy is used, record it alongside measured preprocessing, search, fine-tuning, and inference runtimes so that envelope-only complexity is not mistaken for total wall-clock cost.
