## Abstract 

Vision-Language Models (VLMs) have demonstrated remarkable multimodal capabilities but suffer from prohibitive computational overhead due to the massive number of visual tokens. While token pruning is a promising solution, existing methods still suffer from two major drawbacks: 1) unreliable pruning criteria that fail to accu-rately localize task-specific regions due to inherent attention biases or query-agnostic designs, and 2) suboptimal pruning strategies that employ rigid, non-adaptive schedules, overlooking the dynamic evolution of token redundancy across different model depths. To ad-dress these challenges, we propose LearnPrunerV2, which extends the previous two-stage heuristic framework (LearnPruner) into a fully learnable architecture. Specifically, we integrate lightweight learnable modules into every layer of the VLM backbone, which estimate token importance by fusing intrinsic visual saliency with extrinsic query relevance captured via a shadow token. By uncover-ing a consistent staircase redundancy pattern across VLM layers, we further introduce Budget-Deterministic Pruning (BDP) to establish a hardware-friendly schedule with fixed per-layer budgets. Exten-sive experimental results across various benchmarks and VLMs demonstrate that LearnPrunerV2 consistently outperform previous state-of-the-art methods. Notably, LearnPrunerV2 preserves 99.8% of the original performance when pruning 77.8% of visual tokens on LLaVA-v1.5, achieving a superior accuracy-efficiency trade-off.

## 1 Introduction 

Following the rapid evolution of Large Language Models (LLMs) [1, 24, 42], Vision-Language Models (VLMs) have extended these so-phisticated reasoning capabilities to the multimodal domain, lead-ing to significant advancements in visual understanding and ground-ing. Generally, VLMs employ a pretrained visual encoder to trans-form visual inputs into a sequence of tokens, which are subse-quently concatenated with textual instruction tokens as input for the LLM. Visual tokens often dominate the input sequence, typ-ically numbering in the hundreds and potentially expanding to thousands for high-resolution images or long videos. Since the com-putational complexity of self-attention scales quadratically with sequence length, such a massive influx of visual tokens drastically inflates inference latency, rendering VLMs impractical for real-time or resource-constrained deployment. To alleviate these computational bottleneck, numerous studies have explored token pruning as a viable strategy for reducing the visual sequence length. Given the inherent redundancy in visual modality, prior research has demonstrated that a substantial pro-portion of visual tokens can be discarded with minimal impact on model performance. Fundamentally, the effectiveness of token pruning hinges on two core dimensions: how to prune (the selec-tion criterion) and where to prune (the pruning schedule). In this paper, we argue that existing approaches remain suboptimal in both aspects. 

Unreliability of Pruning Criteria. Existing pruning criteria primarily fall into two categories: importance-based and diversity-based. The former utilizes attention scores from either the visual encoder or the LLM to identify significant tokens. This creates a dilemma: early pruning is computationally efficient but "blind", while late-stage pruning is semantically guided but yields marginal speedup. These methods are inherently query-agnostic, making it challenging to adapt pruning results to different user instructions. This suggests that the internal attention of LLMs remains essential for capturing task-specific visual information. 

Suboptimality of Pruning Strategies. Beyond selection cri-teria, existing methods typically employ static, one-pass pruning schedules or layer specific heuristics, overlooking the intrinsic cou-pling between token redundancy and VLM layer depth. Indeed, re-cent studies have revealed the layer-wise dynamics: AutoPrune [36] demonstrates that VLM attention exhibits significant fluctuations across layers, particularly in reasoning-intensive scenarios. Further-more, TwigVLM [31] reveals that in the deep layers of VLMs, even the complete removal of visual tokens does not necessarily degrade performance. Collectively, these findings suggest that as visual information is incrementally integrated into linguistic representa-tions, the significance of visual tokens is not uniform across layers but evolves throughout the hierarchy, motivating a progressive, layer-adaptive pruning strategy. To address these limitations, LearnPruner [33] takes an initial step by adopting a two-stage token pruning framework that first removes redundant vision tokens via a learnable pruning module after the vision encoder, then retains only query-relevant tokens in the LLM’s middle layers based on text-to-vision attention scores. While effective, its singular input signals and hand-crafted schedule cannot fully capture the continuous evolution of token redundancy. Building upon this, we propose LearnPrunerV2 , a purely learn-able pruning framework for VLMs. Unlike the hybrid design of its predecessor, our framework adopts a unified architecture by inte-grating a lightweight pruning module into every layer of the VLM backbone. Moreover, a learnable "shadow token" is appended to the input sequence during the prefill stage to aggregate the preceding context and capture cross-modal correlations. Each pruning module subsequently combines intrinsic visual saliency from local features with extrinsic query relevance derived from the shadow token to predict importance scores for individual visual token. Through end-to-end joint optimization, LearnPrunerV2 aligns pruning decisions with the layer-specific evolution of visual significance, which in turn allows pruning modules to determine the optimal exit depth for each token. This dynamic mechanism enables the model to flexibly adjust its pruning trajectory based on the complexity of both images and queries. Furthermore, to address practical budget-constrained scenarios, we introduce Budget-Deterministic-Pruning (BDP) strategy. BDP utilizes offline-calibrated statistics to maintain a strictly fixed token count across every layer and adopts diversity-aware selection to mitigate the impact of a non-adaptive schedule. This ensures deterministic inference latency and maximized batch-ing throughput, providing a robust solution for high-concurrency real-world deployment. Benefiting from its model-agnostic design, LearnPrunerV2 can be seamlessly integrated into diverse VLM architectures, such as LLaVA [23, 25, 26] and Qwen-VL [3, 37]. Extensive experiments on multiple VQA benchmarks shows that our method outperforms prior state-of-the-art methods under different retention ratios. For example, when pruning 94.4% visual tokens in LLaVA-NeXT-7B, LearnPrunerV2 preserve 96.2% of the original model performance, while achieving a 9.7 × reduction in TFLOPs. In summary, the con-tributions of our work are threefold: 

• We present LearnPrunerV2, a purely learnable token pruning framework that adopts a progressive pruning strategy. Com-pared to LearnPruner, it offers a more unified and flexible solution. ## 2 Related Work 2.1 Vision Language Models 

Building upon the foundational reasoning power of Large Lan-guage Models (LLMs) [1, 6, 18, 24, 34, 42], Vision-Language Models (VLMs) [3, 20, 26, 50] demonstrate remarkable capabilities showing strong performance on diverse multimodal tasks such as image captioning, visual question answering, and visual grounding. A standard VLM typically comprises three components: a visual en-coder to transform visual inputs into tokens, a vision-language connector to align the modality, and a LLM to serve as the cog-nitive engine for multimodal integration and complex reasoning. However, this paradigm incurs significant computational costs as the massive number of visual tokens. For instance, LLaVA-v1.5 [26] converts a 336x336 image into 576 tokens, advanced VLMs like LLaVA-NeXT [25] (tiling-based) and Qwen2.5-VL [3] (native dy-namic resolution) support high-resolution inputs that further ex-pand visual token sequences. Furthermore, in video understanding scenarios, the visual token count scales linearly with the number of sampled frames [9, 23]. Such extensive sequences pose a severe bottleneck for real-time applications, driving the urgent need for efficient inference of VLMs. 

## 2.2 Vision Token Reduction for VLMs 

Given the inherent redundancy in visual signals, token reduction has emerged as a promising direction to enhance VLM efficiency. Various works [7, 10, 14, 21, 39] attempt to compress vision token into more compact representations. While effective in reducing computation, these methods typically change the model architec-ture and require retraining from scratch, resulting in high training costs and potential degradation of the performance upper bound. In this work, we mainly focus on token pruning techniques that can be directly applied to existing VLMs. From the perspective of pruning criteria, existing methods can be broadly categorized into two types: importance-based and diversity-based. Importance-based methods [8, 31, 43, 46, 47, 49] utilize at-tention scores to select top-k important tokens and discard the rest. To remove query-irrelevant visual tokens, FastV [8] leverages the attention of the last text token at the second LLM layer as the principled signal, while SparseVLM [49] first identifies text to-kens strongly correlated with visual input, and then employs their attention scores to achieve more precise guidance for pruning. Al-ternatively, to eliminate inherent visual redundancy, VisionZip [43] and VisPruner [47] leverage [CLS] attention from the visual en-coder to identify key salient tokens and perform pruning before the LLM. One limitation of importance-based methods is their reliance on attention maps, which makes them incompatible with Flash Attention [11]. Diversity-based methods aims to select a diverse set based on similarity between tokens [2, 17, 38, 48]. DivPrune [2] for-mulates token pruning as a Max-Min Diversity Problem, whereas DART [38] explicitly minimizes duplication by comparing candi-date tokens against a set of pivots. Furthermore, CDPruner [48] calculates inter-token similarity conditioned on their relevance to the current instruction and use the determinantal point process to maximize the conditional diversity. From the perspective of pruning schedules, most studies con-duct static pruning at a specific layer, overlooking the layer-wise evolution of token redundancy. Alternatively, some works adopt multi-stage pruning schedules [19, 33, 36, 40, 44]. For instance, PDrop [40] applies progressive pruning with uniform ratios and intervals; AutoPrune [36] derives sample-specific policies by quan-tifying cross-modal mutual information; LearnPruner [33] employs a two-stage scheme, applying stage-specific pruning criteria to dif-ferent phases. Despite these advancements, such heuristic designs remain misaligned with the dynamic nature of visual information processing, leading to suboptimal pruning schedules.

## 3.3 Budget-Deterministic Pruning 

Once the pruning modules are trained, one can directly apply threshold-based pruning at each layer during inference, yielding sample-adaptive pruning schedules. However, practical deployment typically requires a fixed compute budget, which prior methods ap-proximate by reporting the average number of retained tokens per layer. Furthermore, even with a fixed global budget, different inputs may produce distinct per-layer pruning trajectories, resulting in in-consistent memory access patterns and computation graphs, which is inherently hardware-unfriendly for optimized deployment. To address these issues, we seek a deterministic per-layer sched-ule that fixes the token count at every layer. A natural question arises: how should such a schedule be determined? To this end, we investigate the underlying pruning characteristics learned by our modules. We randomly sample 500 image-text pairs from the LLaVA-mix665k dataset [26] and execute threshold-based pruning with 𝜏 = 0.5 (identical to training). Figure 3 visualizes the mean and variance of the per-layer token retention ratio across all samples on LLaVA-v1.5 and Qwen2.5-VL. Surprisingly, the learned pruning trajectories exhibit high consistency across diverse inputs, with low inter-sample variance, and display a pronounced staircase pattern. This phenomenon suggests that through the joint optimization of all pruning modules, the model has autonomously identified a robust structural redundancy pattern in its architecture, reflecting the intrinsic stages of information flow and assimilation. Taking LLaVA-v1.5 (Figure 3(a)) as an example, the trajectory begins with a sharp drop at the first layer, immediately discarding uninformative visual content such as background regions. In the subsequent early layers, the retention ratio enters a stable plateau, where virtually no further pruning occurs, suggesting that the in-cremental information gained across these shallow layers provides little discriminative signal. As the model progresses into the mid-dle layers, a second significant decline emerges. This aligns with prior findings [33, 39] where the middle layers serve as the primary stage for intensive cross-modal interaction. At this stage, visual features are progressively aligned with linguistic queries, causing a substantial portion of visual tokens to become redundant. Moving into the deep layers, the retention ratio gradually approaches zero, confirming that essential visual information has been integrated into the hidden states of the text tokens, rendering the original visual modality entirely redundant for final response generation. ## 4.2 Main Results 

Results on LLaVA-v1.5. As shown in Table 1, we first evaluate our method on LLaVA-v1.5-7B, where images are represented by a fixed sequence of 576 tokens. The results demonstrate that our method consistently outperforms all previous methods across all three re-tention ratios. Notably, when retaining 128 tokens, our method maintains a remarkable 99.8% of the vanilla model’s performance, surpassing the previous state-of-the-art method by 1.3%, and this performance gap further widens to 2 .1% when the budget is re-stricted to only 32 tokens. Since both our model and LearnPruner employ learnable modules, this significant lead directly validates the superiority of our proposed pruning criterion and pruning schedule. 

Results on LLaVA-NeXT. To verify its scalability for high-resolution scenarios, we evaluate our method on LLaVA-NeXT-7B. LLaVA-NeXT handles high-resolution inputs by partitioning the image into multiple sub-grids and encoding them independently, yielding a massive baseline of 2,880 visual tokens. As shown in Table 2, LearnPrunerV2 retains 99.7% of the original performance while utilizing only 22 .2% of the visual tokens. Furthermore, on several benchmarks such as POPE, MME, and MMBench, our pruned model achieves performance that is either comparable to or even superior to its vanilla counterpart. These results indicate that high-resolution inputs inherently contain substantial visual redundancy, where many tokens may contribute marginal or even distracting signals. By removing uninformative visual context, the model can more accurately focus on the most salient features, thereby leading to more precise predictions. 

Results on Qwen2.5-VL. To further assess the broad applicability of LearnPrunerV2, we extend our evaluation to Qwen2.5-VL-7B, a leading open-source VLM with an architecture distinct from the LLaVA family. Since the original SFT data for Qwen2.5-VL is not publicly accessible, we leverage the LLaVA-mix665k dataset to gen-erate model responses, which serve as pseudo-labels for training our pruning modules. Furthermore, although Qwen2.5-VL natu-rally supports native dynamic resolution, we fix the input size to a standardized budget of 1,280 tokens to ensure a fair and consistent comparison across all pruning methods. As shown in Table 3, Learn-PrunerV2 still maintain its dominant performance, even exceeding 100% average relative accuracy at 66 .7% and 77 .8% pruning rates, showing the robust generalizability of our approach. ## 4.3 Ablation Study 

We evaluate the learnable pruning criterion and budget-deterministic pruning strategy on LLaVA-v1.5-7B. Unless otherwise specified, all experiments use a fixed budget of 64 tokens, and we report average relative performance over GQA, SQA, VQA T, POPE, and MME. 

Input components. As shown in Table 4(a), we compare three con-figurations for the pruning module’s input. When relying solely on the hidden states, the module determines token importance based only on intrinsic visual redundancy, which results in a performance of 96 .6%. By concatenating the relevance feature, the model can further incorporate cross-modal correlations with the textual query, boosting the average performance to 97 .2%. However, inter-layer attention fluctuations often introduce distracting noise. The gating mechanism mitigates this by filtering transient signals, stabilizing importance estimation and yielding a performance of 97 .8%. 

Relevance source. Table 4(b) investigates the influence of different cross-modal relevance sources. While the last instruction token is a conventional choice for sequence aggregation, utilizing its attention scores results in poor performance (96 .5%) because the attention scores are numerically overwhelmed by the hidden states. Although last token logits (97 .4%) alleviate the scaling bottleneck, they still underperform the shadow token (97 .8%), which we attribute to the inherent multi-task interference between next-token predic-tion and pruning guidance. In contrast, the shadow token serves as a dedicated aggregator that focuses exclusively on capturing instruction-relevant token importance. 

Parameter sharing scheme. The learnable pruning module mainly consists of a relevance projector (MLP 𝜙 ) and a scoring network (MLP 𝜓 ). Table 4(c) evaluates different parameter sharing schemes for these components. Our hybrid strategy achieves the best per-formance (97 .8%), outperforming both fully independent (97 .3%) and fully shared (96 .9%) counterparts. This is because attention logits exhibit significant inter-layer variance, necessitating a layer-specific MLP 𝜙 for adaptive mapping. Conversely, a shared MLP 𝜓 

receives more gradients from different layers, facilitating robust convergence and stabilizing the optimization to learn a generalized pruning criterion. 

Visual token selection. Table 5 evaluates the impact of our diversity-aware selection against the standard Top-K strategy under a fixed layer-wise budget. While both approaches perform similarly at a higher budget of 64 tokens (97 .8% vs. 97 .7%), our strategy demon-strates a clear advantage when the budget is restricted to 32 tokens, outperforming Top-K by 0 .9%. This gap suggests that simple Top-K selection is prone to information redundancy, as tokens with the highest scores are often spatially clustered or semantically similar. In contrast, our approach encourages the selection of more diverse visual cues, thereby broadening the overall information coverage within the restricted budget. 

## 4.4 Efficiency Analysis 

To demonstrate the efficiency of LearnPrunerV2, we measure the FLOPs, prefill time, total inference time and KV cache size of the pruned model compared to the vanilla LLaVA-NeXT-7B. All experi-ments are conducted on the POPE benchmark. As shown in Table 6, when the number of visual tokens is reduced from 2,880 to 160, we achieve a 9.7 × reduction in TFLOPs and a 6.2 × speedup in pre-fill time. Furthermore, the KV cache size is slashed by 8.7 ×, while the total inference time improves by 3.7 ×. These results highlight the effectiveness of our framework in reducing the computational overhead of VLMs 

## 5 Conclusion 

We introduce LearnPrunerV2, a learnable progressive token pruning framework for accelerating VLMs. It estimates token importance from intrinsic visual saliency and query relevance, and employs a Budget-Deterministic Pruning (BDP) schedule that assigns fixed per-layer budgets by exploiting the inherent architectural redun-dancy of VLMs. Extensive experiments on multiple VLM archi-tectures and benchmarks demonstrate strong accuracy-efficiency trade-offs and clear gains over prior methods. ## A Appendix A.1 Results on Video-LLaVA 

To further assess the broad applicability of LearnPrunerV2, we ex-tend our evaluation from the image domain to video understanding. Video-LLaVA [23] processes each video by uniformly sampling 8 frames and encoding them independently, producing a total of 2,048 visual tokens. We evaluate on three established video quesion an-swering benchmarks: TGIF-QA [16], MSVD-QA [41] and MSRVTT-QA [41]. Following [47], we use the first 1,000 samples from each benchmark for evaluation, and employ GPT-4.1 to assist in scoring open-ended responses. As shown in Table 7, LearnPrunerV2 con-sistently outperforms FastV across all three benchmarks, especially under lower retention ratios. These results demonstrate the strong generalizability of LearnPrunerV2 from image-based to video-based VLMs. 

## A.2 Data Efficiency 

To investigate the data efficiency of LearnPrunerV2, we train the model with varying proportions of the LLaVA-mix665k [26] training dataset (from 10% to 100%) and evaluate the resulting performance on both LLaVA-v1.5-7B and LLaVA-NeXT-7B under two retention budgets, respectively. As shown in Figure 4, LearnPrunerV2 exhibits remarkable data efficiency across both models. On LLaVA-v1.5, using only 10% of the training data already achieves 98.5% relative performance under the 128-token budget. As the training data scales to 50%, performance largely converges, achieving results on par with full-dataset training. Moreover, we observe that lower token budgets generally require more training data to reach convergence. This can be attributed to the fact that more aggressive pruning demands a more precise pruning criterion, which in turn requires sufficient training signal to learn. 

## A.3 Visualization 

To better illustrate the pruning behavior learned by LearnPrunerV2, we visualize the token pruning results across different layers on LLaVA-v1.5-7B. As shown in Figure 5 and Figure 6, LearnPrunerV2 first removes a large number of uninformative tokens at shallow layers, such as those corresponding to padding regions or back-ground areas. Moving to the middle layers, the retained tokens progressively converge toward query-relevant visual regions. By the deeper layers, nearly all visual tokens have been pruned. This progressive pruning pattern reveals the inherent characteristics of visual information flow within VLMs, which is consistent with findings in prior works [39]. Additionally, we compare the importance score heatmaps pre-dicted by LearnPrunerV2 with the last-token attention scores adopted by previous methods. As shown in Figure 7 and Figure 8, last-token attention suffers from notable positional bias and exhibits substan-tial fluctuations across layers, and typically requires deeper layers (e.g., layer 16) to localize query-relevant regions. In contrast, ben-efiting from our learnable shadow token and gating mechanism, LearnPrunerV2 is able to identify query-relevant regions at much earlier layers (e.g., layer 12), while also exhibiting stronger cross-layer consistency.
