## Abstract 

Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) has emerged as the dominant paradigm for parameter-efficient fine-tuning of large language models. Standard all-layer LoRA applies adaptation throughout the transformer, despite heterogeneous contributions from its components. In this work, we conduct a systematic mechanistic inter-pretability analysis to understand how LoRA affects model internals. We observe that LoRA’s adaptation effect is highly non-uniform, with significant component-wise and layer-wise sparsity. Most critically, we find that important layers are highly task-dependent, with limited overlap across tasks. Motivated by these in-sights, we propose Adaptive-LoRA , a calibration-driven method that automatically identifies task-specific layers via frozen-base zero-ablation, enabling efficient layer selection without exhaustive search. Extensive experiments on Qwen2.5-1.5B across 11 diverse reasoning tasks demonstrate that Adaptive-LoRA outperforms full-layer LoRA while using only 30% of layers, achieving a 3.5 × reduction in adapter parameters. This work bridges mechanistic interpretability and practi-cal efficiency, providing both fundamental insights and a principled approach for adaptive fine-tuning. 

## 1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) have achieved remarkable capabilities across diverse tasks, driving widespread adoption in both research and deployment [9, 28]. Adapting these models to specific downstream domains, however, remains computationally expensive, motivating parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) methods. Among these, Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) [13] has become the de facto standard, enabling adaptation through low-rank weight updates while keeping original parameters frozen. Standard all-layer LoRA uses the same adaptation rank across transformer layers, despite their potentially unequal contributions. Layer-wise structured pruning studies [22] identify substantial variation in layer importance. Meanwhile, mechanistic work on induction heads [27] links specific attention circuits to in-context learning. These observations raise a fundamental question: if layers are not equally important for inference, why should they be equally adapted during fine-tuning? 

LoRA variants improve adaptation through rank allocation [39], learning-rate optimization [11], and dynamic rank search [35]. We study a complementary question: can interventions on a frozen base model identify task-specific layers worth adapting before training begins? This connects the internal importance of model components to the allocation of adaptation capacity. We address this question through mechanistic interpretability , building on studies of internal rep-resentations [8] and behavior-specific circuits [36]. Specifically, we employ path patching [36] to

RQ1: How do different components and layers contribute to LoRA’s adaptation effect, and is this contribution uniform across the model? 

RQ2: Are the important layers consistent across different tasks, or are they highly task-dependent? 

Our mechanistic analysis on Qwen2.5-0.5B across four diverse datasets yields two key insights: ( i)LoRA’s adaptation is highly non-uniform, with MLP components dominating over attention (58.4% vs. 41.6%) and contributions concentrated in early layers; ( ii ) important layers exhibit significant task-dependence, with only 39.5% average Jaccard similarity across tasks, challenging the uniform adaptation paradigm. Building on these insights, we propose Adaptive-LoRA , a calibration-driven method that automati-cally identifies task-specific layers via frozen-base zero-ablation with a default calibration size of 

N =30 . Adaptive-LoRA operates in three phases (calibration, selection, and training) and can be adapted to different efficiency-performance trade-offs. Extensive experiments on Qwen2.5-1.5B [31] across 11 reasoning tasks demonstrate that Adaptive-LoRA outperforms full-layer LoRA baselines while using only 30% of layers, achieving a 3.5 × reduction in adapter parameters. Our contributions are: • We provide the first systematic mechanistic analysis of LoRA’s internal mechanisms, revealing significant heterogeneity in component-wise and layer-wise contributions. • We demonstrate that important layers are highly task-dependent (39.5% overlap), challenging the uniform adaptation paradigm. • We propose Adaptive-LoRA, a principled calibration-driven approach for task-specific layer selec-tion via frozen-base zero-ablation, informed by path-patching analysis. • We empirically validate that Adaptive-LoRA outperforms full-layer baselines with 70% parameter reduction across 11 diverse reasoning tasks. 

## 2 Related Work 

Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) aims to adapt large pre-trained models with minimal computational overhead. LoRA [13] approximates weight updates with low-rank matrices, significantly reducing trainable parameters. Subsequent work has focused on rank allocation [39], learning rate optimization [11], dynamic rank search [35], and weight decomposition [21]. Other PEFT paradigms include prefix tuning [17], adapters [12], and prompt tun-ing [16]. Continual-learning methods further structure low-rank updates through subspace constraints (InfLoRA [18]), hierarchical layer-wise adapters (TreeLoRA [30]), and gating (GainLoRA [19]). Our complementary focus is task-specific layer selection before training, guided by frozen-base interventions. 

Mechanistic Interpretability. Mechanistic interpretability seeks to reverse-engineer model behaviors into human-understandable algorithms [26]. Activation patching (causal tracing) [23] and circuit analysis [36] enable researchers to isolate the contribution of specific components to model outputs. Automated circuit discovery [6] further identifies compact computational subgraphs associated with model behaviors. Our work applies these tools to dissect LoRA’s adaptation mechanism, moving beyond black-box performance metrics to understand how and where adaptation occurs within the network. 

Layer Selection and Pruning. Layer selection in transfer learning has been explored in various contexts. Analysis of BERT attention [15] reveals repeated attention patterns across heads and task-dependent differences in their effects on performance. In the context of PEFT, heuristic strategies such as fine-tuning only the last few layers or specific middle layers have been proposed [10]. However, these static strategies fail to account for task diversity. Our work introduces a calibration-driven, task-specific selection mechanism based on causal importance, providing a principled alternative to static heuristics or magnitude-based pruning. 23 Mechanistic Analysis of LoRA 

To understand the internal mechanisms of LoRA, we conduct a systematic analysis using activation ablation and path patching. We quantify the contribution of different model components across four diverse datasets: Alpaca-ZH (Chinese instruction), Alpaca-EN [34] (English instruction), Code-Alpaca [2] (code generation), and Dolly [7] (dialogue).

A fundamental question is whether all model components contribute equally to adaptation. Prior work hypothesizes that MLPs store factual knowledge [23], while attention handles information routing. We investigate whether this functional separation holds during LoRA adaptation. Specif-ically, for each layer we intervene on each layer’s MLP or attention block output independently and measure the re-sulting prediction shift using Equation (1). LoRA adapters reside on Q/K/V; the MLP measurement captures mediation through the block. As shown in Figure 1, MLP layers consistently contribute more than attention across all four datasets (58.4% vs. 41.6%), with remarkably stable ratios (57.3%– 59.5%). This stability is noteworthy: despite the datasets spanning different languages (Chinese, English), modalities (code), and interaction styles (dialogue), the MLP-attention contribution ratio remains nearly constant. Q/K/V updates change the attention output, which enters the residual stream and is transformed by downstream MLP blocks. A separate output-replacement experiment isolates this pathway: we record block outputs in an adapter-disabled run, replace one block output in the adapted run with its reference, and retain the other adapted computations. MLP mediation is reproduced across two adapter configurations (Appendix B.4). 

Finding 1: MLP Dominance. MLP blocks mediate 58.4% of the measured component effect vs. 41.6% for attention, with task-specific shares of 57.3%–59.5%.

## 4 Adaptive-LoRA 

Motivated by Finding 4, that important layers vary significantly across tasks, we propose Adaptive-LoRA , a calibration-driven method for task-specific layer selection. Unlike static heuristics or exhaustive search, our approach automatically identifies critical layers via frozen-base zero-ablation on a small calibration set with N =30 inputs by default. The approximately 30%-layer experiments achieve competitive performance with about 3 × fewer adapter parameters.

Let M denote a pre-trained transformer with L layers, and Dtrain a task-specific training set. Stan-dard LoRA applies adapters to all layers {0, . . . , L −1}, yielding a trainable parameter count pro-portional to L at fixed rank and per-layer target dimensions. Our goal is to identify a subset 

Lsel ⊂ { 0, . . . , L −1} of size k < L such that adapting only these layers achieves comparable performance while reducing parameters by ≈ L/k .The key challenge is efficiently estimating layer importance without exhaustive search over  Lk

## 5 Experiments 

We evaluate Adaptive-LoRA against strong baselines across diverse reasoning tasks, addressing three research questions: (RQ1) Does Adaptive-LoRA achieve superior performance with significantly fewer parameters? (RQ2) What is the optimal layer budget? (RQ3) Does calibration-based selection outperform heuristic baselines? 

5.1 Experimental Setup Model and Training. We use Qwen2.5-1.5B [31] (1.54B parameters, 28 layers) as the primary evaluation model. Training data: commonsense_170k (10,000 samples) and math_10k (5,000 samples) [14], 3 epochs, max 512 tokens. 

Evaluation Tasks. We evaluate on 11 reasoning tasks: 8 commonsense (BoolQ [3], PIQA [1], Social IQa [33], HellaSwag [38], WinoGrande [32], ARC-Easy [4], ARC-Challenge [4], Open-BookQA [24]) and 3 mathematical (GSM8K [5], SVAMP [29], AQUA [20]). Results are reported as task-level accuracies. 

Baselines. We compare against three LoRA variants: LoRA [13] (standard, r ∈ { 4, 8, 16 , 32 }), LoRA+ [11] (differential learning rates), and DoRA [21] (weight-decomposed). These three baselines apply to Q, K, V in all 28 layers. Adaptive-LoRA uses frozen-base zero-ablation on 250 calibration samples to select the top 8 layers (30%). 

5.2 Main Results

## 6 Discussion 

Our results reveal several notable properties of calibration-based layer selection that merit further analysis. First, the parameter-efficient performance across six models spanning 0.5B to 32B parame-ters (Table 2) suggests that layer importance heterogeneity is a fundamental property of transformer architectures rather than an artifact of any particular model family. The fact that Adaptive-LoRA achieves competitive or superior performance with 30% of layers across both Qwen and LLaMA families indicates that the calibration signal captures genuinely causal structure in the adaptation process. Second, the gains of 1.3–2.2 percentage points across all four ranks in Table 1 support the value of selective adaptation across capacity levels. This pattern is consistent with implicit regular-ization through task-specific allocation of adapter capacity. Third, calibration uses N = 30 inputs and gradient-free interventions, taking 20–62 s in the default A100 study. Smaller adapters directly reduce storage. Training acceleration additionally depends on depth and prefix reuse: accuracy mode prioritizes the unrestricted ranking, while efficiency mode offers a measured speed–accuracy operating point (Appendix E.6). 97 Conclusion 

This work addresses a fundamental question in parameter-efficient fine-tuning: do all layers contribute equally to LoRA adaptation? Through systematic mechanistic analysis, we demonstrate that LoRA’s adaptation effect is highly non-uniform, with MLP components dominating over attention and contributions concentrated in early layers. Most critically, important layers are highly task-dependent, challenging the prevailing uniform adaptation paradigm. Motivated by these insights, we propose Adaptive-LoRA, a calibration-driven method that automatically identifies task-specific layers via frozen-base zero-ablation. Extensive experiments across 11 diverse reasoning tasks demonstrate that selective adaptation on 30% of carefully chosen layers outperforms full-layer LoRA baselines while achieving a 3.5 × reduction in adapter parameters. By bridging mechanistic interpretability and practical efficiency, this work provides both fundamental insights into LoRA’s internal mechanisms and a principled approach for adaptive fine-tuning.

## A Limitations and Future Directions 

Selection Granularity. Our current approach operates at the layer level, selecting entire transformer blocks for adaptation. Layer-level selection achieves 3.5 × efficiency over full-layer LoRA. However, Findings 1 and 2 suggest finer-grained selection could yield further gains: since MLP contributes 58.4% and V-projection dominates within attention, a component-level or even attention head-level selection strategy could explore further parameter savings by choosing projections within the selected layers. Implementing such fine-grained selection would require extending the calibration procedure to score individual sub-components, which increases the calibration cost from O(L) to O(L × C)

where C is the number of components per layer. We leave this exploration to future work. 

Task Coverage. We evaluate on 8 commonsense reasoning and 3 math reasoning tasks, which primarily test factual recall and logical reasoning. Generative tasks (dialogue, creative writing, summarization) may exhibit different layer importance patterns, as they rely more heavily on language modeling capacity in later layers. Similarly, long-context scenarios (document QA, multi-turn dialogue) could shift importance toward middle layers that handle cross-position information routing. Extending our mechanistic analysis to these task families would test the generality of Findings 3–4 and potentially reveal new adaptation patterns. 

Theoretical Foundations. While our empirical findings are consistent and reproducible across architectures, we currently lack a formal theoretical framework explaining why early layers dominate adaptation importance (Finding 3) or why task-specific patterns emerge at specific depths (Finding 4). Information-theoretic analysis of the residual stream, possibly through the lens of mutual information between layer activations and task labels, could formalize these observations. Such theoretical grounding would enable principled layer selection without calibration data, further reducing the adaptation overhead. 

Dynamic Layer Selection. Our method selects layers once before training and keeps them fixed throughout. An adaptive approach that adjusts layer selection during training (e.g., re-calibrating every k epochs) could potentially capture evolving importance patterns as the model learns. B.4 MLP Mediation under Adapter-Disabled Output Replacement

B.6 Training Dynamics 

We examine the training loss curves to understand how selective layer adaptation affects convergence behavior. Training on the commonsense split (10,000 samples, 3 epochs) with Qwen2.5-1.5B at r16, we record per-step training loss for all four methods. Adaptive-LoRA converges faster than full-layer baselines during the first epoch, reaching a training loss of 0.82 after 500 steps compared to 0.91 for LoRA and 0.89 for LoRA+. This accelerated convergence is consistent with the implicit regularization hypothesis discussed in Section 6: by restricting adaptation to causally important layers, Adaptive-LoRA avoids the initial exploration phase where full-layer methods allocate capacity to non-productive parameters. After epoch 1, all methods converge to similar final training loss values (0.42–0.45), but the evaluation accuracy divergence persists and widens, suggesting that Adaptive-LoRA achieves better generalization rather than merely better training fit. Notably, the gradient norms for selected layers in Adaptive-LoRA are 1.4–1.8 × larger than the corresponding layers in full LoRA, consistent with the concentrated adaptation hypothesis. ## F Cross-Architecture Comparison 

F.1 Layer Importance Patterns Across Families 

We compare layer importance patterns across three architecture families: Qwen2.5 (0.5B, 1.5B, 3B), LLaMA-3.2 (3B), and LLaMA-2 (7B). Despite differences in depth, width, and training data, several consistent patterns emerge: 1. Early layer dominance. In all architectures, the first 10–15% of layers consistently rank in the top-5 by importance score, supporting Finding 3. This includes the embedding projection and early attention layers that establish token representations. 2. Middle layer task specialization. Layers at 30–60% depth show the highest variance in im-portance across tasks, consistent with Finding 4. These layers encode task-specific reasoning patterns that vary between commonsense and mathematical domains. 3. Depth-scaled selection. The main comparison in Table 2 uses 7/24, 8/28, 11/36, and 8/28 layers for the 0.5B, 1.5B, Qwen2.5-3B, and LLaMA 3B models, respectively. These are approximately 30% layer budgets. On Qwen2.5-1.5B, Table 1 compares four ranks and yields commonsense gains of 1.3, 2.0, 2.2, and 1.8 percentage points over full-layer LoRA. F.3 MLP vs. Attention Contribution by Depth 

An important question for cross-architecture generalization is whether the MLP-dominance pattern (Finding 1) holds uniformly across layer depth or varies systematically. We decompose per-layer importance into MLP and attention components for Qwen2.5-1.5B and LLaMA-3-3B. In both architectures, early layers (first 20%) show the highest MLP/Attention ratio ( ∼65/35), consistent with the hypothesis that early layers primarily modify feed-forward features. Middle layers (20–60% depth) exhibit a more balanced ratio ( ∼55/45), reflecting the co-importance of both information routing (attention) and feature transformation (MLP) for task-specific processing. Late layers (last 40%) return to MLP dominance ( ∼60/40), though with lower absolute importance scores. This depth-dependent component balance has practical implications for future work on sub-layer selection: a hybrid strategy that selects MLP adapters broadly but attention adapters only in middle layers could achieve even higher efficiency than full-layer selection. This motivates testing finer-grained allocation across components in future work.

## G Broader Impact 

G.1 Positive Societal Implications 

This work contributes to improving the efficiency of large language model adaptation, which has several positive societal implications: 1. Democratized access. By reducing the parameter count for fine-tuning by 3.5 ×, Adaptive-LoRA lowers the computational barrier for researchers and practitioners with limited GPU resources. Organizations that previously could not afford full-layer LoRA adaptation on larger models may now achieve comparable performance with significantly reduced hardware requirements. 2. Environmental sustainability. Smaller adapters reduce parameter storage. Training-time benefits depend additionally on selected-layer depth and prefix reuse, as quantified in Appendix E.6. Energy consumption is a separate system-level measurement. 23 3. Multi-adapter serving. Smaller adapter footprints enable serving more concurrent task-specific adapters on the same hardware, improving resource utilization in multi-tenant cloud environments and enabling personalized model deployment on edge devices. 4. Interpretability and transparency. The mechanistic analysis underlying Adaptive-LoRA pro-vides interpretable insights into how LoRA adaptation distributes across model components. This transparency can help practitioners understand what their fine-tuned models have learned and where the adaptation has occurred, contributing to more responsible AI deployment. 

G.2 Potential Risks and Mitigations 

We do not foresee direct negative societal impacts from this work, as it improves efficiency of existing fine-tuning methods without introducing new model capabilities or training paradigms. However, we acknowledge two indirect considerations: • Lowered cost of misuse. By making fine-tuning more efficient, our method could marginally lower the cost of adapting models for harmful purposes. Task-specific deployment therefore calls for evaluating safety behavior after adaptation, alongside task accuracy. • Layer selection bias. Our calibration-based selection relies on the distribution of calibration data. If calibration data is biased or unrepresentative, the selected layers may not generalize well to underrepresented populations. We mitigate this by using diverse, publicly available training datasets and validating across multiple task families. 24 NeurIPS Paper Checklist
