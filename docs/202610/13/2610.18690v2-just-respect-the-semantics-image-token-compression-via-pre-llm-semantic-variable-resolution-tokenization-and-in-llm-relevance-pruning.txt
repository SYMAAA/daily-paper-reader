Abstract
Image-token compression must reduce computation with-
out hurting the semantics. We introduce Varest, maximiz-
ing semantic fungibility: tokens within a coherent seman-
tic region can be summarized coarsely, while object bound-
aries and literal text require finer distinctions. Object and
text localizers guide variable-resolution merging, preserv-
ing spatial coverage and finer detail at boundaries and text.
Learned [REL] registers then prune visual tokens after
early decoder processing. Training across budgets enables
one checkpoint per backbone. Across three backbones and
13 benchmarks, Varest outperforms 15 baselines in mean
performance retention at every evaluated budget, retaining
86.10–92.33% of full-token performance with only 5.56%
of the original layer-averaged image-token count.
1. Introduction
Image-token compression reduces the substantial language-
model decoder compute imposed by the hundreds or thou-
sands of image tokens in vision-language models (VLMs).
An image token may describe a small part of an object, a
boundary between objects, or a few characters in a sign.
Compressing these tokens requires different decisions.
Prior work has explored merging and pruning using var-
ious signals, including feature geometry [1, 5, 8, 13, 24],
attention scores [7, 33, 36, 39–41, 44], learned retention
utility [34], and object membership [18]. These are nat-
ural ways to approach compression, yet they do not re-
spect the semantics. Pruning poses a complementary prob-
lem: removing a token hurts semantic coverage, so selection
without sufficient reasoning ability through enough decoder

Our key contributions are as follows:
• We introduce Varest, a semantics-preserving variable-
resolution tokenizer guided by object boundaries and
textness. It allocates finer detail to low-fungibility regions
while maintaining full spatial coverage by controlling se-
mantic granularity according to the token budget.
• We combine this tokenizer with learned decoder-stage se-
lection, preserving broader visual access during early pro-
cessing and retaining fewer tokens in subsequent layers.
Optional adaptive halting can save further compute by
dropping all image tokens after the estimated information
horizon.
• Across three VLM backbones (LLaVA-1.5-7B [21],
LLaVA-1.5-13B [21], and LLaVA-NeXT-8B [19]) and
13 benchmarks, Varest achieves the highest mean perfor-
mance retention among 15 baselines at every evaluated
budget with a similar or lower computational cost.
2. Method
Varest allocates visual resolution across the image and vi-
sual access across LLM decoder depth, as shown in Figure
2. In Section 2.1, given an image and user prompt, we first
partition the image-token grid into regions guided by the ob-
ject boundaries and textness. Merging within these regions
produces Km image tokens that have different resolutions
but preserve full spatial coverage. Early decoder layers pro-
cess this representation together with the prompt and four
[REL] tokens/registers discussed in Section 2.2. At the de-
coder midpoint, the registers select Kp image tokens for the
rest of the LLM. The budget splitting over depth follows

the early decoder layers by pruning at the midpoint, keep-
ing the layer-averaged token budget the same. A region may
have already contributed information to contextual states
even if its token is later removed.

Joint adaptation.
We optimize the [REL] registers,
scoring head, multimodal projector, position tags, and
LoRA [12] adapters in the vision and language stacks and
vocabulary head using teacher-forced answer cross-entropy
on the VLM’s own tuning dataset, so the VLM sees no ad-

the merging budget during training and sample the pruning

3. Related Work
Compression signals.
We categorize visual-token com-
pression signals into semantic [18], saliency [1, 5, 8, 24, 40,
41], and query cues [7, 13, 33, 34, 36, 39, 44]. Saliency cues
estimate importance or redundancy from the image alone.
ToMe [5] merges similar features, while VisionZip [40] re-
tains attention-ranked tokens and aggregates the remain-
ing context.
These image-only criteria permit compres-
sion before the prompt is processed, but do not explicitly
account for object boundaries or the finer detail needed
to read text. Query cues use language context to priori-
tize content needed for a particular question, as in Spar-
seVLM [44]. This directs the budget toward task-relevant
evidence.
When derived from decoder attention, how-
ever, these cues depend on decoder processing, and atten-
tion reliability can vary across layers [34]. Semantic cues
add explicit content structure to constrain grouping, help-
ing specify which features can be summarized together.
Varest combines semantic-guided token allocation within
an image with depth-wise budget allocation, using variable-
resolution tokenization and learned decoder-stage selection.
Allocating the token budget according to semantics.
Within an image, semantic priors guide how a token bud-
get is distributed nonuniformly across regions. CORE [18]
guides pooling with object masks and orders the resulting
tokens by their centroids, preserving object membership
and spatial layout. A limitation of CORE’s object-based

Allocating the token budget across decoder depth.
Across decoder depth, the complementary question is how
long visual tokens remain available.
Multi-stage meth-
ods [33, 34, 39] use larger budgets early and fewer to-
kens later, allowing contextual processing before pruning.
DUET-VLM [33] and LearnPruner [34] combine this with
pre-LLM reduction. Varest follows this depth-wise alloca-
tion but differs in selection. LearnPruner learns early patch
selection but uses text-to-image attention to prune visual to-
kens in a middle decoder layer. However, attention does
not necessarily reflect a token’s influence on the predic-
tion. In NLP models, Jain and Wallace [16] find that atten-
tion weights can correlate poorly with gradient-based im-
portance and that different attention distributions can yield
similar predictions. Rather than using attention weights as
an importance proxy, Varest learns a relevance score for
each semantic region through [REL] registers and a scor-
ing head. This adapts the retention criterion to contextual-
ized, variable-resolution semantic region representations.

4.2. Quantitative Analysis
We report mean performance retention by normalizing each
metric against the corresponding uncompressed backbone
and averaging over 15 metrics, with the three POPE vari-
ants counted separately. Comparisons use layer-averaged
image-token budgets; 100% retention means matching the
uncompressed model. Figure 5 and Table 1 show that Varest
achieves the highest mean retention at every evaluated bud-
get across all three backbones. At the smallest token ratio
of 5.56%, it retains 86.10–92.33% of uncompressed perfor-
mance. More importantly, at an 11.11% token ratio, Varest
outperforms every baseline evaluated at 22.22% on all three
backbones.
For example, on LLaVA-1.5-7B, it achieves
97.08% retention at K = 64, exceeding the strongest base-
line’s 94.50% at K = 128. At K = 128, both LLaVA-1.5
models reach approximately 100% retention, matching un-
compressed performance with 22.22% of the tokens.
Figure 1 and Appendix C show that the required bud-
get varies across tasks. General VQA performance remains
relatively stable as the budget decreases, whereas docu-
ment reading, chart understanding, and referring-expression
grounding benefit more from larger budgets.
4.3. Adaptive Halting
We enable adaptive halting using the same checkpoints and
budgets, without additional training or backbone-specific
tuning. Table 2 reports the mean score changes, halting
rates, and image-token savings across 13 benchmarks and
four budgets.
We observe an additional 8.6–12.9% re-
duction in visual-token propagation, measured by image-
token counts summed across decoder layers. Mean score
losses are 0.10–0.21 points on the benchmarks’ native 0–
100 scales, averaged equally over 15 metrics and four bud-
gets. Appendix D expands the aggregated results.
Halting predominantly follows [REL] pruning on
LLaVA-1.5-7B and LLaVA-NeXT-8B, withdrawing the re-
tained tokens from later layers. On LLaVA-1.5-13B, 28.8%
of inputs halt before selection runs, including halts at the
selection boundary. This earlier withdrawal contributes to
its larger savings.
4.4. Efficiency Analysis
Figure 7 compares performance retention and measured
computation, each averaged over four budgets. Across the
three backbones, Varest improves retention over HiDrop,
the strongest baseline, by 3.79–6.39 percentage points while
using 1.0–4.7% fewer FLOPs. Its accuracy advantage there-
fore persists when accounting for measured computation.

5. Discussion
Varest allocates resolution to maximize semantic fungibil-
ity, summarizing compatible regions while reserving finer
representations for text and object boundaries. By main-
taining full spatial coverage before decoder-stage pruning,
it allows early decoder layers to integrate scene context be-
fore reducing subsequent visual access.
The method re-
mains compatible with FlashAttention [9], since token se-
lection does not require decoder attention weights, although
translating computational savings into lower latency also re-
quires an efficient implementation. Adapting Varest to ad-
ditional VLM families, such as Qwen-VL and InternVL,
remains promising future work. More broadly, the same
semantic fungibility principle could extend beyond natural
images. For example, in medical images, healthy organs
are like objects and lesions are like text. Such extensions
require domain-specific validation, but have great potential.

Merge updates.
For each connected region pair (R, S),

ARS/nRS, implementing the mean-boundary rule in Equa-

A.1.6. Adaptive Halting Implementation
Algorithm 2 implements the main-text criterion during pre-
fill, independently for each example. Lens applies the final
normalization and frozen base vocabulary head to the last
prompt state, including the assistant-turn prefix, bypassing
the vocabulary-head LoRA update. Stable3 tests whether
the three most recent top-1 predictions agree; the entropy
condition applies only to the current readout.
Here, ℓcounts completed layers, so halting removes to-
kens before layer ℓ+ 1 and takes precedence over midpoint
pruning.
Deletion preserves surviving tokens’ order and
original position tags. Later layers cache only the shortened
prompt; earlier layers retain their visual cache entries. An-
swer generation continues through all decoder layers using
the adapted vocabulary head, with position tags continuing
from the original prompt length. Halting is not reevaluated
during generation. If no trigger occurs, standard [REL]
pruning proceeds.

E. Further Efficiency Analysis
We describe our efficiency analysis procedure and bench-
mark in Section E.1. We then describe the computational
complexity, before describing quantitative space and com-
plexity measurements per baseline E.2.
E.1. VarestBench and Measurement Protocol
We introduce VarestBench, a benchmark for efficiency
analysis intended to be time–efficient while staying ro-
bust. It contains 180 short-answer VQA cases from AI2D,
ChartQA, DocVQA, GQA, and TextVQA, organized in two
sweeps which vary a workload variable. The first varies the
prompt length from 57 to 74 tokens over 16 levels, with
answers of 1–11 tokens. The second varies the reference-
answer length from 1 to 21 tokens over 20 levels, with
prompts of 59–63 tokens. Each level has five cases, and
token counts use LLaVA-1.5’s tokenizer and prompt tem-
plate. The number of image tokens is fixed on LLaVA-1.5
but varies with resolution on LLaVA-NeXT. The reference-
answer length sets an intended workload, but the model may
generate more or fewer tokens, and all measurements use
the tokens it generates.

F.1. Spatial Information in the Localizer Fields
The main ablations establish the contribution of localiza-
tion. Here, we test whether its spatial correspondence to the
input image matters. We replace localization fields without
retraining, evaluating both the separately trained tokenizer-
only model and joint Varest on all three backbones and four
budgets.
Each intervention preserves its parent’s token-
count schedule; adaptive halting is disabled.
Controlled field replacements.
We use the following in-
terventions:
• CLIP-B: reorder the original image’s object boundary
values according to adjacent-cell CLIP cosine distances.
The smallest boundary value is assigned to the small-
est distance, with stable tie ordering. This preserves the
learned boundary-value histogram and padding bound-
aries while changing their spatial arrangement.
• T = 0: set all textness values to zero, removing both
text-specific allocation and text-border reinforcement.
• Shuffled T: replace textness with another image’s field.
• Shuffled B: replace interior boundaries with another im-
age’s field.
• Both: combine CLIP-B and T = 0.
CLIP-B, T = 0, and Both correspond to −Object Local-
izer, −Text Localizer, and No Localizer in Table 4. CLIP-
B retains the original boundary statistics and therefore does
not remove all information supplied by the object localizer.
Shuffle construction.
Donor images are assigned within
each benchmark without self-assignment, using seeds 1
and 2 for textness and boundaries, respectively. We trans-
fer fields by cell-center nearest-neighbour resampling, han-
dling horizontal and vertical edges separately and preserv-
ing recipient padding boundaries. On NeXT, both the base
and high-resolution views are modified. Unlike CLIP-B,
these controls need not preserve the recipient’s value his-
togram; they test the effect of guidance from an unrelated
image.
Image-specific guidance.
Table 17 uses matched par-
ents evaluated with the same protocol as their interven-
tions; these references can differ slightly from the main-
comparison scores. Shuffled textness causes larger aggre-
gate losses than zero textness for both model variants on
every backbone. On NeXT, for example, shuffled textness
reduces the joint model’s mean score by 0.87 points, com-
pared with 0.02 points for zero textness. Thus, assigning
text-specific resolution to incorrect locations can be more
harmful than withholding that guidance.
Both CLIP-B
and shuffled boundaries also reduce performance across all
backbones. The CLIP-B result shows that preserving the
boundary-value distribution alone is insufficient.
Budget dependence.
Boundary guidance becomes par-
ticularly important under aggressive compression. On Re-
fCOCO, CLIP-B reduces the tokenizer-only model’s score
by 16.56 points at K = 32 on LLaVA-1.5-13B, compared
with 2.54 points at K = 224. On NeXT, the correspond-
ing losses are 32.20 points at K = 160 and 0.16 points
at K = 1120. Incorrect spatial guidance can nevertheless
remain harmful at larger budgets: shuffled boundaries still
cost 5.32 points on NeXT RefCOCO at K = 1120.

F.3.1. Selection Policy at a Fixed Depth
We compare Varest with Random Selection and Oracle Se-
lection at the same pruning layer and token counts. Ran-
dom Selection samples Kp image tokens uniformly. Oracle
Selection ranks tokens by gold-answer attention, averaged
over attention heads, answer positions, and decoder layers
from the pruning boundary onward in an unpruned teacher-
forced pass. It is an answer-informed diagnostic, not a de-
ployable policy or a guaranteed upper bound.
Table 16 reports task-level changes from matched sup-
plementary evaluations. Random selection is particularly
damaging on reading tasks.
On LLaVA-NeXT-8B, its
budget-averaged losses reach 5.17 points on TextVQA,
16.59 on DocVQA, and 16.65 on ChartQA. In contrast,
POPE, MMStar, and MMBench change little on average.
The contribution of learned selection therefore varies sub-
stantially across tasks.
Oracle gains are generally smaller, but the aggregate
comparison conceals task-specific opportunities. On NeXT,

F.4.1. Sensitivity to Prompt Changes
We evaluate Varest on LLaVA-1.5-7B at K = 64, with
(Km, Kp) = (102, 26) and ℓ= 16. For two expressions
naming different objects A and B in the same image, we
measure the fraction of cells inside A’s box whose regions
survive pruning. The swap gain is this fraction when the
prompt names A minus the fraction when it names B. We
exclude box pairs with IoU at least 0.5, average ordered
pairs within each image, and estimate 95% confidence in-
tervals using 2,000 image-level bootstrap resamples.
Table 21 shows swap gains near zero and nearly identical
score rankings for different referents. Despite this weak se-
lection sensitivity, grounding accuracy reaches 67.44% on
RefCOCO and 62.56% on RefCOCOg. The decoder there-
fore answers different prompts effectively even when their
visual rankings change little.
F.4.2. Capacity for Prompt-Conditioned Selection
To test whether this behavior is imposed by the readout ar-
chitecture, we freeze the 7B model and train only its scor-
ing readout using RefCOCO training boxes. At ℓ= 16 and
(Km, Kp) = (205, 51), supervision encourages score prob-
ability mass on regions covering the named object. Training
and held-out examples are separated by image; checkpoints
are selected by held-out recall of target regions among the
retained tokens.
Without changing the original readout architecture, two
training seeds achieve swap gains of +0.395 and +0.394.
Their highest-scoring token belongs to the target region in
88.5% and 87.2% of held-out examples, respectively. The
architecture can therefore express prompt-conditioned rank-
ings. These tests establish readout capacity; they do not
measure an improvement in downstream answering.

Figure 20(b) shows that RefCOCO’s target-specific de-

Dependence on depth and budget.
The selection sweeps
also show later opportunities on reading tasks. At ℓ= 16
and Kp = 73 on 7B, DocVQA retains an answer-informed
advantage of V = 0.0491, compared with 0.0031 on Re-
fCOCO. Figure 20(c) shows the effect of budget pressure:
reducing TextVQA’s retained count from 73 to 18 increases
V (16) from 0.0174 to 0.3107.
These sweeps hold stage counts fixed rather than match-

cern the tested models and interventions. In the ground-
ing experiments, appreciable prompt-to-region signals and
substantial remaining selection benefit overlap within a rel-

tions support preserving broad early visual access before
pruning. Reading tasks retain regional dependence later,
and tighter budgets increase the remaining opportunity for
prompt-conditioned selection.

ToMe [5]
None (training-free)
On NeXT, move merging after CLIP feature extraction so crop
grids can be reconstructed and unpadded before compression.
Merge within unpadded crop regions, order merged tokens by
spatial centroid, and preserve row separators.

None (training-free)
On NeXT, replace fixed per-crop retention with a per-image target
divided across crops to maintain the requested retention ratio across
image layouts.
VisionZip++ [40]
Projector-only tuning on 10% of
instruction data, separately per budget;
vision encoder and LLM frozen.
On NeXT, distribute the per-image target across unpadded crop
regions to exclude padding from selection and budget allocation.
Preserve budget-exempt row separators to retain image-row
structure.
DivPrune [1]
None (training-free)
Exclude already-selected tokens from subsequent selection to
prevent duplicates when distances tie.
VFlowOpt [41]
None (training-free); pruning-policy
search on calibration data.
Replace SigLIP with CLIP attention to match LLaVA. Restore
high-ranked tokens from recycling when needed to meet target
budget. On NeXT, resize recycling grid to unpadded crop geometry.
OTPrune [8]
None (training-free)
None.
ApET [24]
None (training-free)
On NeXT, replace fixed per-crop retention with image-wide budget
allocation across crop patches to match layer-averaged budgets.
FastV [7]
None (training-free)
Since the original FastV paper specifies post-pruning token counts,
recompute retention to match layer-averaged budgets. On
LLaVA-1.5 and NeXT at 32/5.56%, advance the cut to reduce
unpruned overhead. On NeXT, compute retention per image.
SparseVLM [44]
None (training-free)
On LLaVA-1.5 at 32, advance the first cut to reduce unpruned
overhead. On NeXT, scale retained counts per image, including
recycled tokens in the budget.
PyramidDrop [39]
Training-free variant; pretrained
backbone remains frozen.
On LLaVA-1.5 at 128/32, advance cuts to reduce unpruned
overhead. On NeXT, advance the first cut to reach low budgets.
N¨uwa [13]
None (training-free)
On NeXT, fix global/local token allocation to avoid rounding losses
and respect unpadded capacity. Include row separators in budget.
DUET-VLM [33]
Two-stage training: projector
alignment, then joint projector and
full-LLM instruction tuning; vision encoder
frozen.
Preserve prefill position IDs during decoding for cache consistency.
On NeXT, correct crop packing to match NeXT’s unpadded image
layout and preserve budget-exempt row separators.
LearnPruner [34]
Budget-agnostic training of the scoring
MLP on 10% of data; VLM frozen.
On NeXT, score post-projector tokens instead of CLIP features,
using a separately trained MLP for image-wide selection.
HiDrop [36]
Per-budget projector alignment, then
joint projector and full-LLM instruction
tuning; vision encoder frozen.
On LLaVA-1.5 at 32, add an early cut to reduce unpruned overhead.
On NeXT, scale retained counts by image patch count; preserve
row structure with budget-exempt separators until visual exit.
CORE [18]
Projector alignment, then joint
projector and rank-512 LLM LoRA tuning;
vision stack frozen. Budget-agnostic
training with 1,024 visual tokens.
Use fixed-rate rather than adaptive CORE, enforcing an exact
K-token budget. Replace InternLM2 with Vicuna (LLaVA-1.5) or
Llama-3 (LLaVA-NeXT) to match the other methods’ LLMs. If
objects outnumber K, pool the K −1 largest separately and the
remainder into one token.
