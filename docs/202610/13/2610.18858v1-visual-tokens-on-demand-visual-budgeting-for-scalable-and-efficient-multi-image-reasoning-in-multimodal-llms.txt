Visual Tokens on Demand: Visual Budgeting for Scalable and Efficient
Multi-Image Reasoning in Multimodal LLMs
Aimon Rahman, Joymallya Chakraborty, Vidit Bansal, Abhishek Dan
Amazon
{aimonrah, joymally, bansalv, abhidan}@amazon.com
Abstract
Multimodal large language models (MLLMs)
have advanced rapidly, but most remain opti-
mized for single-image inputs. Multi-image
reasoning is costly because each image adds
many visual tokens, increasing encoding and
attention demands. Existing techniques that ad-
dress the above issue, including visual token
pruning, token merging, visual token compres-
sion, learned resampling, hierarchical compres-
sion, retrieval-augmented visual reasoning, and
memory-based architectures, have largely been
designed for single-image scenarios, where re-
dundancy is primarily spatial and local. Multi-
image reasoning introduces additional chal-
lenges: redundancy may occur across images,
task-relevant evidence may be sparse or dis-
tributed, and aggressive per-image compres-
sion can remove details needed for comparison,
counting, temporal reasoning, retrieval, or ev-
idence grounding. Moreover, existing token-
reduction methods are often either (i) training-
free but inference-inefficient, typically relying
on accessing attention matrices to get token-
importance that may be impractical to access
with memory-efficient attention implementa-
tions such as FlashAttention, or (ii) training-
dependent, requiring the base MLLM to be re-
trained or adapted to a new compressed-token
paradigm.
In this work, we study scalable
multi-image reasoning and understanding with-
out retraining the base model and without ac-
cessing attention matrices at inference time.
To this end, we propose mmVB (multi-modal
Visual Budgeter), a lightweight plug-and-play
module that assigns query-conditioned per-
image token budgets via cross-image context,
adding only 1.6M trainable parameters while
reducing visual input tokens by 9×. This re-
duction yields 5× faster time-to-first-token and
7× fewer prefill FLOPs, while maintaining
94.4% of full-resolution accuracy across eight
multi-image reasoning benchmarks (geometric
mean of per-benchmark ratios). These results
show that input-aware visual scaling can sub-
stantially improve efficiency while preserving
multi-image reasoning performance.

Introduction
Although current multimodal large language mod-
els (MLLMs) can accept multiple images as input,
the problem of scaling them efficiently to many-
image settings remains underexplored.
Multi-
image understanding and reasoning is inherently
challenging: the model must identify relevant vi-
sual evidence, compare information across images,
and aggregate details that may be distributed over
the input set. Scaling this capability further in-
creases the difficulty, as every additional image con-
tributes visual tokens and raises memory, latency,
and compute costs. This limitation is especially
important in real-world applications. For instance,
a catalog-oriented LLM may need to process mul-
tiple product images to understand item attributes,
compare variants, and answer user queries, while
operating within a constrained inference budget.
Visual token redundancy has been widely stud-
ied, with many works showing that multimodal lan-
guage models can operate effectively, and in some
cases even better, with fewer visual tokens. Ex-
isting approaches typically reduce tokens through
pruning, merging, or learned compression, either
in a training-free manner or by training additional
modules. However, applying these methods to
scalable multi-image reasoning presents three key
challenges: i) Limited systems scalability. Many
training-free methods rely on accessing attention
matrices, often across multiple layers, to estimate
token importance. This can be memory-intensive
and difficult to integrate with optimized inference
kernels such as FlashAttention, which avoid ma-
terializing dense attention maps. Production sys-
tems often depend on such kernels for speed and
memory efficiency, meaning that methods requir-
ing explicit access to dense attention matrices may
be impractical at scale. Token merging can also dis-

Proposed mmVB Method
2.1
Architecture
Given N images {I1, . . . , IN} of arbitrary reso-
lutions and a text query Q, the Visual Budgeter
assigns each image a token-allocation level ℓi ∈
{0, 1, 2, 3, 4} that approximates the minimum num-
ber of visual tokens the MLLM needs to answer
correctly. In doing so, the budgeter also learns each
image’s relative relevance to the query. The full
architecture is shown in Fig. 2.
Encoding and Context Fusion.
Each image is
encoded by a frozen EfficientNet-B0 (Tan and Le,
2019) and linearly projected to d = 256. The query
is embedded with the frozen MLLM embedding
layer, mean-pooled, and mapped to the same space
through a 64-dimensional bottleneck. The query
token and N image tokens are then processed by a
two-layer, four-head Transformer encoder, which
performs query-conditioned, cross-image reason-
ing about each image’s required detail and relative
importance.

2.3
Training-data Generation
We address image interactions using a three-round
procedure: independent ascending search, joint ver-
ification, and iterative relaxation (Algorithm 1).
For each image, we sweep Levels 0–4 and select
the first level that yields a correct MLLM response,
without assuming monotonic accuracy across reso-
lutions. Single-image samples require at most five
forward passes plus a pre-check, while multi-image
samples cost O(5N +KN) in the worst case. With
K = 5, joint verification succeeds without relax-
ation for most samples, so the average cost remains
close to independent search. Label generation is
performed once offline, and the resulting labels
are reused across training runs and hyperparam-
eter sweeps. A text-only ablation assigns nearly
uniform levels, showing that meaningful allocation
requires modeling the query×image-content inter-
action through multi-resolution MLLM evaluation.
Label Interpretation.
The labels produced by
Algorithm 1 are sufficient feasible allocations, not
provably minimal ones. The ascending sweep finds
the first working level per image without assuming
that correctness is monotonic in resolution; even if
a higher level were to fail due to MLLM stochas-
ticity, the returned level is verified correct. Note
that the pre-check (line 1) guarantees that full reso-
lution produces the correct answer; consequently,
both the Round 1 sweep (which tests each image at
level 4 while others remain at full resolution) and
the line 29 fallback to (4, . . . , 4) are guaranteed to
be valid. No sample that fails at full resolution en-
ters the label generation pipeline. For multi-image
samples, the greedy relaxation in Round 3 searches
single-image increments and then uniform incre-
ments, which may not discover the cheapest subset
combination (e.g., two images needing simultane-
ous promotion). This means the returned allocation
can over-allocate relative to the true joint minimum.
Crucially, this bias is conservative: labels may as-
sign more tokens than strictly necessary but never
fewer, since every returned vector is verified to pro-
duce the correct answer. For training, conservative

Dataset.
The single-image training set contains
484k samples from 30k images drawn from
VQAv2, TextVQA, and GQA. Pairing each image
with multiple queries provides a contrastive signal,
as its optimal token level may vary by question,
encouraging the predictor to model query×image
interactions rather than memorize image-specific
levels. We also generate 50k multi-image samples
from Mantis-Instruct (Jiang et al., 2024) and train
with a curriculum that introduces multi-image data
after single-image training. Mantis-Eval is a sepa-
rate 217-sample benchmark released by the same
authors.

Main Results. Table 1 compares mmVB against
post-encoding baselines. mmVB achieves the high-
est harmonic mean on six of eight benchmarks
(average HM of 0.90), retaining 94.4% of full-
resolution accuracy while reducing visual tokens
by 9.2× and prefill FLOPs by 7.3× (geomet-
ric mean across benchmarks). ZooPrune attains
the best HM on MIBench and Visual Haystacks,
where its aggressive token pruning (keep ratio

offs; however, it still requires a full ViT forward
pass and cannot reduce encoder compute.
Vi-
sionTrim is the strongest post-encoding baseline
in accuracy, matching or exceeding mmVB on
MIBench and MIRB, but it consumes 1.5–9.1×
more tokens per sample than mmVB to achieve
comparable accuracy. Even on benchmarks where
mmVB trails VisionTrim by 1–4 points in accu-

solution, prioritizing query-relevant images while
leaving less relevant images at lower resolutions.

under the same total budget and can recover full
accuracy with substantially fewer tokens. These
results show that visual-token efficiency depends
not only on how many tokens are retained, but also
on how they are distributed across images.

Does the mmVB learn per-image relevance?
Yes. By assigning the lowest token budget to im-
ages irrelevant to the query, the predictor implicitly
learns a relevance signal. On Visual Haystacks, a
needle-in-haystack benchmark where only 1 of up
to 10 images contains the answer mmVB assigns
Level 0 to 87% of images and reserves higher lev-
els for the target. This emergent behavior arises
without explicit relevance supervision: the GT gen-
eration procedure finds that irrelevant images can
be maximally compressed without affecting cor-
rectness, so the predictor learns to map “irrelevant
to query” directly onto minimal resolution. Exam-
ples are illustrated in Figure 4.

5
Conclusion
We presented mmVB, a plug-and-play module that
assigns query-aware token budgets before visual en-
coding. It achieves 9× token reduction, 7× fewer
prefill FLOPs, and 5× faster TTFT while retaining
94.4% accuracy, with negligible latency and strong
cross-model transfer.

Architecture Details
Image Encoding.
Each image is encoded by a
frozen ImageNet-pretrained EfficientNet-B0 back-
bone (Tan and Le, 2019). Its 1280-dimensional fea-
ture is projected to the shared dimension d = 256
using a linear layer and dropout. Processing low-
resolution thumbnails with EfficientNet-B0 is sub-
stantially cheaper than applying the MLLM vision
encoder to every image.
Text Encoding.
The query Q is tokenized and
passed through the frozen MLLM embedding layer.
The resulting dLLM-dimensional token representa-
tions are mean-pooled and projected to the shared
space by Linear(dLLM, 64), ReLU, Dropout(0.3),
Linear(64, 256), and LayerNorm. The narrow bot-
tleneck limits memorization and encourages the
retention of task-relevant query information.
Context Fusion.
The query embedding and N
image embeddings form a sequence of N+1 tokens
with dimension 256. A two-layer Transformer en-
coder with four attention heads and a feed-forward
dimension of 512 performs query-conditioned and
cross-image reasoning, allowing each image to es-
timate both its required visual detail and its relative
importance.
Resolution Head.
Each image representation is
passed through an MLP with dimensions 256 →
64 →4.

Inference and Parameter Count.
Each image is
resized according to its predicted token level, with
the target resolution capped at the original dimen-
sions to avoid upscaling. The budgeter processes
only N + 1 tokens through two Transformer layers,
adding approximately 2ms of overhead. It contains
about 1.6 million trainable parameters: 328k in the
image projection, 181k in the text encoder, 1,054k
in the Transformer, and 17k in the resolution head.
The frozen EfficientNet-B0 adds 5.3 million pa-
rameters. The MLLM embedding layer must also
remain in memory, but introduces no additional
cost when co-deployed with the target MLLM.
Design Choices.
We select EfficientNet-B0 as
the frozen image encoder because it offers the best

accuracy among models at the ∼5M parameter
scale (77.1% ImageNet top-1), outperforming Mo-
bileNetV2 (72.0%), MobileNetV3-Large (75.2%),
and DeiT-Tiny (72.2%).
TinyViT-5M achieves
higher accuracy (79.1%) but requires 3.3× more
compute; since the encoder processes N thumb-
nails per sample, this overhead compounds. Be-
cause the backbone is frozen, parameter count is
immaterial; only representation quality matters for
downstream level prediction. The remaining hy-
perparameters were selected via validation perfor-
mance on the token-based training set (Table 4).
Two Transformer layers provide sufficient capacity
for cross-image reasoning without overfitting; a
single layer under-performs on multi-image bench-
marks where relative importance must be inferred.
Five ordinal levels match the natural 4× token gaps
in vision encoders, providing meaningful resolu-
tion distinctions at each step. The 64-dim text bot-
tleneck restricts information flow from the 2560-
dim LLM embeddings; wider bottlenecks memo-
rize per-sample query identity while narrower ones
discard task-type signal.

benchmark tests whether models reason across im-

C.4
Mantis-Eval
Mantis-Eval (Jiang et al., 2024) contains 217
human-annotated problems in multiple-choice and
short-answer formats. Each sample includes 2–5
images and covers common-sense, complex, log-
ical, and fine-grained visual reasoning. Despite
its small size, the benchmark is difficult: GPT-4V
achieves 62.67%.
C.5
NLVR2
NLVR2 (Suhr et al., 2019) evaluates reasoning
over image pairs. Each sample presents two pho-
tographs and a natural-language statement that
must be classified as True or False. The statements
require reasoning about object properties, spatial
relations, comparison, counting, and set-level quan-
tification. The test set contains 6,967 samples, in-
cluding 3,558 true and 3,409 false instances.
C.6
Visual Haystacks
Visual Haystacks (Wu et al., 2025) is a vision-
centric Needle-In-A-Haystack benchmark for large
multimodal models. Each sample mixes a small
set of “needle” images with distractors and asks
whether all or any needle images also contain a tar-
get object. The full dataset contains 20,100 samples
with 1 to more than 10,000 images. We cap each
haystack at 10 images by retaining all positives and
randomly sampling negatives. The benchmark tests
retrieval and reasoning over large image sets.

C.7
MMIU
MMIU (Meng et al., 2025) contains 93,584 sam-
ples, 77,659 images, and about 52 tasks spanning
semantic, temporal, and spatial relations. Tasks
include forensic detection, correspondence, tem-
poral ordering, action assessment, jigsaw solving,
3D pose estimation, and Raven’s matrices. Each
sample contains 1–62 images and a multiple-choice
question with up to 10 options. The random base-
line is 27.4%, and the best reported model, GPT-4o,
achieves 55.7%.
C.8
MIRB
MIRB (Zhao et al., 2024) contains 1,124 samples
across 14 tasks covering perception, knowledge,
reasoning, and multi-hop reasoning. Tasks include
visual analogy, cross-image counting, multi-view
3D understanding, code and paper comprehension,
ingredient analysis, jigsaw puzzles, plot interpre-
tation, landmark recognition, and visual chain rea-
soning. Answer formats include multiple-choice,
yes/no, counting, and free-form extraction.
C.9
ReMI
ReMI (Kazemi et al., 2024) contains 2,600 samples
across 13 mathematical, spatial, temporal, and log-
ical reasoning tasks. These include Charts, Clocks,
CodeEdit, Collisions, EmojiAlgebra, FuncRead,
GeomCost, GeomShape, IQ, Isomorphism, Maps,
RefCoco, and Schedule. Each sample contains 2–
6 images and requires a short free-form answer.
ReMI emphasizes quantitative and structured rea-
soning rather than visual matching alone.
C.10
mmVB Training Data Composition
We generate resolution labels over a diverse train-
ing set combining single-image and multi-image
sources:
Single-image (484k samples): VQAv2, TextVQA,
GQA covering natural scenes, text-heavy images,
and compositional reasoning respectively.
Multi-image (50k samples from Mantis-Instruct):
17 subsets including NLVR2, LLaVA-multi, spot-
the-diff, visual storytelling, coinstruct, and others,
covering 2–10+ images per sample with diverse
multi-image reasoning tasks.
Samples where the MLLM answers incorrectly
at full resolution are discarded, as they provide no
valid signal for minimum-resolution search. Gener-
ation is parallelized across 8 GPUs with manifest
sharding for throughput.
