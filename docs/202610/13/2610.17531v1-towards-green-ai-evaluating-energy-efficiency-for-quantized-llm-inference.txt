Abstract
While it is known that quantization of large language models (LLMs) reduces mem-
ory usage via lower-bit weights, studies quantifying the resulting impact on energy usage
and carbon intensity are scarce. We present the first unified evaluation of weight-only
quantization strategies for LLM inference across varying input/output lengths and GPUs,
highlighting differences in energy efficiency in addition to accuracy degradation and run-
time. We study quantized inference on Llama 2 7B, Phi 3.5 Mini Instruct, OLMo 1B, Qwen
2.5 7B, and Qwen 2.5 14B across GLUE MNLI, MMLU, HumanEval, and GSM8K datasets,
evaluating 10 post-training quantization (PTQ) strategies on NVIDIA H100, A6000, A100,
and L4 GPUs. We identify a novel trend showing that quantization techniques tend to ex-
hibit peak energy efficiency relative to full-precision baselines when inputs are sufficiently
long and outputs are short. Furthermore, we show that quantization strategies become
marginally more energy-efficient relative to full-precision models as batch sizes increase,
though gains are modest. Notably, fused-kernel implementations such as EETQ int8 and
Bitsandbytes int8 offer the highest energy savings, up to 4× compared to FP32 on short
text generation, with negligible accuracy loss. Finally, we observe that energy usage closely
tracks runtime on our evaluated benchmarks, indicating that, in practice, latency optimiza-
tion can serve as a reliable proxy for reducing the environmental footprint of LLM services.
We conclude with suggested directions for strategically selecting low-carbon quantization
strategies based on specific inference requirements.

Chapter 1
Introduction
Model quantization has emerged as a leading technique to improve memory efficiency of
large language models (LLMs) [18], significantly reducing memory footprint and traffic
by representing model weights in lower precision (e.g., 8-bit integers instead of 16-bit or
32-bit floats). Building on 8-bit success, recent research has pushed to 4-bit quantization
for even greater gains. Techniques like Generalized Post-Training Quantization (GPTQ)
[13] and Activation-aware Weight Quantization (AWQ) [28] claim to achieve near-lossless
4-bit compression by carefully calibrating quantization to minimize the error in each layer’s
outputs. The compression from quantization directly translates to lower memory usage and
can unlock faster inference due to using a lower bitwidth which takes less time to transfer.
LLMs can deliver impressive capabilities, but at high energy cost and carbon emissions
[9, 50, 17, 30, 20]. While the training phase for these models is resource-intensive, the
inference phase, serving billions of queries, is projected to dominate the carbon footprint
of LLMs and is already producing 25× the carbon emissions used to train GPT-3 every sin-
gle year [6]. Prior evaluations of inference using quantization strategies [23, 36, 14, 29, 46]
primarily focus on accuracy and memory efficiency, often lacking a detailed analysis or
quantification of the correlating energy consumption. Some prior works report energy us-
age [22, 35, 2], but their scope is constrained, with narrow dataset coverage and limited
analysis of performance across differing workloads. In contrast, our work systematically
benchmarks energy usage across multiple quantization methods, offering the first com-
prehensive evaluation that directly connects quantization choices with energy efficiency
outcomes, including contributions from input and output stages.
The energy efficiency benefits of quantization stem from multiple factors.
First, a
smaller model (in memory) means less data to transfer during each step from GPU mem-
1


ory to compute units, which improves utilization and mitigates memory-bound stalls [16].
Second, specialized low-bit kernels can compute matrix multiplications using int4/int8
arithmetic, which processes more operations per cycle (e.g., via NVIDIA Tensor Cores
that support 4-bit and 8-bit dot products), thus finishing computations faster [47]. Faster
execution often consumes less energy per task since the GPU spends less time active, even
if instantaneous power draw may be similar. However, achieving these gains in practice
depends on the implementation. If the quantized inference pipeline introduces high over-
head (e.g., per-token dequantization costs, or inefficient kernel launches), the theoretical
savings may not materialize. Recent developments have looked into even quantizing the
KV cache to reduce memory and energy, with 4-bit KV entries yielding up to 2–3× less
memory use for long contexts [42]. In this work, we focus on weight quantization, but we
note that comprehensive solutions to LLM efficiency may combine weight, activation, and
cache quantization. Further, we exclusively analyze PTQ methods as testing quantization-
aware training (QAT) methods would require retraining large models from scratch, which
is computationally infeasible for our setup.
In sum, energy-efficient LLM inference requires balancing compute, accuracy, and mem-
ory considerations, with quantization offering a promising path to cut both memory usage
and compute time. The key questions we investigate are: How do different quantization
strategies compare in energy per token across tasks? Which methods best handle short
outputs versus long outputs? And how do kernel fusion and configuration details impact
real-world energy savings? By evaluating a range of 4-bit and 8-bit approaches on multiple
models and tasks, we aim to provide actionable insights for deploying LLMs with lower
carbon footprints.
1.1
Thesis Contributions
This thesis advances the literature on LLM inference optimization by providing the first
holistic, empirical comparison of weight-only post-training quantization strategies with a
primary focus on energy efficiency. While prior work has largely emphasized memory re-
duction and accuracy impacts, this study systematically measures energy consumption,
runtime, and accuracy across varying input/output lengths, batch sizes, model architec-
tures, and GPU platforms.
Through this analysis we identify several key findings for
practitioners to refer to when implementing or improving real-world systems.

Chapter 2
Background
This chapter establishes the theoretical foundations necessary to analyze the efficiency of
large language models (LLMs). We first review the inference stage of LLMs, followed by
the Transformer architecture that underpins these models, and finally a detailed overview
of the quantization principles used to reduce their memory footprint and computational
cost.
2.1
LLMs for Inference
Deploying LLMs involves a multi-stage pipeline that transforms input text into generated
output. Unlike the training phase, which focuses on weight updates and typically maxi-
mizes throughput via large batches, inference is often latency-sensitive and operates in two
distinct phases: prefill and decode.
The prefill phase occurs when the model receives an input prompt. During this stage,
the model processes all input tokens in parallel. Because the entire prompt is available at
once, the underlying GPU operations consist of large matrix-matrix multiplications.
Following prefill, the model enters the decode phase which generates output tokens.
Since LLMs are autoregressive, they generate output one token at a time. The model
predicts the next token, appends it to the sequence, and feeds it back as input for the sub-
sequent step. Unlike prefill, this phase involves matrix-vector multiplications because each
decoding step processes a single newly generated token, resulting in a token representation
that is multiplied against the model’s weight matrices.

2.2
Transformer Architecture
The Transformer architecture was introduced by Vaswani et al. for sequence modeling
tasks [44]. It is based solely on attention mechanisms and feed-forward networks. The
Transformer processes an input sequence by first embedding the tokens and adding po-
sitional encodings to retain word order. Each Transformer layer consists of a multi-head
self-attention mechanism followed by a feed-forward network, with residual connections
and layer normalization applied around each sublayer. The encoder stack maps the en-
tire input sequence to a sequence of contextual representations that capture relationships
among all tokens.
Transformers are widely used in large language models because they enable parallel
computation and capture long-range dependencies. In a Transformer, self-attention con-
nects every pair of positions in the input with a constant number of sequential operations,
in contrast to recurrent networks whose computation steps scale with sequence length.
This design is more parallelizable and trains faster than recurrent architectures. As a re-
sult, very large Transformer-based models can be trained efficiently on vast datasets, which
attain state-of-the-art performance on many language tasks.

Transformer, an attention function maps queries and keys to an output via a weighted sum
of values. Specifically, the Transformer uses scaled dot-product attention. Given a query
matrix Q, a keys matrix K, and a values matrix V , the attention output is described as

2.2.2
Encoder vs. Decoder
The Transformer architecture includes an encoder and a decoder. The encoder consists of
a series of identical layers; each encoder layer has two sublayers: multi-head self-attention
and a feed-forward network. The encoder reads the entire input sequence in parallel and
produces a set of contextualized embeddings that capture relationships between tokens.
Encoder-only models (such as BERT [12]) use only this stack of layers without a decoder.
The decoder is also a stack of identical layers, but each layer has three sublayers. Each
decoder layer is made up of a masked multi-head self-attention layer, a multi-head attention
over the encoder’s output, and a feed-forward network. The masked self-attention in the
decoder ensures that each position can only attend to earlier positions, enforcing auto-
regressive generation. Decoder-only models (such as GPT [34]) omit the separate encoder
and cross-attention sublayer and apply masked self-attention to the input prompt. During
inference with a decoder model, there is a prefill phase, processing the input context in
parallel, and a decode phase, generating tokens one by one. In practice, models first process
the input prompt (the prefill phase) to initialize its key–value memory, and then generate
each new token sequentially, conditional on all previously generated tokens.

2.3.2
Quantization Granularity
Quantization granularity refers to the scope over which a set of quantization parameters
(scale and zero-point) is shared. Different granularities trade off memory and compute
efficiency for accuracy.
Per-Tensor Quantization
Per-tensor quantization uses a single scale and zero-point for an entire tensor (e.g.,
all weights of a layer). This is the simplest variation since every value in the tensor is
quantized uniformly using the same mapping. While it minimizes metadata and is easy to
implement, it may suffer from substantial accuracy degradation if values across different
channels vary widely in magnitude.
Per-Channel Quantization
Per-channel quantization assigns separate quantization parameters to each output chan-
nel (row/column of the weight matrix, depending on convention). In convolutional and
linear layers, this typically means each output neuron is quantized with its own scale and
zero-point. This approach better preserves the relative precision of weights across chan-
nels, particularly when their distributions differ significantly. The main trade-off with this
approach is increased memory and compute overhead, as additional scale and zero-point
values must be stored and calculated per channel.
Per-Group (Block) Quantization
7


Per-group, or block-wise, quantization generalizes per-channel quantization by grouping
weight elements (or channels) into fixed-size blocks, each sharing a common scale and zero-
point. Typical group sizes tend to be in the range of 32 to 128. Smaller group sizes improve
accuracy by using tighter mappings to local variations in the weights, whereas larger group
sizes can lower memory usage and computational costs.

Chapter 3
Related Work
This chapter is intended to provide context of existing surveys on different quantization
strategies and their energy usage.
Li et al. (2024) perform one of the first large-scale evaluations of post-training quan-
tization on LLMs [27]. They apply weight, activation, and KV-cache quantization to 11
model families (125M–180B parameters) across diverse tasks (basic NLP, emergent abili-
ties, trust, dialogue, long-context). Their key findings include that moderate quantization
(e.g., 4-bit weights, 8-bit activations, 4-bit KV) generally causes negligible accuracy loss.
They also observe that larger models tolerate more aggressive weight/KV quantization (so
a big model at 3-bit can outperform a smaller model at higher precision under the same
memory) [27]. However, Li et al. focus primarily on accuracy across benchmarks and do
not evaluate real-world inference efficiency, GPU latency, or energy measurements, nor do
they examine variability across different hardware or sequence lengths. In contrast, this
thesis extends their evaluation by systematically benchmarking quantized LLM inference
on multiple different GPUs, including latency and energy consumption as well as accuracy.
This makes our study the first to jointly consider accuracy, latency, and energy tradeoffs
in quantized LLMs across multiple hardware and model configurations.
Jin et al. (2024) propose a structured framework for evaluating quantization in LLMs
along three axes (knowledge/capacity, alignment, and efficiency) [23]. They conduct ex-
periments on ten diverse benchmarks and find that 4-bit quantization typically retains per-
formance comparable to the full-precision models. They also show that larger-parameter
quantized LLMs generally outperform smaller ones, reinforcing the notion that model scale
can offset quantization loss [23]. Importantly, Jin et al. note that quantization yields mem-
ory savings but often slows down inference (i.e., a speed/memory tradeoff). While they
9


highlight this efficiency issue, they do not provide detailed hardware-level measurements
of inference time or power draw. In particular, they do not quantify energy usage or study
performance variability across different GPUs or sequence lengths. Our work builds on
these insights by explicitly measuring the hardware efficiency of quantized inference across
different models and GPUs. This allows us to fill the gap left by Jin et al., namely, to
report how various quantization schemes trade off accuracy and energy/latency in practice.
Hu et al. (2024) perform a cluster-level analysis of six months of LLM development
workloads in a production GPU datacenter [21]. They characterize how LLM training and
evaluation jobs differ from traditional deep learning, finding that many LLM jobs are rela-
tively short evaluation tasks (leading to queuing delays) and that resource usage is highly
imbalanced (e.g., pretraining jobs use the vast majority of GPU-hours while evaluation jobs
dominate job count) [21]. Crucially, they observe that GPUs dominate power consumption
in the cluster, roughly two-thirds of server power is drawn by GPUs (with CPUs and PSUs
consuming ∼11.2% and 9.6% respectively) [21]. They use these observations to motivate
fault-tolerant training and smarter scheduling for LLMs. However, Hu et al.’s study is at
the systems/datacenter level and does not examine model quantization or inference at the
user level. They do not investigate how quantization affects per-query latency or energy
on different GPU types. Our benchmark complements their work by drilling down to the
model-inference level: we measure how quantizing LLMs impacts both performance and
energy efficiency on modern GPUs. We bridge the high-level datacenter perspective of Hu
et al. with fine-grained inference evaluation, establishing the first benchmark for quantized
LLMs that explicitly measures energy consumption alongside accuracy and latency.
Alizadeh et al. (2024) provide a broad experimental analysis of energy consumption
and accuracy trade-offs specifically for software development tasks [2].
They evaluate
model families across four distinct coding activities: code generation, bug fixing, docstring
generation, and test generation. Their findings indicate that energy usage varies signif-
icantly by task type and that larger models often yield diminishing returns in accuracy
relative to their power costs. However, Alizadeh et al. treat quantization as a uniform
variable, relying exclusively on the GGUF format used by the Ollama runtime without
distinguishing between the underlying algorithms. They do not investigate how different
post-training quantization techniques or their specific kernel implementations influence ef-
ficiency. In contrast, our work shifts the focus from model selection to strategy selection:
we systematically benchmark ten distinct quantization methods and analyze how specific
algorithmic choices and input/output token ratios drive energy efficiency, providing the
granular technical analysis of quantization overhead that Alizadeh et al. overlook.

Chapter 4
Quantization Techniques
This chapter provides a detailed technical analysis of the seven PTQ techniques selected
for evaluation in this study. These methods represent a diverse cross-section of the current
state-of-the-art, varying in their optimization objectives (weight reconstruction vs. output
error minimization), target bit-widths (4-bit vs.

4.1
Quanto
Quanto [8] is a PyTorch-based quantization strategy designed for flexibility and ease of
use.
4.1.1
Quantization Approach
Quanto implements linear weight quantization in multiple precisions (int8, int4, int2, and
float8). Quanto quantization can be applied per-tensor or per-channel, with per-channel
default for int8 and float8, to better capture each layer’s weight distribution, per-group
quantization as a default for lower bitwidths such as int4. By default, weight quantization
is symmetric (zero-point 0), using simple statistics like min/max or absolute max as the
scaling factor. For example, 4-bit weights are linearly mapped into 16 levels (-8 to 7) by
dividing each weight’s range by a scale and rounding.
Quanto supports very low bit-widths (2-4 bits) by allowing group-wise quantization:
weights are divided into groups that each get their own scale/zero-point to reduce quantiza-
tion error. Overall, the initial PTQ procedure in Quanto closely follows PyTorch’s standard
approach: insert quantize/dequantize hooks around layers and determine scales/zero-points
via calibration or known distributions.
4.1.2
Quantized Inference Mechanism
Once calibrated, Quanto automatically inserts quantized modules (e.g., QLinear, QConv2d)
to replace float operations. Each module stores quantized weights along with the per-
12


tensor, per-group or per-channel scales (and zero-points if asymmetric) needed to recon-
struct them. On CUDA devices, Quanto uses low-level integer matrix multiplication kernels
to perform operations on quantized data directly. For int8 weights, it can leverage native
tensor-core int8 GEMM. If a subsequent layer is also quantized, Quanto keeps the output
in quantized form (stored as a special QTensor) to avoid unnecessary dequantization and
requantization steps. Otherwise, the output of a quantized linear layer is computed in
higher precision (int32) and then scaled back down.
This quantized tensor propagation means that only a few key layers need to explicitly
handle quantization boundaries. For example, a quantized Linear layer will take quantized
input, multiply int8×int8 →int32, apply the weight’s scale and input scale to produce a
correctly scaled output, and then pass along a quantized output tensor. Biases remain in
full precision and are added after the integer matmul. Notably, Quanto supports float8
quantization on newer GPUs, using NVIDIA’s FP8 formats for activations or weights when
available (falling back to int8 if not).
4.1.3
Distinctive Features and Tradeoffs
Quanto extends PyTorch’s native quantization with features like int2/int4 support (which
core PyTorch lacks) and operation on any device (including GPU and Apple MPS). Unlike
some LLM quantizers, Quanto does not fine-tune or adjust its quantization parameters
using sample data to reduce the difference between the models quantized and original
outputs. This makes it more general but potentially less accurate for very low-bit LLM
quantization unless group-wise schemes are used. Quanto modifies standard PyTorch layers
in-place, making it seamlessly compatible with existing models and workflows. In summary,
Quanto provides a broad PTQ toolbox: any Linear/Conv layer can be quantized to 8, 4,
or 2 bits with minimal code changes.
4.2
HQQ (Half-Quadratic Quantization)
HQQ [3] is a data-free PTQ method that formulates weight quantization as an optimization
problem.
13


4.2.1
Quantization Approach
Unlike other strategies which require real calibration data to minimize discrepancies in
layer outputs, HQQ directly minimizes reconstruction error in the weights, without relying
on any input data. It assumes the pre-trained weights are the only input and tackles the
quantization of each weight tensor by finding the optimal quantization parameters (scale
and zero-point) that best reconstruct the original weights. HQQ’s core idea is to make
quantization more robust by reducing the impact of large magnitude outlier weights. It
does this by modeling the quantization error with a heavy-tailed (hyper-Laplacian) loss
function that penalizes large errors less aggressively, preventing a few extreme weights
from dominating the optimization. Since solving this directly is complex, HQQ uses a
two-step approach: one step adjusts the residual errors through a thresholding update
that pushes small differences towards zero, and the other step updates the quantization
parameters using a simple averaging rule. This alternating process leads to more stable and
accurate low-bit quantizations. This procedure converges in only a few iterations (thanks
to closed-form updates rather than slow gradient descent).
HQQ supports a key parameter axis which controls in which direction quantization
groups are formed. Setting axis = 0 means grouping along the output dimension so that
each output neuron’s weights are quantization separately. Conversely, setting axis = 1
groups along the input dimension, which usually matches hardware kernel layouts and
runs faster, but can lead to a slight drop in accuracy since all outputs share the same
quantization parameters. HQQ allows a flexible group size as well to balance granularity
and storage overhead.
4.2.2
Quantized Inference Mechanism
The output of HQQ’s optimization is a quantized model with integer weights and associated
scales/zero-points for each group. Importantly, HQQ uses standard linear dequantization
output = scale × (int value −zero point), which means it is fully compatible with ex-
isting optimized inference kernels. Because HQQ quantization can be done for very low
bit-widths, it leverages grouped or packed kernel implementations. HQQ itself does not
enforce a custom runtime, producing quantized weights that can be loaded into a variety
of backends. The implementation provides a wrapper HQQLinear module for PyTorch
that replaces nn.Linear layers and under the hood unpacks the low-bit weights for mul-
tiplication. For maximum speed, HQQ recommends using axis=1 (per-column quant) in
combination with fused CUDA/Triton kernels that can handle int4/int2 arithmetic. In
fact, HQQ quantized models can be executed with existing high-performance kernels such
14


as those from NVIDIA’s CUTLASS or implementations like GPTQ’s Triton kernel, since
the dequantization is a linear scaling that can be fused into the matrix multiplication. If
a less common configuration is chosen (e.g., per-row 4-bit), the library can fall back to a
PyTorch implementation which performs dequantization in a slower loop on the fly. Aside
from linear layers, due to its model-agnostic nature, HQQ can quantize other modules, but
focus is usually placed on transformer linear layers since they dominate memory. HQQ
stores its quantized weights as regular integers (int8 or smaller) for easy compatibility.
4.2.3
Distinctive Features and Tradeoffs
HQQ’s major advantage is that it needs no calibration data at all; a user can quantize a
model of any size in minutes just from its weights. This contrasts with GPTQ and AWQ
which require sample data. It explicitly targets weight outliers (common in LLMs) whereas
simpler quantization strategies might just clip or round them. HQQ supports a wide range
of bit-widths (8, 4, 3, 2, and 1-bit) under a unified framework. Another difference is speed
during the actual quantization phase: thanks to closed-form updates, HQQ can quantize
extremely large models quickly (the authors report quantizing a 70B model to 2-bit in
under 5 minutes). In summary, HQQ is a calibration-free, optimization-based quantizer
that yields linearly quantized weights. It is often used as an on-the-fly quantizer (e.g.,
quantizing models at loading time) because of its speed and simplicity of deploying the
resulting model.
4.3
TorchAO
TorchAO [32] is PyTorch’s native library that includes advanced PTQ techniques, providing
a high-level API to quantize models with minimal code changes.
4.3.1
Quantization Approach
TorchAO supports both traditional 8-bit quantization and newer 4-bit weight-only quanti-
zation for LLMs. The weight-only int4 mode applies quantization to nn.Linear (and simi-
lar) layers’ weights while typically leaving activations in higher precision (FP16/FP32) to
avoid accuracy loss. By default, it uses per-row quantization for linear weights: each output
channel (row of the weight matrix) gets its own scale and zero-point (usually symmetric,
so zero-point = 0) for 4-bit values. This row-wise granularity mitigates the variation in
weight distributions across different neurons.
15


4.3.2
Quantized Inference Mechanism
Models quantized by TorchAO use custom module implementations and tensor subclasses
to execute efficiently. When a linear layer is quantized, TorchAO replaces it with a special
int4 kernel. Internally, weights are typically packed into 8-bit containers. For int4, each
byte holds two 4-bit values, and for int8 no additional packing would occur. TorchAO’s
inference engine then uses low-level vectorized kernels to multiply quantized weights with
the incoming activations. In an int4 weight-only scenario, the activations are still float16,
so the kernel will read a block of int4 weights, unpack them into int8 or int16, multiply
with the FP16 activation values, accumulate in FP16/FP32, and produce an FP16 output.
(On NVIDIA GPUs, these kernels may use Tensor Cores by treating 4-bit weights as 8-bit
by zero-extending).
TorchAO emphasizes compatibility with torch.compile, meaning its quantized ops are
written in a way that Torch’s JIT or compiler can fuse them and optimize execution across
modules. Under the hood, the quantized linear layer uses an operation sequence that follows
three steps: (a) pack int4 weights into a contiguous int32 tensor, (b) on each forward pass,
use a kernel to convert int4 to int16 on the fly and perform matrix multiplication with
the FP16 inputs, (c) apply the per-row scale to the result. Because the per-row scales
are constant, TorchAO can pre-multiply them into the weight values or post-multiply the
output vector for each row. For int8 quantization, TorchAO can use more standard int8 *
int8 GEMM routines.
In addition, TorchAO’s quantization is designed to integrate with sparsity: one ad-
vanced feature is the ability to impose 2:4 structured sparsity (meaning that out of every 4
consecutive weights, exactly 2 are non-zero and 2 are forced to zero) alongside int4 quantiza-
tion, which further speeds up inference on supported hardware. Overall, during inference
the layers operate mostly in low-precision space with minimal overhead from dequanti-
zation. Any necessary dequant (for operations that are not quantized, like a nonlinear
activation or residual addition) is done as late as possible and quantization of outputs is
done again before the next quantized layer. This mirrors the approach of PyTorch’s static
quantization, but TorchAO extends it to sub-8-bit regimes and GPU execution.
4.3.3
Distinctive Features and Tradeoffs
TorchAO is PyTorch’s official framework for LLM low-bit quantization, so it places a
strong emphasis on performance and integration. Unlike algorithm-specific quantizers (e.g.,
GPTQ, AWQ), TorchAO is not a standalone algorithm but a framework; it can incorporate
16


techniques like HQQ or potentially others as options. This modular design means TorchAO
can serve as a unified runtime for models quantized with different strategies. Compared
to Hugging Face’s Quanto, TorchAO is more focused on speed optimizations, whereas
Quanto prioritizes broad support and ease of use. TorchAO’s inclusion of FP8 dynamic
quantization is forward-looking, anticipating new hardware capabilities. In contrast, most
other tools focus only on int4/int8. In summary, TorchAO brings together many PTQ
techniques under one roof, from straightforward int8 to cutting-edge int4 with grouping,
backed by efficient kernels and optional enhancements like HQQ. It transforms a float
model to a quantized model in one line, and ensures that the model can be run (and even
fine-tuned with LoRA) just like a normal PyTorch module.
4.4
Bitsandbytes
Bitsandbytes (bnb) [10] provides lightweight quantization for LLMs with a focus on 4-bit
and 8-bit weight compression.
4.4.1
Quantization Approach
Bitsandbytes’ 4-bit quantization method uses a novel NF4 (Normal Float 4) data type,
introduced in the QLoRA paper [11] to quantize weights nonlinearly. The process works
as follows. First, each weight matrix is divided into fixed-size blocks (e.g., 64 values per
block). For each block, bnb computes a scale factor equal to the block’s maximum absolute
value, S = maxi |wi|. The block’s weights are then normalized by S. Next, instead of a
linear quantization (which would map this range into 16 equal levels), Bitsandbytes uses
a predefined set of 16 floating-point levels that are optimized for normally distributed
data. These NF4 quantization levels are spaced such that there are more levels near 0
and fewer toward the extremes. In fact, they are chosen as quantiles of a standard normal
distribution, then scaled.
Each normalized weight in the block is assigned to the nearest of these 16 levels and the
corresponding 4-bit code is stored. Thus, the weight block is compressed into 4-bit indices
plus the scale S needed for dequantization. This strategy effectively gives higher precision
around 0, which is beneficial because neural network weights tend to cluster around zero.
For 8-bit quantization, Bitsandbytes uses a simpler scheme where most weights are int8 but
a few outlier columns stay in higher precision. Specifically, for int8 it identifies “outlier”
feature dimensions (e.g., those with very large norms) and exempts them from quantization,
17


storing them in 16-bit to preserve model accuracy. This avoids the extreme error that a
single badly quantized column could introduce. Bitsandbytes does not typically require a
calibration dataset, it relies on the weight distribution itself. It also offers an alternative
FP4 quantization, but NF4 is recommended due to its better accuracy.
4.4.2
Quantized Inference Mechanism
At runtime, Bitsandbytes uses custom CUDA kernels to perform inference with quantized
weights efficiently. The 4-bit weights are stored in a packed format where each byte contains
two 4-bit values.
The library provides implementations of a quantized matrix, vector
multiply that unpack and dequantize on the fly. In essence, the computation is performed
in higher precision after a partial dequantization, Bitsandbytes does not do pure integer
arithmetic for 4-bit inference, it instead trades off memory for compute: storing weights
at 4-bit, but doing arithmetic in 16-bit or 32-bit.
The library allows configuring the
computation dtype so that one can choose to accumulate in FP32 for maximal accuracy,
or use FP16/BF16 for quicker compute.
Bitsandbytes also implements a technique called double quantization: after quantizing
weights to 4-bit with scales, it can further quantize the scale values themselves (since
storing one FP16 scale per 64 weights still incurs memory). It quantizes those scales to 8
bits with a second-scale, reducing memory overhead of the scales. During inference, the
double quantized scales are first dequantized to FP16 and then used to scale the weight
blocks. This adds a minor overhead but saves memory. The net effect is that Bitsandbytes
can load extremely large models in limited GPU memory by storing weights in 4-bit, and
the runtime only modestly increases the compute (which is often bottlenecked by memory
bandwidth rather than arithmetic).
Additionally, Bitsandbytes provides optimized 8-bit kernels. For LLM.int8(), it splits
each weight matrix into the int8 part and the high-precision part (outlier columns). The
int8 part is multiplied efficiently meanwhile, the outlier columns (few in number) are multi-
plied in full-precision normally and added to the result. This way, the overhead of keeping
some weights at full-precision is small but prevents large errors. Bitsandbytes integrates
with Hugging Face Transformers via a bnb.MatrixMultiplicator or replacing linear layers
with bnb.nn.Linear8Bit/Linear4Bit classes that override the forward pass. The rest of the
model remains unchanged. One constraint is that Bitsandbytes currently quantizes only
weights (activations remain in full-precision and are never quantized) to preserve model
accuracy.
18


4.4.3
Distinctive Features and Tradeoffs
Bitsandbytes was one of the earliest tools to enable LLM inference at 8-bit and 4-bit without
retraining, and it remains popular due to its simplicity. It does not minimize layer output
error like GPTQ, nor does it use calibration data. It relies on statistical assumptions (e.g.,
weights follow normal distribution) and simple rounding. Therefore, purely on accuracy, it
is not theoretically the most optimal design. However, Bitsandbytes’s NF4 quantization is
surprisingly effective. By using non-uniform levels, it retains much of the model accuracy.
In terms of speed, Bitsandbytes runs on GPU but does not fully leverage int4 Tensor
Cores, instead it uses half-precision operations. This is one reason libraries like EETQ
(below) claim higher speed, EETQ uses true int8 cores, whereas bnb’s 4-bit uses more
instructions to unpack and multiply weights using a closed-form, fixed scheme. In summary,
Bitsandbytes offers practical and memory-efficient PTQ by compressing weights with per-
block scales, then performing inference by quick dequantization to higher precision on the
fly. It is straightforward to use and does not need calibration, making it a common baseline
for compressing large models.
4.5
AutoAWQ (Activation-aware Weight Quantization)
AutoAWQ [28] is a library that implements AWQ, a PTQ strategy tailored for LLMs that
uses a small calibration set to guide weight quantization.
4.5.1
Quantization Approach
The primary insight of AWQ is that not all weights (or channels) are equally important
for model accuracy. In particular, some weights (i.e., entire columns or rows of a weight
matrix, corresponding to certain neurons) have a disproportionate effect on the output
and are more sensitive to quantization error. AWQ identifies these salient channels by
analyzing the activation distribution. It runs a set of representative inputs through the
model and records statistics such as the variance or magnitude of each channel’s outputs.
For example, in a linear layer Y = WX, each output dimension j has activation yj = wj·X.
If the distribution of yj has high variance or large magnitude relative to other channels,
then the weights wj are “salient”, a small change in them can cause a large change in
output. AWQ’s strategy is to protect these top channels during quantization. Instead
of resorting to mixed-precision (which would leave those channels in higher bit-width,
complicating hardware execution), AWQ uses an equivalent scaling transformation: it
19


multiplies the salient weight channels by a factor to make them larger. To preserve the
overall computation, it divides the corresponding activations by the same factor at inference
time.
In practice, this is done by folding the scale factor into the next layer’s weights or the
layer’s normalization so that the model’s outputs remain the same. The scale factors are
determined offline from the collected activation stats. Essentially AWQ finds a scaling such
that each protected channel’s quantization error is below a certain threshold or that the
channel’s variance is brought in line with others. Typically, only a very small fraction of
channels (∼1%) need significant scaling. After scaling, AWQ then quantizes all weights
uniformly to low bit-widths. It uses standard per-channel linear quantization (with zero-
point = 0) for all channels, including the scaled ones. Because the salient channels were
amplified, when they are rounded to 4-bit integers the relative rounding error is much
smaller. Thus, AWQ effectively achieves a result similar to mixed precision but within a
single precision (e.g., all 4-bit), which is hardware-friendly and requires no special handling
of different precisions.
4.5.2
Quantized Inference Mechanism
The AutoAWQ library is an implementation that provides an efficient inference backend
for AWQ-quantized models. AutoAWQ includes a custom CUDA kernel for 4-bit matrix
multiplication, similar to other frameworks. It packs the int4 weights and uses a fused
kernel to multiply the model’s weights and activations. The kernel is optimized for both
the context (prefill) stage and generation (decode) stage of LLM inference. It supports
efficient batched GEMM for initial token processing and fast vector-matrix multiplies for
single-token decoding. The result is that an AWQ-quantized model can run significantly
faster and with less memory than the full-precision model. AutoAWQ specifically integrates
with Hugging Face Transformers, so one can load a model that has been quantized with
AWQ directly. It provides an AutoAWQForCausalLM class that will load the quantized
weights.
During the forward pass, the quantized linear layers call the AutoAWQ kernel.
It
reads 4-bit weight blocks, multiplies by input activations, and accumulates. Because AWQ
quantization uses per-channel scales, the kernel must apply each channel’s scale after the
dot-product. A typical implementation is to perform the dot-product for one output chan-
nel completely, then multiply the result by that channel’s scale. Since AWQ does not
change activation precision, no special handling for activation quantization is needed. One
extra step is the transformer layer norm, AWQ often relies on the model having a final
20


layer normalization that can absorb the scaling differences. In some cases, AutoAWQ may
tweak the model’s normalization layers to account for the channel scaling (ensuring the
outputs match the original model). But this is done once at model load time. In summary,
inference with AWQ models is very similar to inference with plain 4-bit models (like GPTQ
models), integer-packed weight multiplications plus a set of per-row scale factors applied
to the output.
4.5.3
Distinctive Features and Tradeoffs
AWQ sits somewhere between simple data-free methods and complex calibration-based
methods. It does use calibration data but in a lightweight manner, just to compute ac-
tivation statistics, not to run heavy optimization. The method is fast to execute (scaling
and rounding weights is O(N) per layer). Compared to GPTQ, AWQ is much faster in
the quantization step (no iterative solving), and theoretically generalizes better to varied
tasks because it does not overfit to a small calibration set through reconstruction. It pri-
marily prevents catastrophic outlier errors. The AWQ paper noted that protecting as few
as 1% of channels was enough to greatly reduce the worst-case quantization error. This
leads to strong results on benchmarks, especially for 4-bit. Another advantage is hardware-
friendliness: all weights end up in int4, so inference can use one uniform kernel without
branching for special cases.
AutoAWQ, the library, further differentiates itself by providing integration with sys-
tems like Hugging Face and vLLM. In summary, AWQ’s approach is activation-aware: by
using knowledge of how weights are actually used (their activations), it achieves a good
compromise of accuracy and efficiency. It is particularly noted for preserving performance
on instruction-tuned and multi-modal models, where some other methods struggle.
4.6
GPTQ (Generalized Post-Training Quantization)
GPTQ [13] is an algorithm that treats quantization as a layer-wise error minimization
problem.
4.6.1
Quantization Approach
For each layer (typically a large fully-connected weight matrix in an LLM), GPTQ opti-
mizes the quantized weights to ensure the layer’s output on representative inputs closely
21


matches the original full-precision output. Unlike naive methods that round weights in-
dependently, GPTQ accounts for the correlations between weights through a process of
sequential quantization with error compensation. The algorithm quantizes the weight ma-
trix one group at a time. When a group is quantized, it inevitably introduces a small
amount of error into the output. Before moving to the next column, the algorithm calcu-
lates this error and adjusts the remaining unquantized weights to compensate for it. This
update relies on an approximate Hessian to determine exactly how the remaining weights
should be shifted to absorb the error, ensuring the total degradation of the layer’s output
is minimized.
Conceptually, it means if quantizing column j caused a small output difference, you
nudge the other columns’ weights in the opposite direction to cancel out that difference.
The algorithm thus alternates between quantizing one column (find the best quantized
values for that column given current residual), then recompute the residual on Y and
apply a correction to all subsequent float columns to absorb the error. To perform these
steps efficiently, GPTQ precomputes some matrices. In the literature, they derive a formula
using Cholesky decompositions to solve for the optimal qj in closed form. The result is that
GPTQ produces a quantized weight matrix that is extremely close to the original in terms
of output on the calibration set. It typically uses asymmetric quantization for weights
(meaning a zero-point may be non-zero) to better fit the distribution. For LLMs, GPTQ is
commonly run with 4 bits per weight and a small calibration set (e.g., 128 random samples
or a few hundred tokens from an evaluation dataset). One can also configure a group
size instead of quantizing entire columns at once. This introduces more scales/zero-points
(one per subgroup) and further reduces error, at the cost of extra storage for those scales.
In many implementations, group size=128 or group size=64 is used as a sweet spot for
4-bit quantization of large models, it cuts down error significantly compared to per-column
quantization.
4.6.2
Quantized Inference Mechanism
A model quantized with GPTQ ends up with weight matrices in a custom format: for each
weight matrix, there are typically: a set of small int matrices (each storing the quantized
values for a group of weights) and accompanying scale/zero-point vectors for each group.
For example, a linear layer of shape [out dim, in dim] quantized to 4-bit with group size
128 will be stored as ⌈in dim
128 ⌉groups per output. Each group has a 4-bit weight matrix
of shape [out dim, 128] and a scale and zero-point per out dim (if grouping along input
axis) or per 128 block segment. At inference, these quantized weights are used much like in
22


Bitsandbytes or AWQ. For each output channel, the kernel multiplies 4-bit weights by full-
precision inputs, accumulates in FP32, then multiplies by the weight scale and adds bias.
If a zero-point (offset) is used, the kernel subtracts the zero-point during the multiplication
(effectively shifting the integer values up or down before scaling). Because GPTQ often
uses per-row scales, the scaling can be applied as a simple vector multiply to the output
of the matmul. In summary, inference looks exactly like a standard affine quantized layer,
done with efficient low-bit arithmetic. Many GPTQ-quantized models can run at nearly
the same speed as 8-bit models, since the bottleneck is memory bandwidth and 4-bit cuts
memory movement in half.
4.6.3
Distinctive Features and Tradeoffs
By explicitly minimizing output errors and using calibration data, GPTQ outperforms
naive methods in preserving model quality. However, this comes at the cost of complexity:
quantization with GPTQ is relatively slow (it can take hours for very large models) and
requires storing the calibration data’s activations (it precomputes XXT or similar, which
can be heavy for big layers). HQQ, by contrast, is much faster but might not always reach
the same level of accuracy on extremely low bits because it ignores output correlations.
AWQ focuses only on a subset of weights and does not account for interactions between
weights, so GPTQ often gives lower final error than AWQ as well.
Another point is
that GPTQ quantizes one layer at a time, assuming others remain in float during that
layer’s calibration. This layer-wise independence simplifies the process but is not globally
optimal. In practice, 4-bit GPTQ has become a common standard for compressing models
like LLaMA 7B,70B with minimal performance loss. Finally, GPTQ’s outputs are often
used as input to further fine-tuning, showcasing that it preserves model functionality well.
In summary, GPTQ is an output-error-minimizing quantizer that produces quantized
weights (usually groupwise int4) ready for fast inference.
Its custom kernels and data
structures are a bit more complex than uniform quantizers, but numerous libraries now
make it seamless to load and use GPTQ-quantized models. This work demonstrates that
PTQ can sometimes push to 4 bits without significant accuracy loss, using clever error
compensation instead of more brute-force methods.
23


4.7
EETQ (Easy & Efficient Transformer Quantiza-
tion)
EETQ [26] is a quantization strategy that focuses on 8-bit weight-only quantization for
transformer models.
4.7.1
Quantization Approach
EETQ offers a relatively straightforward approach to quantizing all linear and embedding
weights to int8 in a per-channel manner, similar to symmetric per-channel min-max quan-
tization used in other quantization frameworks. The zero-point is usually set to 0. Because
EETQ targets 8-bit (which has 256 levels) and most transformer weight distributions are
reasonably well-behaved, this linear quantization already achieves high accuracy, typically
incurring very small relative errors. Importantly, no calibration data is required for basic
EETQ quantization, instead relying on its own weights. EETQ quantizes weights on the
fly when loading a model, allowing the user to provide a full-precision model and an Eetq-
Config(”int8”) to the loader, it converts all appropriate matrices to int8 with scales. EETQ
does not quantize activations, it assumes activations will remain unchanged at runtime.
4.7.2
Quantized Inference Mechanism
EETQ is paired with the EET (Easy & Efficient Transformer) inference engine, which is
highly optimized for NVIDIA GPUs. After quantization, each linear layer has int8 weights
and a per-row scale vector.
To compute y = Wqx (where Wq are int8 and represent
approximately W), EETQ uses custom fused GPU kernels that leverage NVIDIA’s Tensor
Cores in int8 mode. On modern GPUs, Tensor Cores can perform 8-bit integer matrix
multiply-accumulate very quickly, but they typically multiply int8 * int8 (giving int32
accumulations). Dynamic quantization of the input can be as simple as scaling the input
vector by a constant factor (to use the full int8 range) each time. The phrase “per-channel
weight-only quantization” means the output of each channel will be scaled correctly by the
weight’s scale. The result is that EETQ quantized models run extremely fast, since they
effectively use int8 arithmetic throughout.
24


4.7.3
Distinctive Features and Tradeoffs
EETQ is a more conservative quantization scheme compared to others in this presented
list of quantization strategies (since it stays at 8-bit). The benefit is that accuracy loss is
minimal and well within what can often be recovered by subsequent fine-tuning if needed.
EETQ’s primary focus is on speed and efficiency. By quantizing to int8 and using optimized
kernels, it significantly boosts throughput. In many deployment scenarios, int8 quantiza-
tion (with good kernels) is ideal for production because it retains full accuracy and gains
some speed. 4-bit methods might save more memory but can suffer slight accuracy drops
or lack stable hardware support. Because it is weight-only, one can still use techniques like
LoRA fine-tuning on top of an EETQ model (LoRA layers remain in full-precision and are
added to the int8 weights during inference). The Hugging Face documentation explicitly
mentions LoRA fine-tuning is supported on EETQ quantized models.
In comparison to PyTorch’s native int8 quantization which is mainly CPU-focused and
might not fully exploit GPU tensor cores, EETQ is specialized for GPU. EETQ was built
to be simple, exposing a single knob (“int8”) to the user, and letting the library handle
the rest, whereas something like TorchAO or Quanto provide more configuration options.
In summary, EETQ quantization uniformly quantizes all weights to int8, uses efficient per-
channel scaling, and relies on a highly optimized transformer engine to execute the int8
model. This yields a noticeable throughput improvement and memory reduction, with
negligible accuracy loss, making it attractive for enterprise deployments that need to serve
LLMs at scale.

Evaluation
This section presents a comprehensive evaluation of energy efficiency, runtime performance,
and memory usage across a range of quantized LLM configurations.
5.1
Methodology
We benchmark five popular, open-source models representative of state-of-the-art trans-
former architectures: AllenAI OLMo 1B [15], Llama 2 7B [41], Phi 3.5 Mini Instruct
(3.8B) [1], Qwen 2.5 7B [48], and Qwen 2.5 14B. Most transformer architectures at their
core consist mainly of matrix multiplication operations, which are impacted by quantiza-
tion techniques the most. We evaluate these models across 4 tasks with varying output
lengths. GLUE MNLI [45] and MMLU [19], which involve natural language inference and
multiple-choice question answering respectively, generate very short single-token outputs,
while HumanEval [5] and GSM8K [7] require long-form text generation, including multi-
line Python functions and step-by-step math solutions. For GSM8K, we evaluate a 10%
sample of the dataset due to the high computational cost and the low variance observed
in accuracy outcomes.
We evaluate ten quantization strategies derived from seven distinct libraries, including
AutoAWQ int4, Bitsandbytes nf4, Bitsandbytes int8, EETQ int8, GPTQ int4, HQQ int4,
HQQ int8, Quanto int4, Quanto int8, TorchAO int4, and compare a full-precision FP32
baseline.
These methods vary substantially in their backend implementations, ranging
from fused CUDA A100 and Triton [40] kernels to PyTorch matrix multiplication paths,
which affect both overhead and scalability. When initializing these quantization strategies
26


we use the default settings, which is symmetric quantization for all strategies, noting that
their granularities differ: GPTQ, HQQ, and TorchAO default to per-group quantization;
Bitsandbytes uses per-group for int4 and per-channel quantization for int8; and Quanto and
EETQ use per-channel quantization. None of the evaluated strategies use pure per-tensor
quantization by default.
Experiments were conducted over four NVIDIA GPUs: the A100, H100, and RTX
A6000, representing higher-end datacenter accelerators, and the L4, a low-power inference-
optimized GPU. Inference was performed using the PyTorch framework and the Hugging
Face Transformers library. We deliberately avoided specialized inference engines such as
vLLM [25] or NVIDIA TensorRT-LLM [31], as their integrated optimizations would con-
flate the performance gains of the orchestration framework with those of the quantization
methods.
This choice ensures the isolation of quantization-induced gains, providing a
controlled environment to measure the direct impact of each quantization strategy.
Metrics collected include total energy consumption, runtime, memory usage, and ac-
curacy degradation relative to the vanilla FP32 baseline, which refers to the unquantized
full-precision (32-bit float) model without any compression or optimization. This choice
aligns with prior studies such as Shen et al. [37], who evaluate 8-bit formats for post-
training quantization across a diverse set of network architectures, using FP32 as their
primary comparison point. Moreover, FP32 remains the golden standard for accuracy,
while FP16 introduces numerical approximations that can already diverge from the orig-
inal model fidelity. Since our goal is to isolate the effects of quantization, using FP32
avoids conflating quantization-induced error with half-precision optimization effects. This
provides a clearer lens through which to analyze the trade-offs between accuracy, memory
footprint, runtime, and energy consumption introduced by quantization strategies alone.
For all results presented as a ratio (Energy Efficiency, Speedup, and Memory Reduction),
we normalize the performance of the vanilla FP32 model to 1.0. Ratios greater than 1.0
indicate an improvement over the baseline.
To ensure that energy measurements reflect only the workload under evaluation, each
benchmark was run on an otherwise idle GPU. Before each experiment, we verified that
no processes were active on the device using the NVIDIA System Management Interface
(nvidia-smi). GPU energy consumption was then measured using the ZeusMonitor interface
[49], which records energy in joules over user-defined execution windows. For each run,
ZeusMonitor tracked GPU power draw throughout the inference period and returned total
energy and elapsed time. This setup provides a consistent and reproducible measurement
of GPU energy usage across all quantization strategies.
In the following subsections, we analyze how quantization strategies impact energy

efficiency across different output lengths, and discuss how kernel design and runtime char-
acteristics shape real-world performance.

for each new token.

Figure 5.1 presents the energy efficiency ratios (i.e., baseline FP32 energy use divided

and Bitsandbytes int8 - consistently achieve the highest efficiency ratios across all three
tested models (Llama 2 7B, Phi 3.5 Mini, and Qwen 2.5 7B). GPTQ int4 also performs well,
notably on GSM8K and HumanEval which involve longer outputs. These methods yield
energy savings significantly above the 1.0 baseline, demonstrating the substantial energy
reductions they offer relative to the full-precision baseline. It is interesting to note that
Quanto int4/int8, EETQ int8, and Bitsandbytes int8 are unique among the tested strategies
in their use of per-channel quantization instead of per-group quantization, with EETQ int8
and Bitsandbytes int8 performing the best. This might suggest that per-channel quantiza-
tion, despite its potential for increased metadata overhead, offers advantages in capturing
nuanced weight distributions and enabling efficient low-bit kernel implementations that, in
turn, lead to strong energy efficiency. However, these gains should be interpreted as arising
from the combination of per-channel quantization and optimized kernel implementations,
rather than quantization granularity in isolation.
In Figure 5.2 we extended these experiments on a top of the line Nvidia H100 GPU, as
well as a smaller 1B model and a larger 14B model to show generalization. Unfortunately

models show the same findings as the A6000 energy efficiency experiments, demonstrating
that these trends can generalize well.
Other quantization strategies, including AutoAWQ int4, Bitsandbytes nf4, and Tor-
chAO int4, also improve energy efficiency, but inconsistently across benchmarks and to a
lesser extent due to the limited native acceleration for 4-bit arithmetic on current hardware.
These int4 quantizers introduce additional overhead or underutilize tensor core throughput,
which can reduce the expected benefits, aligning with reports that quantization without
specialized hardware support can even slow down inference in some cases [23].
Figure 5.3 (legend and caption on page 37) shows energy efficiency ratios for autogen-
erated randomized data when input lengths are fixed and output lengths vary. The trends
observed show that quantization implementations accelerate large matrix multiplications
(matmuls) more effectively than smaller ones. In particular, during the prefill phase, where
large attention matrices (L×L) are processed, low-bit quantized matmuls result in substan-
tial energy improvements over vanilla FP32. However, in the decode phase, where matrix
multiplications are smaller (for example, 1 × L), the relative energy advantage diminishes.

This is partly because the overall runtime for these smaller operations is lower, so the fixed
overheads associated with quantization such as kernel launch costs and memory manage-
ment account for a larger proportion of the total runtime, thereby limiting the potential
energy savings.
Consistent with these results, our benchmarks for MMLU and GLUE
MNLI, which generate only single-token outputs, show substantial reductions in energy
consumption, whereas benchmarks HumanEval and GSM8K that involve long-form text
or code generation exhibit minimal energy savings due to their decode-heavy workloads.
It is interesting to note that when input sizes are large (such as 1000 or 5000 tokens),
prefill dominates the computation, and quantized models achieve considerable energy sav-
ings, often exceeding a 4 to 8 times improvement over vanilla FP32. On the other hand,
when input sizes are small (for instance, 10 tokens), decode costs dominate, and the energy
efficiency advantage of quantization is reduced. These efficiency gains are observed only
when the prefill matmuls are sufficiently large. For example, on Llama 2 7B running on an
A6000, gains begin to appear around 100 tokens, whereas on a higher-memory GPU like
the A100, significant improvements emerge only around 5000 tokens.
Figure 5.4 displays the energy curves of the inverse experiment, fixing output token
lengths to 1 and varying input lengths. The results provide further evidence that energy
savings increase steadily as input token lengths grow. Notably, HQQ and Quanto strategies,
which rely on PyTorch’s built-in matrix multiplication kernels, exhibit higher overhead
with small input sizes, leading to relatively poor energy efficiency.
However, as input
length increases, the relative impact of this overhead diminishes, and their energy usage
asymptotically approaches that of the vanilla FP32 baseline.
Figure 5.5 displays the speedup ratios (i.e., baseline inference time divided by quantized
time) for the same models, GPU, and quantization strategies as the original benchmarking
experiments. The int8 methods again lead, with both EETQ int8 and Bitsandbytes int8
achieving the greatest speedups, confirming that their energy efficiency translates into
faster inference throughput. The static 4-bit methods (GPTQ, AWQ, HQQ) and general
quantization toolkits (Quanto, TorchAO) register more moderate speedups, often actually
underperforming the FP32 baseline. Again, in Figure 5.6, run on a newer H100 GPU
and a wider subset of models, we can see speedups for supported quantization strategies.
Interestingly, these speedups are slightly diminished on the newer hardware, even though
the energy reduction is not.

quantization generally leads to energy savings, the corresponding reductions in inference
time are not always as pronounced. Some quantization strategies reduce power/energy

Runtime as a Proxy for Energy Efficiency
As shown in Figure 5.9, there is a strong correlation between benchmark runtime and
energy usage across quantization strategies. This trend is consistent across all three models
and all four benchmarks, with Pearson correlation coefficients ranging from 0.989 to 1.000.
On average, the correlation is extremely high at 0.9966, with a standard deviation of just
0.0033, indicating very little variation across tasks and models.
This exceptionally strong correlation implies that optimizing for inference speed is
an effective proxy for optimizing energy efficiency.
In practice, strategies that reduce
runtime, such as low-bit quantization, fused kernel execution, or more efficient attention
mechanisms, will almost always result in proportional reductions in total energy usage.
Therefore, it can sometimes be simpler and more practical to focus on minimizing latency,
which serves as a reliable method to indirectly reduce the environmental footprint of LLM
inference.
Takeaway 5: Reducing inference latency is an effective proxy to lower en-
ergy consumption, given the strong correlation between runtime and energy
usage in practice.

Chapter 6
Conclusion
In this work, we presented an extensive analysis of energy efficiency for quantized LLM
inference, evaluating 10 quantization strategies across three models and four diverse tasks.
We demonstrated that modern 4-bit and 8-bit weight-only quantization techniques can dra-
matically reduce joules per token during generation. In particular, fused low-bit methods
such as EETQ int8 and Bitsandbytes int8 consistently provided the best energy-accuracy
trade-offs, achieving up to 4× energy savings for single-token outputs, with EETQ sus-
taining nearly 2× savings even for long sequences.
Importantly, int8 quantization was able to preserve model accuracy within 2.5% of
full-precision baselines, showing that efficiency gains no longer require major accuracy
sacrifices at 8-bit precision, although 4-bit methods still pose risks. Our findings reinforce
that quantization is not only an optimization, but a practical and powerful tool for making
LLM services significantly more sustainable.
From a practical engineering perspective, our results indicate that latency serves as
a reliable proxy for total energy consumption.
This correlation is driven by the high
static power use of modern GPUs. Since an idle GPU maintains a substantial baseline
power draw, reducing the total runtime of an inference task is often more impactful than
minimizing instantaneous power consumption. Consequently, practitioners can effectively
optimize for energy efficiency by prioritizing low-latency execution. We further observe
that increasing batch sizes can moderately amplify these efficiency gains by enhancing
hardware utilization and distributing fixed architectural overheads over a greater volume
of processed tokens.
Finally, the most important and novel finding of this study is that quantization strate-
gies exhibit peak energy efficiency relative to full-precision baselines on long input, short
55


output workloads. We provide an intuitive architectural explanation for this phenomenon
based on the varying arithmetic intensity of LLM inference phases.
During the prefill
phase, the large matrix-matrix multiplications required for prompt processing allow the
overhead of dequantization to be effectively amortized over a high volume of floating-point
operations. In contrast, the auto-regressive decoding phase relies on smaller matrix-vector
operations where dequantization costs represent a much larger fraction of the total execu-
tion budget. Therefore, as input lengths increase, the system spends a greater proportion
of its energy in the computationally dense prefill stage, where the advantages of quantized
computations most clearly outweigh their overheads.
6.1
Future Work
Building on the empirical findings of this study, this section outlines several promising di-
rections for future research. The following subsections explore avenues for further optimiz-
ing LLM sustainability through intelligent resource management, expanded quantization
scopes, and hardware-specific advancements.
6.1.1
Context-Aware Scheduling and Precision Allocation
One promising direction is the development of intelligent scheduling policies that dynam-
ically select model precision based on the requirements of each request.
For example,
short-answer or less complex queries can be routed to an efficient 8-bit quantized model,
while longer or mission-critical queries can be directed to a full-precision model to maxi-
mize accuracy. This approach leverages the observation that quantized models often retain
high accuracy while consuming significantly less energy. A key insight is that energy cost
scales with output length, since each generated token consumes a roughly constant amount
of energy for a given model. Therefore, if a request is expected to produce a lengthy output
(e.g., a narrative or code completion), a scheduler could assign it to a full-precision model
to maximize accuracy. Conversely, tasks with low token output such as multiple-choice
questions can be assigned to a quantized model, reducing energy use without compromis-
ing quality. This adaptive allocation reduces energy consumption while maintaining output
quality by assigning the appropriate model to each task.
In addition to precision selection, cloud providers can incorporate carbon-aware or-
chestration. Many data centers now monitor the carbon intensity of their power supply
throughout the day. Non-urgent or batch inference jobs can be deferred to times when the
56


grid is cleaner, or migrated to regions where renewable energy is more abundant [38]. By
shifting flexible workloads to off-peak hours or greener geographic locations, schedulers can
lower emissions and energy costs with minimal impact on latency. In summary, intelligent
scheduling that combines precision allocation with carbon-aware workload placement can
enable large cloud data centers to serve LLM requests more sustainably.
6.1.2
Extending Quantization Beyond Model Weights
Another direction is to extend quantization beyond model weights to activations and the
KV cache, thereby unlocking greater efficiency. Most current LLM quantization focuses
on compressing model weights to lower bit-widths. However, for long-context or streaming
inference, the memory overhead from activations, particularly the KV cache in Transform-
ers, becomes a bottleneck. For instance, storing a 10,000-token context for a 7B model in
FP32 can require ∼10 GB of memory, whereas an 8-bit cache would reduce this to ∼2.5
GB. By compressing the cache into 8-bit or even 4-bit formats, LLMs can support much
longer inputs and outputs on the same hardware budget.
Beyond the cache, quantizing layer activations throughout the model is a compelling but
challenging step. A fully quantized deployment, where most matrix multiplications use int8
or int4 arithmetic end-to-end, would enable faster inference and lower energy consumption
per token, allowing more requests to be served on the same hardware. The main challenge is
preserving model fidelity, as aggressive quantization of activations can introduce numerical
error or instability due to outlier values in intermediate layers. Nevertheless, extending
quantization beyond weights is a promising avenue for further reducing the runtime costs
of LLM inference.
6.1.3
Low-Bit Hardware and Accelerator Optimization
On the hardware side, future work could evaluate quantized inference on TPUs [24] and
emerging AI accelerators optimized for low-bit operations to examine energy trends. Such
directions offer practical pathways to extend and apply these findings, with the goal of
advancing scalable and sustainable LLM deployment.
6.1.4
Sources of Energy Efficiency in Quantized Inference
A deeper understanding of the specific origins of energy savings in quantized inference
is essential for guiding the design of more efficient models, quantization techniques, and
57


hardware. Future work should explicitly separate the effects of reduced memory-access
cost from the effects of lower-precision computation. Quantization decreases the number of
bytes transferred through the memory hierarchy and also changes the arithmetic performed
on tensor cores, but the relative contribution of each factor to overall energy reduction is
not yet well understood.
A systematic breakdown of memory traffic, compute activity, and power consumption
during inference would help clarify where the dominant energy savings arise. Such insights
would guide the development of quantization methods that target the most influential
components of the energy budget and inform future designs that better align with the
actual sources of efficiency gains.
6.1.5
Quantization Configuration
While our study provides valuable insights into the energy efficiency and accuracy trade-offs
of various quantization strategies, it does not fully explore the role of quantization granu-
larity or symmetry. Future research could investigate the impact of different granularity or
symmetry settings within each quantization strategy, as the optimal configuration may be
task-dependent and interact with other hyperparameters. A more thorough understanding
of these parameters could lead to further improvements in quantized LLM performance.
6.1.6
Overall Outlook
In summary, the four main directions — smarter scheduling, broader quantization, hard-
ware specialization, and quantization configuration — converge toward scalable and sus-
tainable deployment of large Transformer-based models. Intelligent workload routing and
precision selection allow cloud data centers to reduce waste while maintaining service qual-
ity. Extending quantization beyond weights lowers the resource footprint of each inference
with minimal accuracy loss, and aligning these methods with low-bit hardware ensures en-
ergy savings translate into real-world impact. As inference continues to dominate machine
learning energy consumption, advances in these areas are both timely and significant. To-
gether, they provide a roadmap for deploying large language models responsibly, balancing
efficiency with the quality users expect.
