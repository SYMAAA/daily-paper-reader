Abstract
Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) have achieved
strong performance on diverse vision-language tasks, but
their computational demands limit deployment under con-
strained budgets, particularly on edge devices. Many works
focus on Knowledge Distillation (KD) and try to transfer
knowledge from powerful teacher models to students. In
addition to reducing model size, visual token compression
provides a complementary route to efficiency, but intro-
duces mismatched visual token counts that complicate di-
rect token-wise knowledge transfer. To mitigate this, we pro-
pose DVTR-KD, a knowledge distillation framework based
on Dense Visual Token Reconstruction, which reconstructs
the teacher’s dense visual tokens from the student’s com-
pressed tokens. We reconstruct visual states after the final
LLM layer and at the input to an intermediate layer iden-
tified as important for visual-information readout. At the
intermediate layer, reconstruction is supervised through the
teacher’s response predictions rather than by direct token-
wise KL or MSE matching. An auxiliary attention-readout
loss complements this response-level supervision by align-
ing how the student and the original teacher integrate con-
textual information when predicting responses. Evaluations
on diverse benchmarks demonstrate that DVTR-KD im-
proves the performance of token-compressed students while
retaining the inference efficiency afforded by visual token
compression.
1. Introduction
Large language models (LLMs) [2, 6, 13, 15, 38] have
achieved strong performance in language understanding,
generation, and reasoning. Building upon these advances,
recent studies have sought to extend LLMs beyond the text
modality by integrating visual perception capabilities, giv-
ing rise to Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs).
By connecting vision encoders with powerful LLMs [3, 19,
20, 23, 24], MLLMs are able to perceive visual content
and perform language-based reasoning over multimodal in-
puts. This paradigm has achieved impressive performance
across a wide range of vision-language tasks, including vi-
sual question answering [1, 14, 17, 29], image understand-
ing [27], optical character recognition [28, 30, 31, 33], and
visual reasoning [39], establishing MLLMs as a promising
foundation for general-purpose multimodal intelligence.
However, unlike earlier MLLMs that process images at
fixed resolutions [23–25, 40], advanced models increas-
ingly support native-resolution visual inputs to preserve
fine-grained visual information [3, 20]. While such designs
improve visual perception, especially for high-resolution
images in perception tasks, they often produce substan-
tially longer visual token sequences and consequently in-
troduce considerable computational cost.
This issue be-
comes particularly pronounced in the LLM decoder, where
the computational cost grows quadratically with the se-
quence length. To improve the inference efficiency, some
researchers focus on reducing the number of visual to-
kens while preserving the capabilities of MLLMs [9, 35].
These methods reduce visual redundancy, enabling MLLMs
to process high-resolution visual inputs with significantly
fewer tokens.
Knowledge distillation (KD) provides an effective way
for transferring knowledge from a knowledgeable teacher
model to a student model, typically by encouraging the stu-
dent to imitate the teacher’s output distributions or repre-
sentations. In the context of MLLMs, several studies have
explored knowledge transfer between teacher and student
MLLMs. LLaVA-KD [4], for example, presents a compre-
hensive MLLM distillation framework that combines con-
ventional response distillation with visual representation
and relation distillation. However, relatively little attention
has been paid to distillation between a teacher and a token-
compressed student, where the teacher and student produce
visual sequences of substantially different lengths. EM-KD
[11] addresses this mismatch using the Hungarian matching

algorithm to establish one-to-one correspondences between
teacher and student visual tokens. Despite its effectiveness,
such one-to-one matching inherently utilizes only a subset
of the teacher’s dense visual tokens. Given NT teacher to-
kens and NS < NT student tokens, one-to-one matching
uses at most NS teacher tokens, leaving at least a fraction
1 −NS/NT without direct token-level supervision. Con-
sequently, an increasingly large fraction of the teacher’s vi-
sual representations remains unmatched as the compression
ratio increases, potentially limiting the fine-grained visual
knowledge transferred to highly compressed students.
To address this limitation, we propose DVTR-KD,
which takes different direction: rather than selecting a sub-
set of teacher tokens to match the compressed student se-
quence, we reconstruct dense visual representations from
the student’s compressed visual tokens and align them
with the complete teacher visual sequence. By performing
token-wise distillation on the reconstructed representations,
DVTR-KD allows supervision from all teacher visual to-
kens, encouraging the compressed student representations
to preserve sufficient information to recover the teacher’s
fine-grained visual knowledge.
In addition, while Align-KD [10] explores cross-modal
distillation through first-layer attention and visual-feature
alignment, the importance of visual information may vary
across different depths of the LLM. To investigate this, we
conduct a layer-wise visual ablation study on MLLMs built
upon Qwen3 [38] and MoonViT [19], trained under differ-
ent compression ratios and quantities of training samples.
Specifically, we zero out the value vectors of visual tokens
in the self-attention module of one LLM layer at a time and
measure the resulting increase in response NLL. As illus-
trated in Figure 1, removing visual information at the first
layer has a relatively limited impact, whereas perturbations
at certain intermediate layers lead to substantially larger
degradation. We refer to the layer exhibiting the largest
∆NLL as the Principal Visual Readout Layer (PVRL).
We observe that for a given LLM backbone, the PVRL re-
mains same across different training data scales and visual
token compression ratios. Meanwhile, stronger models con-
sistently exhibit smaller performance degradation under the
same intervention, suggesting different degrees of sensitiv-
ity to visual information at this critical layer.
These observations motivate us to perform visual knowl-
edge distillation at the model’s PVRL, where visual infor-
mation has a particularly strong influence on response pre-
dictions. Specifically, DVTR-KD employs a reconstructor
to recover the teacher’s dense visual representations from
the student’s compressed visual states. The reconstructed
representations are then processed by the remaining teacher
layers, providing teacher-guided supervision that encour-
ages the student to preserve visual information essential for
subsequent language generation. We also align the student’s

In conclusion, our contributions are as follows:
• We propose DVTR-KD, which reconstructs dense vi-
sual states from compressed student tokens to enable dis-
tillation between models with different visual sequence
lengths.
• We use layer-wise visual-value interventions to select an
intermediate reconstruction layer. We supervise recon-

• We evaluate DVTR-KD on various benchmarks. The ex-
perimental results demonstrate that across the evaluated
settings, DVTR-KD achieves state-of-the-art (SOTA) per-
formance, without adding modules or computation at in-
ference time.

2. Related work
2.1. Multimodal Large Language Models
Following the remarkable success of large language models
(LLMs) in natural language processing [32, 36], substan-
tial efforts have been devoted to extending their capabilities
to visual understanding. Early MLLMs typically connect
a pretrained visual encoder with a pretrained LLM through
lightweight cross-modal modules. BLIP-2 [23] introduces
a Querying Transformer (Q-Former) to bridge frozen image
encoders and LLMs, while LLaVA [24] adopts a simpler ar-
chitecture that projects visual features into the LLM embed-
ding space and further performs visual instruction tuning.
Building upon the LLaVA architecture, subsequent models
substantially improve visual resolution and task coverage.
LLaVA-NeXT [26] introduces higher-resolution visual in-
puts to enhance fine-grained perception and OCR capabil-
ities, while LLaVA-OneVision [22] further unifies single-
image, multi-image, and video understanding within a sin-
gle model. More recent MLLMs support flexible-resolution
visual inputs: Qwen3-VL [3] dynamically represents im-
ages of different resolutions with different numbers of vi-
sual tokens, while Kimi-VL [19] employs native-resolution
visual encoding to preserve fine-grained image informa-
tion. These advances have greatly enhanced the perceptual
and reasoning capabilities of MLLMs, but processing high-
resolution visual inputs also introduces increasingly long
visual sequences and substantial computational overhead.
2.2. Distillation for MLLMs
Knowledge distillation has been extensively studied for
transferring the capabilities of large language models to
students. MiniLLM [16] introduces an on-policy method
and performs distillation on student-generated sequences
instead of ground-truth responses, while DistiLLM [21] de-
velops a skewed-KL objective together with an adaptive off-
policy strategy for more efficient LLM distillation.
Recent studies further extend knowledge distillation to
multimodal language models. LLaVA-KD [4] provides a
general MLLM distillation framework that combines re-
sponse distillation with visual representation.
Align-KD
[10] focuses on cross-modal knowledge transfer by distill-
ing first-layer vision–language matching. More recently,
Switch-KD [34] instead feeds student visual representations
into the teacher’s language pathway, enabling multimodal
supervision within a shared text-probability space. DVTR-
KD instead reconstructs the teacher’s intermediate visual
states from the student’s visual representations and opti-
mizes this reconstruction using response-level supervision.
Most existing methods assume compatible visual repre-
sentations between teacher and student and do not explic-
itly address the unequal visual sequence lengths introduced
by token compression. EM-KD [11] directly studies this
problem and employs Hungarian matching to establish one-
to-one correspondences between teacher and student visual
tokens, followed by visual semantic and vision–language
affinity distillation. However, when the student is highly
compressed, only the matched subset of teacher visual to-
kens directly participates in token-level supervision. In con-
trast, DVTR-KD reconstructs a dense visual representation
from compressed student tokens and aligns it with the com-
plete teacher sequence, allowing all teacher visual positions
to contribute to direct visual supervision.

4. Experiments
4.1. Experimental Settings
Implementation Details.
We adopt two variants of
Qwen3 [38], Qwen3-0.6B and Qwen3-4B, as the language
backbones to construct two teacher-student configurations.
Within each configuration, the teacher and student share the
same LLM architecture, while differing in their visual to-
ken compression strategies. We primarily conduct experi-
ments with Qwen3-0.6B and further extend our evaluation
to Qwen3-4B to validate the generality of our method across
different model scales. Both teacher and student models are
initialized from the same officially released text-only LLM
checkpoints, without prior multimodal fine-tuning. For vi-
sual feature extraction, we employ a pretrained MoonViT-
SO-400M [19] as the frozen visual encoder, which sup-
ports native-resolution image processing and produces fine-
grained visual representations.
For the uncompressed teacher models, we employ a two-
layer MLP projector to map visual representations into the
LLM embedding space without reducing the number of vi-
sual tokens. For compressed models, we investigate two
visual-token compression ratios, ρ2 ∈{4, 16}.
For the
4× compressed models, we adopt a pixel-unshuffle projec-
tor [7], which concatenates each 2 × 2 group of spatially
adjacent visual tokens along the channel dimension, fol-
lowed by projection into the LLM embedding space. For
the 16× compressed models, inspired by LLaVA-UHD v4
[9], we additionally insert a compressor after the 6-th ViT
layer. This intra-ViT module employs local window atten-
tion to aggregate neighboring visual information, followed
by 2 × 2 pixel-unshuffle and feature fusion to reduce the to-
ken count by a factor of four. Combined with the final pixel-
unshuffle projector, this achieves an overall 16× compres-
sion ratio. To accommodate different compression ratios,
we adopt ratio-dependent image padding strategies. Specif-
ically, the image height and width are padded to multiples of
14 pixels for the teacher, 28 pixels for students with ρ2 = 4,
and 56 pixels for students with ρ2 = 16.
The experiments are conducted on 8× NVIDIA A800
GPUs. The weights of loss functions are λ = 0.5, λD = 2,
λr = 1 and λv = 0.5. Based on the PVRL analysis in
Figure 1, we use ℓ∗= 12 and ℓ∗= 20 for Qwen3-0.6B and
Qwen3-4B, respectively. More implementation details are
shown in supplementary materials.
Training Datasets.
As shown in Table 1, we compare
distillation from same-data teachers, trained on the same
data as the students, and data-rich teachers, trained with
additional data. Before distillation, we prepare the mod-
els through the following stages. The ViT remains frozen
throughout.
In Stage 1 (pretraining), all models use 558K image-
caption pairs [24] to align visual representations with the
LLM embedding space.
We train the projector and any
learnable student compressor while freezing the LLM. Only
data-rich teachers undergo Stage 1.5, where we jointly train
the LLM and projector on detailed image descriptions from
LLaVA-ReCap-558K [26] to improve visual understanding.
In Stage 2 (visual instruction tuning), students and
same-data teachers use the LLaVA-NeXT 779K instruction-
tuning dataset [26]. Data-rich teachers use approximately
3.3M instruction-tuning samples following the LLaVA-
OneVision single-image recipe [22]. This stage updates the
LLM, projector, and any learnable student compressor.
For knowledge distillation, all methods use the LLaVA-
NeXT 779K instruction-tuning dataset and initialize the stu-
dent from its corresponding Stage-2 SFT checkpoint.
Baseline Methods.
To demonstrate the effectiveness of
DVTR-KD, we make fair comparisons with several rep-
resentative methods: MiniLLM [16], LLaVA-KD [4] and
EM-KD [11]. Note that LLaVA-KD cannot be directly ap-
plied to MLLMs with unbalanced visual tokens. We re-
implement it with a reconstructor to align the number of
visual tokens between teacher and student MLLMs, which
is one of the key contributions in our work.
Benchmarks.
We evaluate the effectiveness of our
method on 11 benchmarks.
They are categorized into
three groups based on different areas. (1) General Ques-
tion Answering: GQA [17], MME [12], RealWorldQA
(RWQA) [37]; (2) Comprehensive Knowledge Reasoning:
ScienceQA-IMG (SQA) [29], AI2D [18], MMMU [39],
MMStar [5]; (3) OCR and Chart Parsing: ChartQA [30],
DocVQA [31], TextVQA [33], and OCRBench [28]. To
compare the performance, we report the mean score across
all 11 benchmarks, dividing MME scores by 28 before av-
eraging.

5. Conclusion
This paper proposes DVTR-KD, a novel knowledge distil-
lation framework for transferring visual knowledge from
a teacher MLLM to a token-compressed student. To ad-
dress the mismatch in visual sequence lengths, DVTR-
KD reconstructs dense visual representations from com-
pressed student tokens, enabling token-wise alignment
with the complete teacher visual sequence.
We further
introduce intermediate-layer distillation at PVRL, iden-
tified as the layer most sensitive to visual information
removal.
Specifically, we replace the teacher’s visual
states at the input to PVRL with student-reconstructed
representations and supervise them through the remaining
teacher layers using response-level objectives. Comprehen-
sive experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of DVTR-
KD.

A. Geometry-aware reconstruction
We detail the reconstructor. Following the main text, Hv,S
denotes the student visual states and ˆHv,R their dense re-
construction; the layer index is omitted for brevity.
Image Geometry.
Teacher and student use different to-
ken grids and add different amounts of padding to the bot-
tom and right of the image. For example, in our experi-
ments, the patch size of ViT’s processor is 14 × 14, hence
the teacher’s image height and width are padded to multi-
ples of 14 pixels, whereas a student with ρ2 = 16 requires
multiples of 56 pixels. A 524 × 524 image is therefore
padded to 532 × 532 for the teacher and 560 × 560 for the
student, yielding 38 × 38 = 1,444 and 10 × 10 = 100
visual tokens, respectively. The actual token-count ratio is
thus Nv,T /Nv,S = 14.44, rather than exactly 16, because
padding rounds up each dimension separately. To align their
grids, we locate each teacher token center in image coordi-
nates and express it in student-grid coordinates. This uses
each model’s token-cell size rather than scaling the two full
padded grids to match.
Bilinear Upsampling.
At each mapped location, we in-
terpolate up to four neighboring student visual states. For
each neighboring student token, we calculate the fraction
of its area covered by the image itself, excluding padding.
We multiply its bilinear weight by this fraction, then nor-
malize the weights.
This gives one upsampled state per
teacher token and produces U = B(Hv,S).
In imple-
mentation, we bilinearly sample the features multiplied
by these fractions and the fractions themselves, then di-
vide the two results. The denominator is clamped below
at 10−6. Both sampling operations use zero padding and
align corners=False.
Algorithm 1 explains the same computation as a per-
token loop; the implementation is vectorized. The argument
image denotes the image itself, excluding padding. The
helper cell size divides each model’s padded image di-
mensions by its token-grid dimensions. Token indices start
at zero, and arithmetic on two-dimensional coordinates is
component-wise. The loops omit points outside the student
grid, equivalent to zero-padded sampling before renormal-
ization.

B. Experiment Details
PVRL Diagnosis.
We use 1,024 unique images drawn
from twelve traceable LLaVA-NeXT [26] sources, with 85-
86 images per source. Each sample is under a maximum
text length of 2,048. All models share the manifest and re-
tain their native preprocessing.
At each block independently, the diagnostic zeros the
value projections of all visual tokens, while preserving
queries, keys, the attention denominator, and residual states.
We compute the NLL difference between the original and
intervened models on the same response tokens, averaging
first over tokens within each sample and then equally over
samples.
Training Hyperparameters.
The training hyperparame-
ters are summarized in Table 6. During Stage-1, we freeze
the ViT and LLM, optimizing the projector with a learning
rate of 10−3 and the additional compressor for 16× students
with 10−4. Stage-1.5, Stage-2 and knowledge distillation
(KD) share a learning rate of 2 × 10−5. We use τ = 2 in
computing KL divergence and τ = 1 for cross entropy. All
stages are trained for one epoch using AdamW with cosine
learning rate decay and a 3% warmup ratio.
Evaluation Protocol.
Table 7 specifies the evaluation
splits, example counts, and metrics for benchmarks. All
eleven benchmarks use at most 512 generated tokens. The
43 multi-image questions in MMMU are converted to la-
beled image grids consistently across models.

Similarly, EM-KD [11]
introduces Vision-Language Affinity Distillation (VLAD),
which aligns the correlations between visual and textual to-

To investigate the effectiveness of relation distillation un-
der aggressive token compression, we reproduce LLaVA-
KD in our setting by reconstructing dense visual tokens
from the student’s final-layer outputs, enabling both visual
semantic and relation distillation.
As shown in Table 8,
adding Lvis to response supervision improves Avg11 from
51.0 to 51.2. However, further introducing Lrel reduces the
score to 50.6 under ρ2 = 16, even below the response-only
baseline.
One explanation is that aggressive compression makes it
difficult to preserve fine-grained relationships among visual
tokens. Although reconstruction restores spatial correspon-
dence with the teacher, it does not necessarily recover the
original relational structure. Enforcing such relationships
may therefore introduce additional constraints that interfere
with response prediction. Based on these observations, we
retain visual semantic distillation but omit relation-based
losses from our proposed DVTR-KD objective.
