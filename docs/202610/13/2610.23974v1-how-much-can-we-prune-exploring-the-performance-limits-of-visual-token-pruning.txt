HOW MUCH CAN WE PRUNE? EXPLORING THE PER-
FORMANCE LIMITS OF VISUAL TOKEN PRUNING
Janghoon Cho, Shubhankar Borse, Reza Pourreza
Juntae Lee, Jihwan Bang, Munawar Hayat, Fatih Porikli
Qualcomm AI Research∗
ABSTRACT
Visual token pruning is increasingly used to reduce the memory and inference
costs of vision-language models. Existing work has largely focused on design-
ing method-specific selectors and demonstrating incremental improvements over
prior methods at selected token budgets. It remains unclear whether the resulting
performance reflects an inherent limit of visual token pruning or the limitations
of current token-selection algorithms. We take a complementary perspective and
ask: how well can visual token pruning perform when offline search cost is not
the primary constraint? We formulate pruning for general image understanding
as selecting, under a fixed budget, one shared visual-token subset that supports a
broad distribution of questions about an image. We approximate this utility with
a finite auxiliary QA set generated by a frontier VLM and optimize a teacher-
forced answer-probability objective with the evaluated model frozen. Motivated
by the conceptual monotonicity and diminishing returns of adding visual evidence,
we construct nested subsets greedily and introduce QA mini-batch greedy (QA-
MBG), which estimates token marginal gains from randomly sampled QA com-
ponents at each step. Empirically, QA-MBG is both cheaper and more effective
than full greedy. With one frontier-VLM call and approximately 0.5 H100 GPU-
hours per image, it produces a nested subset trajectory covering keep ratios up to
0.3. Across Qwen3-VL-4B and Gemma 4 E4B on 900 images from nine bench-
marks, we find significant headroom over evaluated pruning baselines on held-out
QA and image-grounded long-form generation, while the searched subsets sub-
stantially narrow the gap to full-token inference.

INTRODUCTION
Vision-language models (VLMs) obtain strong visual understanding by converting an image into
hundreds or thousands of visual tokens and processing them together with text (Bai et al., 2025;
Wang et al., 2025).
These tokens substantially increase language-model prefill computation,
decoding-time attention cost, KV-cache memory, and end-to-end latency. Visual token pruning has
therefore become a major direction for efficient VLM inference. Recent methods score or consol-
idate tokens using attention, query relevance, feature similarity, redundancy, diversity, or learned
selection modules, often retaining only a small fraction of the original sequence (Chen et al., 2024a;
Alvar et al., 2025; Wen et al., 2025b; Yang et al., 2025).
Most work follows the same evaluation pattern: propose a new selection heuristic and demonstrate
an incremental accuracy–efficiency improvement over previous selectors at selected token budgets.
This competition improves deployable algorithms, but leaves a more fundamental question unan-
swered: how much performance can visual token pruning actually preserve at a fixed budget? A
gap from full-token inference conflates unavoidable information loss with recoverable selector error,
while small differences among current methods do not show that they are close to the best available
subset. Diagnostic studies further reveal strong sensitivity to evaluation protocol and cases in which
simple rules rival elaborate methods (Wen et al., 2025a; Peng et al., 2025; Liao et al., 2026). The
achievable performance frontier of visual token pruning remains unexplored.
We expose this frontier by relaxing the search-time compute constraint. Rather than designing an-
other inexpensive inference-time heuristic, we invest substantial offline computation to discover a

strong subset for each image. Every discovered subset is a feasible operating point at its retained-
token budget; a large, reproducible gap over existing selectors therefore directly measures algorith-
mic headroom. This changes the question from which current heuristic wins to how much room
remains for better pruning algorithms.
The key challenge is defining subset quality for general image understanding. A subset selected for
one known query can discard evidence needed by another. We instead seek one shared subset that
supports the distribution of questions that could reasonably be asked about an image, formalized as
expected question-answering utility under a cardinality constraint. Because this distribution is un-
available, we approximate it with a finite, diverse auxiliary QA set generated by a frontier VLM. The
evaluation questions and answers are never used to score candidate subsets in the main experiment;
after search, the same nested trajectory is reused for held-out benchmark questions and long-form
description prompts.
This formulation connects visual-token selection to classical information-gathering problems, where
adding observations yields monotone, diminishing-return utility and enables near-optimal greedy se-
lection (Krause et al., 2008). Frozen-VLM QA utility is only approximately monotone and submod-
ular, but it exhibits the same saturation pattern empirically. We therefore build a nested trajectory
by repeatedly adding the token with the largest estimated marginal gain. Full greedy is prohibitively
expensive because every step evaluates every remaining token against every auxiliary QA.
We introduce QA mini-batch greedy (QA-MBG), which samples a fresh mini-batch of auxiliary
questions at each step, evaluates all remaining token candidates on those components, and adds
the best token. This is the decomposable-objective counterpart of mini-batch submodular maxi-
mization (Schwartzman, 2024), and differs from stochastic greedy, which samples token candidates
while evaluating the full objective (Mirzasoleiman et al., 2015). QA-MBG reduces QA evaluations
and improves the irreversible sequence of greedy decisions: it raises held-out QA and long-form
performance over full greedy while producing more balanced gains across auxiliary questions. Can-
didate batching, prefix-KV reuse, and teacher-forced scoring make the offline search practical. One
frontier-VLM call and approximately 0.5 H100 GPU-hours per image produce a complete nested
trajectory through keep ratio 0.3.
We evaluate Qwen3-VL-4B and Gemma 4 E4B on 900 images and 2,226 held-out questions from
nine benchmarks. Across both model families, searched subsets establish a clear empirical frontier
above the evaluated pruning baselines and substantially narrow the gap to full-token inference. The
gains are especially pronounced on document, chart, infographic, and diagram understanding, and
transfer to image-grounded long-form generation despite search using a short-answer objective.
Our contributions are threefold:
• We formulate visual token pruning for general image understanding as selecting one shared
subset that maximizes expected QA utility under a fixed token budget, and use frontier-
generated auxiliary QAs to expose its empirical performance frontier.
• We introduce QA mini-batch greedy, which samples objective components rather than to-
ken candidates and improves both search cost and downstream subset quality relative to
full greedy and token-level stochastic greedy.
• Across two VLM families, nine benchmarks, held-out QA, and long-form generation, we
reveal substantial headroom over existing pruning methods, showing that current selectors
are far from exhausting what is achievable at the same visual-token budgets.

RELATED WORK
Visual token pruning in VLMs.
Visual token pruning reduces multimodal inference cost by re-
taining a subset of visual tokens before or within the language model. Existing selectors use multi-
stage dropping (Liu et al., 2024), attention or head importance (Zhu et al., 2026; Gao et al., 2026),
learned selection (Zhu et al., 2025), diversity (Alvar et al., 2025), redundancy (Wen et al., 2025b),
or combinations of relevance and contextual coverage (Pei et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025c; Oh &
Kim, 2026; Wang et al., 2026). Query-conditioned selection targets evidence for a particular ques-
tion, whereas query-agnostic selection seeks to retain information without access to that question;
SparseVILA explicitly distinguishes these settings (Khaki et al., 2025). Adaptive methods addition-
ally vary the budget with the input, as in GlimpsePrune, PromPrune, and OccamToken (Zeng et al.,
2026; Lee et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026). These approaches primarily target efficient, deployable se-
lection. Our focus is on the performance achievable by a shared image-token subset at a specified

Oracle analyses and search-based selection.
SparseVILA constructs a query-aware greedy ora-
cle that selects visual tokens to maximize agreement with the unpruned model’s responses, and uses
its deterioration across subsequent conversation rounds to illustrate the limitations of irreversible
query-dependent pruning (Khaki et al., 2025). Our setting instead uses diverse auxiliary QA pairs
to search for one subset intended to support multiple evaluation questions. EvoComp performs evo-
lutionary search for visual-token masks that minimize response loss, constrains selection through
semantic grouping, and uses the resulting masks to supervise a learned compressor (Song et al.,
2026). We share the use of answer-based search signals, but study a different target: the achievable
performance of a shared multi-QA subset and the effect of search granularity on that performance,
rather than compressor training. Related oracle analyses include the label-conditioned Grad-CAM
oracle in video classification studied by Principles of Visual Tokens (Hao et al., 2025). Our con-
tribution therefore lies in combining shared-subset exploration, evaluation with target-question an-
notations withheld from candidate scoring, and an analysis of QA mini-batch greedy, rather than in
introducing answer-guided token search itself.
Subset optimization.
Classical stochastic greedy reduces oracle evaluations in cardinality-
constrained subset selection by sampling candidate elements at each iteration (Mirzasoleiman
et al., 2015). Mini-batch submodular maximization instead considers a decomposable objective,

auxiliary-QA objective has the same decomposition across questions: QA-MBG samples QA com-
ponents while evaluating every remaining visual-token candidate. This directly separates QA-MBG
from token-candidate stochastic greedy and provides the closest combinatorial-optimization prece-
dent for our search rule. Because frozen-VLM answer probability is only approximately monotone
and submodular, its existing approximation guarantee does not directly transfer; we therefore evalu-
ate both the full auxiliary objective and downstream performance of the resulting trajectories.

EXPERIMENTS
4.1
EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
Evaluation sets.
We use two cohorts from nine benchmarks: AI2D (Kembhavi et al., 2016),
ChartQA (Masry et al., 2022), DocVQA (Mathew et al., 2021), InfographicVQA (Mathew et al.,
2022), MME (Fu et al., 2025), MMStar (Chen et al., 2024b), RealWorldQA (xAI, 2024), TextVQA
(Singh et al., 2019), and V∗Bench (Wu & Xie, 2024). The main set contains 100 images per bench-
mark (9 × 100: 900 images, 2,226 actual QAs); the small set contains 20 per benchmark (9 × 20:
180 images, 392 actual QAs). Actual-QA evaluation uses all associated benchmark questions. These
annotations are separate from the generated auxiliary QA pool, although their semantic content can
overlap. Long-form evaluation uses two description prompts per image.
Models.
We evaluate Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct (Bai et al., 2025) and Gemma 4 E4B-it (Gemma
Team, 2026), keeping their parameters frozen. Our search selects visual tokens after visual encoding
and projection, before language-model processing. Each selected subset is shared across evaluation
questions about the same image. We use an image-processor cap of 1M pixels for Qwen3-VL and
a maximum of 1,120 visual soft tokens for Gemma 4. Architecture-specific integration and baseline
adaptations are detailed in Appendix A.
Auxiliary QA generation.
We use GPT-5.6-SOL to generate image-grounded auxiliary question–
answer pairs; the generation protocol, prompts, validation, and provenance are documented in Ap-
pendix C. Because generated auxiliary QAs can semantically overlap with actual QAs, we com-
pare the original search with a search after removing overlapping auxiliary QAs. We also evaluate
dataset-agnostic generation and repeat QA generation with Opus 5 to test sensitivity to the genera-
tion protocol and frontier model. The resulting 30,273 auxiliary QAs average 33.64 per image. A
selected trajectory is held fixed during actual-QA and long-form evaluation, without re-searching
for each prompt.
Search and baselines.
The main search configuration uses QA mini-batch greedy with QA sam-
pling fraction α = 0.2, evaluating all remaining token candidates at each step. It optimizes the
length-normalized geometric-mean answer-probability objective in Eq. (6), averaged over the sam-
pled auxiliary QA pairs. We first interpret headroom against question-independent post-vision subset
selectors, including Uniform, FLoC, and DivPrune, which also retain a shared subset of unchanged
visual embeddings. The broader comparison includes feature merging, query-aware selection, and
decoder-internal pruning. These methods can access different selection signals, change retained fea-
tures, or process all tokens in early decoder layers. Prior pruning methods do not share a common
model backbone, and LLaVA—widely used in earlier work—is an older architecture that is not rep-
resentative of the contemporary VLMs evaluated here. We therefore implement architecture-specific
adaptations for Qwen3-VL and Gemma 4; Appendix A details their pruning locations and schedules.
Full-token inference provides an uncompressed reference.
Choice of QA mini-batch fraction.
The QA mini-batch fraction α is the only search hyperpa-
rameter of our method. We set α = 0.2 empirically using the small-set experiments; Section 4.3
describes the selection analysis.
Budgets and reporting.
We report visual-token keeping ratios up to 0.30. Subsets at different bud-
gets are prefixes of one nested search trajectory for each image. The top row of Figure 2 expresses
native benchmark performance relative to the corresponding full-token model, whose reference level
is 100%. The searched trajectories provide denser budget coverage than the fixed baseline operating
points. Comparisons at a given retained-token budget measure achievable quality; equal final to-
ken counts do not imply equal computational cost across pre-LLM and progressive decoder-internal
methods. Search execution and measured runtime are reported in Appendix B.

4.2
MAIN RESULTS
Achievable headroom across two model families.
Figure 2(a,b) shows that QA-guided subset
search achieves higher aggregate native benchmark performance than the plotted baselines at com-
parable token budgets for both Qwen3-VL and Gemma 4. The separation is particularly pronounced
at low and moderate keeping ratios and remains visible toward 0.30. Thus the performance lost by
existing selectors is not determined by the token budget alone: stronger retained subsets are available
under the same model and budget constraints.
Performance as the budget increases.
The explored curves rise rapidly at small budgets and then
approach a plateau closer to the full-token reference. The baselines also improve with additional
tokens, narrowing the gap at larger budgets, but do not close it over the displayed range. Notably,
this headroom also persists against query-aware pruning methods that select tokens using the eval-
uation question itself, even though our search produces one shared subset for all questions about an
image. Observing this pattern in both model families supports the usefulness of shared-subset ex-
ploration beyond a single VLM architecture. The gap to direct shared-subset selectors demonstrates
achievable quality beyond those selection rules under the auxiliary-supervision protocol. Compar-
isons with merging and decoder-internal adapters broaden the empirical context, while retaining the
differences in representation and computation described above.
Transfer to long-form descriptions.
Figure 2(c,d) shows that the same searched representations
also improve long-form quality over the plotted compression baselines at all six evaluated budgets
for both models. These prompts request broad descriptions rather than the short answers used by
the search objective. The advantage therefore extends beyond the native short-answer evaluation.
For Qwen3-VL, QA-MBG retains a clear margin over the strongest plotted baseline throughout the
range, while its gap to full-token descriptions narrows as the budget increases. Averaged over the

4.3
ANALYSIS
We examine the choice of search strategy, its effect on auxiliary-objective optimization, sensitiv-
ity to benchmark conditioning and direct QA overlap, the size of the supervision pool, and the
auxiliary-QA generator. These analyses complement the paired uncertainty estimates and long-form
evaluation above.
QA mini-batching versus full greedy.
Figure 3 compares QA batch sizes on the small set. Green
shading and error bars show the α = 0.2 three-seed min–max ranges, not confidence intervals;
the yellow envelope spans baseline methods rather than repeated runs. Other settings are single
runs (Appendix I.3). At keep ratio 0.20, α = 0.2 achieves mean relative actual-QA performance of
93.44% across three seeds (range 91.90–95.95%), versus 75.56% for full greedy and 89.96% for the
reported α = 0.5 run. Long-form quality is 2.989 (range 2.939–3.031), versus 1.869 for full greedy
and 2.903 for α = 0.5. All three α = 0.2 seeds exceed full greedy at the six standard budgets in both
evaluations, so the advantage is not confined to the original sampling trajectory. The larger fraction
0.5 yields lower long-form quality than every 0.2 seed at all six budgets despite evaluating more
QA components per candidate. This supports 0.2 as a practical setting, while 0.1 and one QA per
step remain competitive. The reversal—QA-MBG succeeding where full greedy underperforms—is
particularly striking. We hypothesize that full greedy repeatedly fits the same finite generated-QA
objective, whereas resampling QA components changes the objective estimate at every step and acts
as a regularizer against overfitting to pool-specific artifacts. The following QA-sampling-dynamics
analysis probes this hypothesis. Appendix I gives complete results, including the supplementary
token-level control and seed protocol.
QA sampling dynamics.
On the small set, QA sampling improves the lower tail of generated-
QA probabilities compared with full greedy. At keeping ratio 0.30, α = 0.5 achieves the highest
overall mean (0.7203 versus 0.6790 for full greedy) and bottom-20% mean (0.3566 versus 0.2756)
among the tested fractions. The lower tail is recomputed at each step; it is not a fixed group of
8


Preprint
initially difficult questions. At α = 0.2, the overall mean is similar to full greedy while the lower
tail improves. Step-level diagnostics show focused gains on sampled QAs alongside temporary
regressions on unsampled QAs, consistent with resampling redistributing improvements across the
search trajectory. These diagnostics characterize optimization of the generated-QA objective; they
do not imply a monotonic benefit from smaller fractions. Downstream transfer is assessed separately
using actual QAs and long-form descriptions. Appendix H provides definitions, trajectories, and
numerical summaries.
Controlling the QA distribution.
When the target question distribution is known, as in
benchmark-specific QA generation, auxiliary QAs can align with the actual-QA evaluation and in-
troduce potential question-level leakage. We use two controls to separate this effect from headroom
attributable to token selection. First, dataset-agnostic generation withholds benchmark names, task
profiles, dataset-specific instructions, and same-benchmark examples while retaining the image-
grounding and diversity requirements. At keep ratio 0.20, it achieves 92.79% full-token-relative
performance, compared with 96.18% for the original setting and 83.47% for the strongest baseline.
Its margin over the strongest baseline remains positive at all six budgets, showing that benchmark
information helps but is not required for the observed headroom.
For the overlap control, after generating the auxiliary QAs, we use a text-only GPT-5.6-Luna judge
to compare generated and actual QA pairs and filter generated QAs that ask about semantically
equivalent visual facts. We then re-run subset search with the filtered generated-QA pool. At keep
ratio 0.20, filtered search reaches 91.36% full-token-relative performance, compared with 96.18%
without filtering and 83.47% for the strongest baseline. The filtered trajectory remains above the
strongest baseline at all six budgets. Actual-QA annotations are used only to define which gener-
ated QAs are filtered; they never score candidate subsets. Because filtering also removes potentially
useful visual supervision, the performance drop does not measure leakage alone. Together, the con-
trols show that task conditioning and semantic overlap strengthen, but do not explain, the measured
headroom. Appendices K and L provide protocols and full results.
Number of generated QAs.
On the small set, we vary the search pool using nested 25%, 50%,
75%, and 100% subsets. At keep ratio 0.20, relative performance increases from 86.33% with one
quarter of the pool to 96.18% with the complete pool; using three quarters reaches 93.87%. Larger
pools generally improve aggregate performance, with benchmark-dependent and occasionally non-
monotonic effects. This measures sensitivity to the amount of auxiliary supervision. The per-step
QA fraction remains 0.2, so increasing the pool also increases the sampled QA count and search
compute; it does not isolate diversity at fixed compute. Appendix F provides the construction, exact
counts, and results.
Auxiliary-QA generator.
Replacing GPT-5.6-SOL with Opus 5 on the small set produces a
closely matched actual-QA curve: the six-budget mean relative performance is 93.14% versus
91.69%, with a mean absolute pointwise difference of 2.30 points. Opus-generated subsets also
exceed the best plotted long-form baseline at each budget by 0.56 judge-score points on average
(3.21 versus 2.65). Appendix M gives the per-budget results.
Qualitative examples.
Appendix N presents one selected favorable example from each bench-
mark, showing retained visual tokens, actual-QA responses, and long-form descriptions for the same
image. These cases illustrate preserved evidence and differences in description quality; aggregate
comparisons use the full evaluation cohorts above.
5
CONCLUSION
We ask how much performance visual token pruning can preserve when offline search cost is re-
laxed. We formulate general-understanding pruning as selecting one image-specific subset shared
across questions, approximate its utility with frontier-generated auxiliary QAs, and optimize it with
QA mini-batch greedy. Across Qwen3-VL and Gemma 4, the resulting trajectories reveal substan-
tial headroom over evaluated pruning baselines on both actual QA and long-form descriptions, while
narrowing the gap to full-token inference. On the small set, QA mini-batching also outperforms full
greedy with fewer QA evaluations; its step-wise dynamics are consistent with a regularizing ef-
fect from resampling objective components. The headroom persists when benchmark information
is withheld, semantically overlapping generated QAs are filtered, and the auxiliary-QA generator
9


Preprint
is changed. It also remains with smaller generated-QA pools, although performance generally im-
proves as more auxiliary QAs are used.
These results establish a strong empirical frontier across the evaluated models and supervision set-
tings. The consistent gaps show that a substantial portion of current pruning loss is recoverable
selector error rather than inevitable information loss from the token budget. Visual token prun-
ing can therefore perform substantially better than current heuristics, and the searched trajectories
provide concrete targets for learning or designing the next generation of efficient selectors.

Decoder-internal methods begin prefill with all visual tokens and compact the sequence at one or

and the Gemma decoder-internal adapters require batch size one.
For Gemma, compaction jointly updates hidden states, per-layer inputs, absolute positions, full/s-
liding masks, dynamic caches, shared KV state, and retained-position metadata used during genera-
tion. Masks are rebuilt from compacted absolute positions to preserve hybrid full/sliding attention.
Attention-ranking stages use full-attention layers. For progressive adapters, the cumulative targets
are defined relative to the original visual-token count and calibrated to reach the requested final ratio;

B
EFFICIENT EXECUTION OF SUBSET SEARCH
The Qwen3-VL implementation combines cached visual inputs, candidate batching, prefix KV
reuse across QAs, and teacher-forced scoring. These execution choices are independent of the QA-
sampling policy.
B.1
CACHING, BATCHING, AND TEACHER FORCING
Reusable inputs.
Image processing, tokenization, and pre-LLM tensors are cached for the current
image. Candidate construction gathers visual embeddings, masks, multimodal positions, and Deep-
18


Preprint
Stack features from these tensors, avoiding repeated vision-encoder and merger execution. This
input cache is distinct from the decoder KV cache.
Candidate batching.
At step t, candidates are the subsets St ∪{v} for remaining tokens v. For a
fixed QA, every candidate has the same sequence length, so batches of up to K candidates require
no padding. The implementation compacts embeddings, masks, positions, and DeepStack features
consistently. We use K = 320 in the tuned runtime measurement and split batches if GPU memory
is insufficient.
Teacher-forced objective and selective logits.
Candidates use the reference-answer score in
Eq. (6). Teacher forcing computes all answer positions in one causal pass, and the LM head is eval-
uated only at required positions. No autoregressive answer-generation loop is used during search;
score reductions use FP32.
Prefix KV reuse across QAs.
For each candidate, the system-and-image prefix through the fi-
nal <|vision end|> marker is shared by all sampled QAs. A batched prefix pass creates an
independent KV state for each candidate. Each question/reference-answer suffix reuses that state,
restoring the cache to the prefix boundary before the next QA. Prefix identity, positions, and masks
are checked, and DeepStack features are consumed only in the prefix pass. Reuse is across QAs for
a fixed candidate batch; different subsets, candidate batches, and greedy steps require new prefix
states.

AUXILIARY QA GENERATION
C.1
GENERATION PROTOCOL
GPT-5.6-SOL generates auxiliary QAs from the target image, a benchmark-specific profile, and
nine text-only QA examples from other images of that benchmark. Examples follow manifest order,
excluding the target image ID. The prompt requests about 32 concise, image-grounded QAs (prefer-
ably 28–36), emphasizing independent facts and peripheral evidence, with at most two substantial
paraphrases per fact. Answers normally contain 1–12 words. The exact template is in Appendix C.3.
Image and generation settings.
Images are converted to RGB JPEG, initially at quality 95, with
compression or downscaling when needed to meet the API payload limit. Requests use JSON output
and a 16,000-token cap. Saved responses report temperature 1.0 and top-p = 0.98; the client does
not override these settings.
Validation and overlap policy.
We require nonempty questions and answers, normalize whites-
pace, and remove duplicate normalized questions. Questions longer than 300 characters and answers
longer than 180 characters or 24 words are rejected, as are Unanswerable answers for the evalu-
ated benchmarks. The requested count is a soft target.
The main pool is not filtered against actual QAs. Target-image evaluation annotations are absent
from the generation prompt and candidate scoring, although they are used separately for overlap
diagnostics. Thus prompt exclusion does not imply semantic non-overlap; Appendix K describes
the filtering control.

J.4
INTERPRETATION AND LIMITATIONS
Both QA-MBG and top-k relax the requirement that every addition maximize immediate all-QA
improvement. Sampling changes candidate ranking and hence the greedy path; it does not estimate
the all-QA marginal more accurately than full evaluation. Although QA-MBG is unbiased for a fixed
candidate (Eq. 9), maximizing that estimate need not preserve the full-greedy action. Completed
runs show that alternative paths can reach better final auxiliary scores and actual-QA performance;
the same-state analysis characterizes the local trade-offs these rules permit.
Top-k is complementary evidence for focused updates, not proof of QA-MBG’s exact mechanism.
The diagnostics do not establish that a particular regression or forgone improvement causes later
success, or that sampling escapes a specific greedy trap. Replay covers one QA-MBG seed through
ratio 0.15, without following alternative actions forward. The downstream comparison covers one
backbone and the small set, with single-run full-greedy/top-k controls and no top-k long-form eval-
uation. We interpret the evidence as a characterization of search behavior, not a causal proof or a
universally optimal QA fraction.

PROTOCOL AND CONTROLLED PROMPT CHANGES
We compare benchmark-conditioned and dataset-agnostic auxiliary QA generation with Qwen3-VL

MBG with α = 0.2 and all remaining token candidates. The conditioned pool is shared with the
count and removal controls. The dataset-agnostic prompt is reproduced below.
Dataset-agnostic generation removes every explicit benchmark-specific input: the dataset name,
benchmark-character description, dataset-specific question patterns and answer-style instructions,
avoidance rules, and few-shot QAs from other images of that benchmark. It replaces these with a
generic inventory of visual evidence types, including entities, attributes, readable text and values,
counting, spatial relationships, structure, comparisons, and short visual reasoning. Actual evaluation
questions and answers are absent from both generation prompts.
The grounding, answer format, and diversity requirements remain fixed: questions must require in-
specting the image, answers must be supported by visible evidence, unsupported or ambiguous ques-
tions are excluded, independent facts are preferred over paraphrases, and at most two substantially
different questions may target one fact. Both prompts request about 32 short-answer QAs, prefer-
ably 28–36, with a JSON-only output schema. The target is soft, allowing fewer questions when
warranted by the image. The resulting pools contain 6,062 conditioned and 6,024 dataset-agnostic
QAs (means 33.68 and 33.47 per image). Thus the requested count and format are controlled, but
the realized per-image counts and QA content need not match exactly. This is an ablation of the
complete benchmark-conditioning package, not of few-shot examples alone or of equal-count QA
pools.

ROBUSTNESS TO THE AUXILIARY-QA GENERATOR
We test whether the searched frontier depends on the frontier VLM used to generate auxiliary QAs.
On the Qwen3-VL small set, we replace GPT-5.6-SOL with Opus 5 while keeping the generation
prompt, QA-MBG search configuration (α = 0.2), probability objective, evaluation images, and
downstream protocols fixed. Figure 17 compares the resulting trajectory with GPT-5.6 generation
and the evaluated pruning baselines.
The generator change preserves the main result. Across the six reported budgets, the Opus 5 and
GPT-5.6 trajectories differ by only 2.30 relative-performance points on average, and both exceed
the strongest pruning baseline at every budget. Opus 5 is higher at five of the six checkpoints,
reaching 99.21% of full-token actual-QA performance at keep ratio 0.30. Its subsets also remain the
strongest compressed representation in the long-form evaluation at every checkpoint, with overall
judge scores increasing from 2.76 at 0.05 to 3.43 at 0.30. Thus the observed headroom and transfer
to open-ended descriptions are not specific to GPT-5.6 auxiliary QAs.
