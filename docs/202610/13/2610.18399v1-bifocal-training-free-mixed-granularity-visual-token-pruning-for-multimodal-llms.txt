Abstract
Visual token pruning for multimodal large language models (MLLMs) appears highly effective, with
existing methods retaining over 95% of unpruned performance on common VQA benchmarks using
a quarter of the visual tokens or fewer. However, our extensive evaluation of ten methods across
three MLLM families and five ability groups reveals a critical gap: strong general performance
often comes at the cost of severe degradation on fine-grained tasks such as OCR and visual
grounding. We trace these failures to two limitations of the prevailing paradigm of retaining the
highest-ranked native patches. A single importance ranking overlooks the complementary evidence
required by different tasks, while sparse patch selection disrupts both image content and spatial
integrity. To address these limitations, we propose Bifocal, a training-free, two-stage pruning
method built on a mixed-granularity framework. It allocates separate budgets to query-focused
and image-intrinsic evidence, complemented by a pooled coarse representation of the entire image.
Before the LLM, Bifocal matches question words to visual regions through a soft conjunction
balanced against norm-debiased image saliency, while selecting image-intrinsic evidence to maximize
representational coverage. Inside the LLM, it further prunes tokens using density-adjusted text-
to-image attention. With roughly a quarter of the original visual tokens, Bifocal is the only
evaluated method to retain over 90% of unpruned performance in every ability group across all
three models, and ranks first on average in all nine model–budget settings. The code is available
at: https://github.com/EIT-NLP/Bifocal
Visual token pruning has attracted considerable attention for its ability to improve inference
efficiency in multimodal large language models (MLLMs) [3, 8, 17, 37, 47, 48]. Existing methods
report impressive results, often retaining about 95% of the unpruned performance on common
VQA benchmarks with a quarter of the visual tokens or fewer. Such results have fostered a
widespread view that visual tokens can already be effectively pruned with minimal performance
loss.This optimism, however, does not survive a broader evaluation. We conduct an extensive
evaluation of ten representative pruning methods on the widely used LLaVA-1.5-7B [23] under
three layer-averaged visual token budgets. The evaluation spans 14 benchmarks in five ability
groups: general VQA, reasoning, OCR and document understanding, object hallucination, and
visual grounding. Our evaluation reveals a starkly different picture. General VQA proves a
lenient test, since even random selection or plain pooling retains most of its performance. On
fine-grained tasks, by contrast, methods with near-lossless general performance degrade severely
or even collapse. Figure 1(a) illustrates this striking contrast on LLaVA-1.5-7B: with a third of
the visual tokens, HiPrune [24], EADP [43], and SID [10] retain 93.9%–98.1% of the unpruned
general performance, yet only 9.5%–14.0% of the grounding performance. OCR erodes as well:
the OCRBench score of Nüwa [15] falls from 314 to 152 with 11% of the tokens. Related

We trace these failures to the paradigm shared by existing training-free methods: score each
visual token by an importance estimated from the image [1, 9, 53, 57], the question [14, 20, 46, 58],
or both, and keep the top-ranked native patches. Our analysis across 20 benchmarks reveals two
limitations of this paradigm.
First, a single ranking makes the retained evidence homogeneous. However many cues
a score combines, all tokens compete in one ordering, so the budget flows to whichever kind of
evidence the score favours. Yet different tasks need different evidence. Under the same budget,
image-driven and question-driven selections retain largely distinct visual regions: OCR tasks
favour the former, while visual grounding tasks favour the latter. Fortunately, simply splitting
the budget evenly between the two already achieves performance close to the upper envelope of
either selection alone across tasks.
Second, subset-based pruning compromises the dual integrity of the visual represen-
tation. As a subset of the original visual sequence, the retained patch set is spatially sparse
and irregularly distributed, while part of the visual content is discarded. This disruption of
representational integrity, rather than inaccurate token selection, limits fine-grained task perfor-
mance. Even keeping exactly the patches on the ground-truth object yields only 39.1 Acc@0.5 on
RefCOCO testA. In contrast, allocating part of the budget to pooled tokens distributed across
the image raises the accuracy to 63.0, close to the unpruned 64.5.
Since both limitations concern what the retained set contains, a better scoring function alone
cannot remove them. We therefore ask: what visual evidence should be preserved under
a reduced token budget to support diverse tasks?
We answer with a training-free
mixed-granularity visual token pruning framework that allocates the limited budget to three
complementary token sets at two granularities. For the first limitation, two fine-grained evidence
sets are kept as native patches under separate budgets: query-focused evidence preserves the
details the question refers to, and image-intrinsic evidence preserves what best represents the
rest of the image. Evidence composition thus becomes an explicit allocation rather than a by-
product of one ordering.For the second, a coarse context of pooled tokens covers the whole image,
summarising the discarded content and restoring an image-wide layout around the scattered fine
tokens. Plugging existing methods into this framework improves their grounding accuracy by 9.7

We further propose Bifocal, a training-free two-stage visual token pruning method. In Stage 1,
before the LLM, Bifocal applies the mixed-granularity framework with dedicated selection
strategies for the two fine-grained evidence sets. For query-focused evidence, we first derive an
image saliency map by removing the component of CLS attention explained by patch norms.
Spatially smoothed word saliency maps are then weighted by their spatial correlation with this
map and combined through a weighted geometric mean, forming a soft and that favours patches
jointly supported by informative words. For image-intrinsic evidence, we greedily select patches
that maximise the marginal gain in attention-weighted representational coverage of the image,
given the tokens already retained. Finally, coarse context is constructed by pooling the remaining
patches within spatial blocks, completing the mixed-granularity representation. In Stage 2, inside
the LLM, Bifocal further removes redundant visual tokens using text-to-image attention adjusted
by local token density.
In our evaluation, Bifocal is the only method that consistently retains over 90% of the unpruned
performance across all five ability groups on all three models with roughly a quarter of the visual
tokens. In contrast, every baseline falls below 90% in at least one group, typically grounding or
OCR. On LLaVA-1.5-7B with a layer-averaged budget of 128 visual tokens, Bifocal’s weakest
group, OCR, still retains 92.4%, while the second-best method on grounding retains only 86.3%
on OCR. On Qwen2.5-VL-7B with 25% of the visual tokens, the strongest baseline retains
89.2% on OCR and 85.4% on grounding, compared with 92.3% and 90.2% for Bifocal. On
LLaVA-NeXT-7B with a layer-averaged budget of approximately 640 visual tokens, no baseline
retains more than 73% of the unpruned grounding performance, whereas Bifocal retains 90.7%.
Across all nine model–budget settings, Bifocal also ranks first in both general and grounding
averages.
Our contributions are threefold. (1) Diagnosis. Motivated by the lopsided performance of
existing pruners, we examine 20 benchmarks under a small token budget. First, image- and
question-driven visual evidence are complementary across tasks, and explicitly allocating the
budget between them improves robustness. Second, mean pooling neatly restores both the
content and positional integrity broken by pruning. (2) Framework. We propose a mixed-
granularity framework that combines separately budgeted query-focused and image-intrinsic
evidence with pooled coarse context, and that also improves existing methods. (3) Method.
We propose Bifocal, which instantiates the framework with dedicated selection rules. Bifocal
achieves state-of-the-art averages in all nine model–budget settings and, with roughly a quarter
of the visual tokens, retains over 90% of the unpruned performance in every ability group.
1 Related Work
Text-Guided Visual Token Pruning.
Within an LLM, textual hidden states progressively
integrate linguistic context and visual information through multiple layers of causal self-attention.
A simple and computationally efficient strategy is therefore to use the final text token as a
proxy for the full instruction and estimate visual token importance from its attention to visual
tokens [3, 7, 51]. Building on this idea, subsequent methods select or aggregate multiple text
tokens to extract task-relevant textual cues for visual token pruning [25, 38, 58]. Beyond directly
reusing existing textual hidden states, some studies explicitly construct or learn text-conditioned
representations for visual token pruning. IVTP [14] constructs a pseudo-CLS token that is
semantically associated with the current instruction and uses it as a reference for visual token
selection. LVPruning [42] introduces learnable cross-attention modules to explicitly fuse textual
and visual representations and predict visual token retention decisions.
Before visual features enter the LLM, instruction embeddings have not undergone deep contextual-
ization or extensive interaction with the current visual input, although pretrained vision encoders
3

and modality projectors already provide a certain degree of cross-modal semantic alignment.
Accordingly, methods such as TRIM [40] exploit embedding-level text–vision relevance by measur-
ing the similarity between CLIP text embeddings and visual patch embeddings, while subsequent
approaches apply pooling or aggregation to derive text–vision relevance scores [12, 26, 56]. Rather
than treating the entire instruction as a single unit, CRISP [20] extracts keywords and compares
them with projected visual tokens, whereas ICCTP [55] uses instruction nouns as visual-semantic
anchors for cross-modal clustering and visual token compression.
Image-Guided Visual Token Pruning.
Unlike methods conditioned on the current textual
instruction, image-guided approaches compress visual tokens solely according to intrinsic visual
cues, such as saliency, informativeness, similarity, and redundancy. One line of work estimates
visual token saliency from the attention maps produced by the vision encoder. A common
strategy is to use the attention from the CLS token to image patches to identify visually important
tokens [36]. HiPrune [24] further exploits distinct attention patterns across different vision-encoder
layers to capture object-level and global visual information, while EntropyPrune [44] measures
visual token informativeness from an information-theoretic perspective using representation
entropy.
Another line of work focuses on similarity and redundancy among visual tokens,
reducing repeated information by retaining representative or diverse visual features [1, 53].
VisPruner [57] combines these two types of visual cues by first selecting salient tokens according
to visual attention and then removing redundant tokens based on feature similarity.
Joint Text–Image-Guided Visual Token Pruning.
Some approaches jointly exploit textual
and visual cues for visual token compression. Multi-stage pruning strategies naturally fall into
this category because they use complementary guidance signals at different compression stages.
MustDrop [25] first removes redundant tokens according to visual similarity during visual encoding
and subsequently leverages textual semantics to further filter visual information. DUET-VLM [38]
first performs redundancy-aware merging based on correlations among visual tokens and then
applies progressive pruning within the LLM using text-to-vision attention. Another line of work
combines textual and visual signals within a unified selection process to estimate visual token
importance. AdaV [12] and CrisPrune [29] both use ViT CLS–patch self-attention to characterize
visual saliency and the cosine similarity between visual tokens and text embeddings to estimate
textual relevance. AdaV aggregates textual relevance using the maximum similarity, whereas
CrisPrune further combines the two signals into a token-quality score. CoIn [6] measures intrinsic
visual saliency using the ℓ2 norm of visual token embeddings and evaluates textual relevance
through the cosine similarity between visual tokens and the mean-pooled text embedding. The
two normalized scores are then combined to obtain an overall informativeness score.
2 Mixed-Granularity Pruning Framework
2.1 Information: Two Sources
Existing training-free pruning methods often exhibit pronounced task-wise biases [4, 7]. We
therefore turn the question around: what visual evidence does an LLM actually need to
solve different tasks?
At inference time, a training-free pruning method has access to only two sources of information:
the image itself and the question. We therefore examine how token selections driven by each
source behave under the same budget. We fix the token budget at one quarter of the visual
tokens and turn each source into a route with a common, simple scoring rule: the image route
ranks patches by CLS attention, and the text route ranks them by their cosine similarity to the
mean word embedding of the question.

2.2 Structure: Two Integrities
Even after combining the two routes, grounding remains the least recovered capability. Is the
remaining gap simply a matter of inaccurate token selection? Figure 3 suggests otherwise. In
③, we push selection to the extreme and retain only the patches on the ground-truth target,
yet localisation falls well below the two-route selection in ②. Conventional visual token pruning
methods, including our two routes, share the same compression paradigm: retaining a subset of
native patches from the full visual sequence. This subset-based compression breaks the
integrity of the original visual sequence in two ways: positional integrity and content
integrity.
Positional integrity.
Subset pruning leaves tokens at only a sparse and irregular set of
positions, breaking the global positional organization of the original visual sequence. Starting
from this extreme strategy in ③, we progressively restore coverage beyond the target—first
around its boundary (④), then across the full image with random or lattice layouts (⑤, ⑥).
Localisation improves substantially as the retained tokens recover a more complete image-wide
distribution.
Content integrity.
token pruning also incurs an unavoidable loss of content. To examine this
effect, we keep the image-wide positional layout fixed and vary only what the distributed tokens
contain. In ⑧, all distributed tokens carry the same image-wide average, yet localisation improves
over the native lattice in ⑥. Replacing this global summary with region-specific summaries (⑨)
improves performance further. Discarded content need not be preserved at its original resolution,
but retaining a low-resolution account of it is important for content integrity.
A layer of pooled coarse tokens addresses both deficiencies at once: each coarse token summarizes
content that would otherwise be discarded, while together they restore an even positional layout

design: fine tokens preserve task-relevant native detail, while coarse tokens recover, at a lower
resolution, the content and positional integrity lost through subset selection. Appendix B

3 Methodology
Figure 4 gives an overview of the design of Bifocal. We describe query-focused evidence in §3.1,
image-intrinsic evidence in §3.2, coarse context in §3.3, and Stage 2 in §3.4.
3.1 Query-Focused Evidence
Query-focused evidence assigns each patch a question-dependent score Ij and retains the top-K
patches as the evidence the question needs. Existing methods typically compress the question
into a single vector and score each patch by its cosine similarity to it. Whether the words are
pooled by a max or a mean, a strong match to one word can stand in for the absence of another,
an or-like rule under which a “red shirt” is no different from a red fire hydrant elsewhere in the
image. Since no model reads the question as a sentence before the LLM, we treat it as a set of
words and model two ingredients explicitly: word weighting and word conjunction. For weighting,
we invite the image back to sit on the jury alongside the question: a word earns a louder voice
when its saliency map agrees with what the image itself finds salient. For conjunction, we combine
the words with a geometric mean, a soft and that favours patches supported by all relevant
words jointly.

3.4 Stage 2: Pruning inside the LLM
Stage 1 decides which visual evidence enters the LLM, yet the visual tokens remain highly
redundant inside it [3]. By then the causal attention has also let them absorb the question,
so the text-to-image attention reliably reflects which visual information the answer still needs.
Following FastV, at layer L we score each visual token by the attention tj it receives from the
last text token and keep the top-K.
We make one adjustment, with the integrity of the retained set in mind. After Stage 1 the grid is
sparse, and this attention often peaks on isolated survivors whose neighbours have been pruned.
We therefore weight each score by a local-density prior:

analysis (Appendix H) and visualisations (§4.4).
4.1 Experimental Setup
Models and benchmarks.
We report main results on LLaVA-1.5-7B and verify on LLaVA-
NeXT-7B and Qwen2.5-VL-7B. The benchmarks are VQAv2 [11], GQA [16], MMBench [27],
OK-VQA [32], ScienceQA-IMG [30], AI2D [19], TextVQA [39], OCRBench [28], DocVQA [34],
ChartQA [33], POPE [21], RefCOCO, RefCOCO+ [54] and RefCOCOg [31].
Baselines.
We select representative visual-token pruning methods as baselines, including
FastV [3], PDrop [51], VisionZip [53], and PruMerge+ [36]. We further reproduce recent publicly
available representative works and conduct fair comparisons with open-source methods that
provide favorable reproducibility, including DART [45], Nüwa [15], HiPrune [24], EADP [43],
SID [10], and VLM-Pruner [49].
4.2 Main Results
Results on LLaVA-1.5-7B.
Existing methods are markedly lopsided across tasks. On the
general benchmarks they are hard to tell apart: at the 192-token budget in Table 1, the top
five baselines are all crammed between 96.7% and 98.1%. Yet they diverge sharply the moment
they leave the general tasks: the OCRBench score of Nüwa drops from 314 without pruning to
244 at 192 tokens and to only 152 at 64, and on grounding (Table 2) HiPrune, EADP and SID
retain merely 10.6%, 14.0% and 9.5% even at 192 tokens, no longer a mild degradation but a
breakdown. Bifocal holds both ends. It attains the best general average at all three budgets,
reaching 98.5%, 96.3% and 90.6%, while on grounding it scores 111.0% and 101.8% at 192 and
128 tokens, above the unpruned model, and still retains 66.7% at 64, 11.8 points above the
strongest baseline, Nüwa. That gap is larger than it looks, because Nüwa itself already leads the
third-place method, EADP, by 48.7 points at 64 tokens.

4.3 Ablation Study
Bifocal selects and allocates three kinds of tokens from different angles, so we verify that each of
them is indeed useful. Taking LLaVA-1.5-7B with an average of 128 tokens per LLM layer as an
example, we vary how the 224 Stage-1 tokens are split among C, I and Q (Table 4). C clearly
pays off on grounding: adding it lifts REC from 83.6% to 89.0% with I alone, and from 97.0% to
101.8% with Q alone. Like I, C is query-free, so the general average changes little. As expected,
removing I lowers POPE from 84.7 to 83.2, and removing Q drops REC from 102.7% to 89.0%.
We also note that the full configuration is not the best on every benchmark, which is a matter
of balance. As analysed in Section 2.1, keeping all three kinds of tokens yields the best overall
ability, making it the only allocation that leads on both averages, with 95.4% on general and
102.7% on grounding.

5 Conclusion
This paper approaches visual token pruning from a novel perspective and proposes a mixed-
granularity pruning framework before the LLM, composed of query-focused evidence, image-
intrinsic evidence and a coarse context, which preserves the abilities of the model more com-
prehensively. Building on this framework, we optimise the selection of each kind of token. We
combine the word and image saliency maps to better preserve query-focused evidence through
word weighting and word conjunction, then select the optimal token subset by representational
coverage, and finally add a coarse context pooled from the remaining patches. Together with
Stage 2, which further removes redundant tokens inside the LLM, Bifocal achieves the best
overall performance across multiple models and budgets.

A.2 The text route: a necessary condition for grounding
For the text route, we use a deletion test to ask the most direct question: can we do without it?
Starting from the unpruned sequence of 576 visual tokens, we remove equal numbers of tokens
selected by the text route, the image route, or at random, while progressively increasing the
number removed. Figure 7 shows a consistent trend across eight REC splits: at low removal
rates, random deletion has only a limited effect, whereas removing text-route tokens causes
Acc@0.5 to drop sharply, usually faster than removing image-route tokens. The text route may
not carry the majority of the evidence, but it provides query-specific evidence that grounding
cannot do without—if the model does not know what the question refers to, how can it draw
the box accurately? As the removal rate approaches one half, the curves of the two evidence
routes gradually converge. At this point, large-scale information loss begins to dominate, and
which route the removed tokens come from is no longer the main issue. This further supports
our conclusion in the main text: the two evidence routes cannot substitute for each other and
should each be allocated their own share of the fixed budget.

A.3 The image route: a wider view
However well the text route is optimised, what it keeps is still word-level evidence: before the
LLM there is no real understanding of the question, so the route can only keep the patches that
look like the words it was given. This fails in two ways: the object the question refers to is not
in the image at all, or the answer sits on something the question never names (or the question
has no target to begin with). In both cases, selecting tokens purely by word similarity means
that, under a reduced budget, the model is shown the wrong evidence, and hallucination follows.
This is exactly what the image route is for: it does not read the question, and its wider view
brings a more diverse sample of the scene into the budget.
Figure 8 shows three cases. In the first, the same image is asked two different questions: the
tokens selected by the text route change with the question, while the image route does not read
the query and its two rows are identical patch for patch. When asked about a bottle that is not
in the image, only the text route answers “yes”, while the image route and the half-and-half
mix both answer correctly. When asked about the sink that is present, all four inputs answer
correctly—so the text route does not say “yes” to everything, it says “yes” on fragments that
merely look like the queried object. The remaining two cases show the other failure: what the
question names is not where the answer lies, the text route again answers wrong, and the image
route is needed to bring that wider view back.
B Plugging Existing Pruning Methods into the Framework
To verify whether the mixed-granularity framework is effective in itself, we plug existing pruning
methods into it (Table 5). Each method replaces the selection rule of only one kind of fine
evidence, while the budgets of all three kinds of tokens stay fixed, and is compared with the same
method used on its own under the same total budget. For image-intrinsic evidence, HiPrune and
VisionZip replace our image-evidence selection on LLaVA-1.5-7B. Grounding on LLaVA-1.5-7B
hardly separates different query-focused methods, so for query-focused evidence EADP replaces
our query-evidence selection on Qwen2.5-VL-7B. With the mixed-granularity framework, every
method improves substantially, most clearly on grounding: the per-dataset gains range from 9.7
to 20.6 points, and Avg. rises by 6.8 to 9.7 points.

C Per-Cell Noise in the Cosine Map and What Smoothing Removes
The starting point is simple. Pruning keeps the top-m cells by score, and 576 cells compete
for that small handful of seats at the upper tail. If the score of each cell has picked up the
perturbation that comes with computing a cosine, the quality of the top-k suffers. This section
is therefore about three things: that the noise exists, that a simple averaging operation can filter
it out, and that this operation is safe, since the low-pass filter does not remove the information
that matters.

cell out it is unrelated to its neighbours, which says that what we dropped is not content. The
blue curve is the spectrum after smoothing, pushed down only at high frequency and almost
coinciding with the red curve at low frequency, which says the content at low frequency is still
there as it was. The 3 × 3 filter is therefore not smearing detail away but removing the jitter
that should not win a seat: the part it removes carries no content, and the step is safe.

I Details of Benchmarks
VQAv2 [11]. evaluates open-ended visual question answering on balanced image–question pairs,
with complementary images designed to reduce language priors and encourage stronger visual
grounding.
GQA [16]. GQA evaluates compositional visual reasoning over natural images using questions
derived from scene graphs, emphasizing objects, attributes, spatial relations, and structured
reasoning.
MMBench [27]. MMBench is a comprehensive multimodal benchmark that evaluates a broad
range of perception and reasoning abilities through carefully designed multiple-choice questions.
OK-VQA [32]. OK-VQA evaluates knowledge-based visual question answering, where answering
often requires external or commonsense knowledge beyond information directly observable in the
image.
ScienceQA [30]. ScienceQA evaluates multimodal scientific reasoning over questions from natural
science, social science, and language domains, requiring models to combine visual understanding
with contextual and domain knowledge.
AI2D [19]. AI2D evaluates diagram understanding and reasoning over scientific illustrations,
requiring models to interpret visual components, textual labels, symbols, and their structural
relationships.
TextVQA [39]. TextVQA evaluates visual question answering over text-rich natural images,
requiring models to recognize scene text and reason jointly over textual and surrounding visual
