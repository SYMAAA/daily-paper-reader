Lightweight but Linguistically Fair?
Quantisation Robustness across Bavarian, German, and English
Jophin John1
Michael Hoffmann1
Piroska Lendvai2
Florent Dufour3
1Leibniz Supercomputing Centre (LRZ), Garching, Germany

Abstract
Post-training quantisation reduces the memory
cost of large language models, but it remains
unclear whether compression preserves lan-
guage skills of regional varieties as reliably as
their standard-language counterparts. We study
this question for Bavarian, Standard German,
and English using BAVGROUND, a multiple-
choice benchmark, where 206 source questions
are aligned across all three languages. Across
14 open-weight 7B–10B instruction models, we
compare BF16 with 8-bit GPTQ and two 4-
bit settings, GPTQ and AWQ. Eight-bit GPTQ
remains close to BF16 and largely preserves
the relative behaviour of the three languages,
while 4-bit quantisation was found to be much
less stable. The two 4-bit methods reduce av-
erage accuracy by about 1.4–1.5 percentage
points and change 11.9–16.0% of BF16 deci-
sions, with changes concentrated on examples
where the BF16 model is already uncertain.
This instability does not translate into a con-
sistent Bavarian disadvantage. Although Bavar-
ian, a low-resource dialect, has lower base-
line accuracy than German and English, the
language most affected at 4 bits varies across
models and quantisation methods.
Calibra-
tion further shapes these cross-language effects:
the relative Bavarian–German effect changes
with calibration language, while changing the
calibration source has little average effect on
GPTQ-4 but can affect AWQ-4 much more
strongly. Free-form evaluation on five mod-
els further shows that 4-bit compression can
degrade generated responses even in settings
where multiple-choice accuracy changes only
modestly, while 8-bit GPTQ remains compar-
atively stable. Overall, our results show that
quantisation robustness for regional language
varieties depends on the model, quantisation
method, calibration data, and evaluation setting,
and cannot be measured by aggregate multilin-
gual accuracy alone.

Introduction
Post-training quantisation is increasingly used to re-
duce the memory cost of large language models and
enable deployment under tighter hardware budgets.
Weight-only methods such as GPTQ (Frantar et al.,
2023) and AWQ (Lin et al., 2024) can substantially
reduce model size while often preserving aggre-
gate benchmark accuracy. This makes quantisation
particularly attractive for open-weight models and
deployment settings in which memory capacity is
a primary constraint.
However, preservation of aggregate performance
does not imply that all languages are preserved
equally well. Multilingual models are trained and
evaluated disproportionately on high-resource stan-
dard languages, while regional and low-resource
varieties often receive less representation in train-
ing data, tokeniser construction, and benchmark de-
sign (Ahia et al., 2021; Hooker et al., 2021). Prior
work has shown that quantisation can affect lan-
guages unevenly and that automatic metrics can
underestimate degradation visible in generated out-
puts (Marchisio et al., 2024). What remains less
understood is whether these effects also extend to
regional varieties that are linguistically close to a
high-resource standard language.
Answering this question requires a compari-
son in which language varies while the underly-
ing content remains fixed. Standard multilingual
benchmarks such as MMLU (Hendrycks et al.,
2021), MGSM (Shi et al., 2023), and FLORES-
200 (NLLB Team et al., 2022) do not provide the
aligned regional-variety–standard-language setting
needed for such a comparison. Without this align-
ment, differences between language varieties can
be difficult to separate from differences in the ques-
tions or evaluation conditions themselves.
We address this gap using BAVGROUND (John
et al., 2026), a multiple-choice benchmark, whose
206 source questions are aligned across Bavarian,
arXiv:2610.18343v1  [cs.CL]  13 Oct 2026


Standard German, and English. This allows us to
compare each quantised model with its own BF16
baseline on the same underlying questions and ex-
amine whether compression affects Bavarian dif-
ferently from German and English. We evaluate
14 open-weight 7B–10B instruction models under
BF16, GPTQ-8, GPTQ-4, and AWQ-4, and further
test how these cross-language effects change with
both the language composition and source of the
calibration data.
Across this controlled comparison, GPTQ-8 re-
mains close to BF16 and largely preserves the rela-
tive performance of Bavarian, German, and English.
At 4 bits, robustness becomes more dependent on
the model, quantisation method, and calibration
data, without consistently disadvantaging Bavarian.
Free-form evaluation further reveals degradation
that is not always apparent from multiple-choice
accuracy.
Our contributions are threefold:
• We conduct a controlled quantisation study
across 14 open-weight 7B–10B models using
aligned Bavarian, Standard German, and En-
glish questions, allowing direct comparison of
a regional variety with its standard language
across precision levels and quantisation meth-
ods.
• We examine how calibration data shape cross-
language quantisation robustness by varying
both their language composition and source,
and relate item-level prediction instability to
BF16 decision margins.
• We extend prior findings on evaluation-
dependent multilingual quantisation effects to
a dialect–standard-language pair. Our blinded
free-form evaluation tests whether robustness
observed in multiple-choice accuracy carries
over to generated responses.

Related Work
Post-training
quantisation:
Post-Training
Quantization (PTQ) (Frantar et al., 2023; Lin
et al., 2024; Xiao et al., 2023) converts the weights
and, in some approaches, the activations of a
neural network from their original 32- or 16-bit
floating-point representations to lower-precision
formats, typically 8-bit or 4-bit integers. GPTQ
uses approximate second-order information to
minimise layer-wise reconstruction error during
low-bit weight quantisation (Frantar et al., 2023),
whereas AWQ identifies activation-salient channels
and rescales them prior to weight quantisation
(Lin et al., 2024). These methods can provide
strong quality–efficiency trade-offs, with prior
evaluations showing that moderate quantisation
often preserves much of a model’s aggregate
performance,
particularly
for
larger
models
(Giagnorio et al., 2026).
Early studies established that neural networks
can retain useful performance under reduced nu-
merical precision, while highlighting their sensitiv-
ity to quantisation (Sung et al., 2015). Aggregate
performance, however, does not always change
uniformly with precision. Quantised models can
sometimes match or even slightly outperform their
higher-precision counterparts on particular tasks
or benchmarks (Badshah and Sajjad, 2024; Lee
et al., 2025). Such improvements tend to depend
on the model, task, and evaluation setting rather
than reflecting a general benefit of lower precision.
More importantly, stable or improved aggregate
scores can conceal losses on particular subsets of
the evaluation data. This makes it necessary to ex-
amine systematically where quantisation changes
model behaviour rather than treating aggregate ro-
bustness as evidence that all capabilities are pre-
served equally.
Multilingual and language-aware quantisation:
Recent work has examined these effects more di-
rectly in multilingual settings. Marchisio et al.
(2024) show that quantisation robustness varies
substantially across languages and that automatic
metrics can miss degradation visible in generated
outputs.
Calibration data can also shape these
effects.
Zeng et al. (2024) show that multilin-
gual calibration can improve preservation of lower-
resource languages, while Chimoto et al. (2026)
find that the language composition of GPTQ and
AWQ calibration data affects multilingual quantisa-
tion quality. These studies establish that language-
specific robustness depends on more than bit width
alone. However, they primarily compare distinct
languages, where differences in script, typology,
content, and benchmark construction can accom-
pany language identity. This leaves open whether
similar effects arise between a regional variety and
its closely related standard language when the un-
derlying content is held fixed.
Regional and cultural evaluation:
Accomplish-
ing regional and cultural evaluation requires tar-
geted benchmark datasets that can distinguish


language-specific effects from differences in the
evaluated content. This is particularly challenging
for regional and culturally specific language vari-
eties, which are often unevenly represented in both
training data and existing benchmarks. Evaluation
of dialectal language skills receives increasing at-
tention in the context of assessing Large language
model (LLM) fairness and bias (Lin et al., 2025).
Often, the focus of these studies is on English, in-
volving synthetically generated data and human-
in-the loop validation, e.g. by Ziems et al. (2023),
but there are examples involving LLM-as-judge
approaches too (Faisal et al., 2025). LLMs have
been shown to encode cultural knowledge unevenly
across regions and languages (Santy et al., 2023;
Arora et al., 2023), while reliably evaluating such
knowledge remains an open problem (Hershcovich
et al., 2022). Li et al. (2024) propose a method
for incorporating cultural differences into LLMs,
while Durmus et al. (2024) investigate how lan-
guage models represent subjective opinions across
countries.
Recent benchmarks address these concerns
through more targeted cultural evaluation. Chiu
et al. (2025) introduce CulturalBench, comprising
1,696 human-written and human-verified English
questions across 45 global regions, and show that
multilingual capability does not necessarily imply
multicultural competence. Closer to our setting,
John et al. (2026) introduce BAVGROUND, a bench-
mark of Bavarian cultural knowledge with aligned
Bavarian, Standard German, and English questions.
We use BAVGROUND for our quantisation study
and describe the benchmark and its alignment in
Section 3.
3
Experimental Setup
We evaluate post-training quantisation by compar-
ing each quantised model with its own BF16 base-
line. The three-way alignment of BAVGROUND
allows us to measure performance changes on the
same underlying questions in English, Standard
German, and Bavarian. We additionally measure
peak GPU memory and batch-size-one throughput
on a single NVIDIA GH200 GPU. Full hardware
and runtime details are provided in Appendix B.
3.1
Benchmark
Our work uses the BAVGROUND dataset that con-
tains 206 source questions, each realised in English,
German and Bavarian, yielding alltogether 618
multiple-choice instances. The questions of this
dataset span eight cultural domains which are each
divided into two subsets: GEN, covering broadly
accessible (or general) cultural knowledge, and
GRD, which consists of question-answer pairs hand-
made constructed from regional newspapers, his-
torical references, and specialist anthropological
sources. The evaluated set contains 240 GEN and
378 GRD instances. Because all three language
versions correspond to the same source question,
cross-language comparisons are paired by source
item rather than across independent monolingual
benchmarks.
3.2
Models and Quantisation
We evaluate 14 open-weight 7B–10B instruction
models chosen to cover a range of language and
pretraining profiles.
The main panel includes
the Bavarian-adapted GENBA-10B (Hoffmann
et al., 2025), the German-focused LeoLM and
LLaMmlein, five European multilingual models
(EuroLLM, Mistral, Occiglot, Teuken, and Sala-
mandra), and six broader multilingual models
(Llama 3.1, Qwen2.5, Gemma 2, Aya Expanse,
Granite 3.0, and OLMo 2). GENBA-10B is specif-
ically adapted to English, German, and Bavarian,
while Llama 3.1 provides a closely related parent-
model comparison. Exact checkpoint identifiers
are listed in Appendix C.
For each model in this main panel, we compare
the original BF16 checkpoint with GPTQ-8, GPTQ-
4, and AWQ-4, yielding 56 model–precision con-
figurations. The GPTQ-8 and GPTQ-4 compari-
son isolates the effect of precision within the same
quantisation method, while GPTQ-4 and AWQ-4
compare two methods at the same 4-bit precision.
All quantised models use a group size of 128, with
embeddings and output layers retained at their orig-
inal precision. Quantisation is calibrated on ap-
proximately 250k tokens balanced across English,
German, and Bavarian, using translated instruction-
style examples from Alpaca, Dolly, and OpenHer-
mes. These data are used only for post-training
quantisation, with no detected exact-text overlap
with BAVGROUND questions, answer options, or
recorded source references.
We additionally test calibration sensitivity on
four models by repeating GPTQ-4 and AWQ-4
with balanced, English-only, German-only, and
Bavarian-only calibration data while keeping the
calibration budget, sequence length, and other
quantisation settings fixed (Appendix A.8). To test


whether these effects also depend on the type of
text used for calibration, we repeat the 4-bit ex-
periments with version-pinned Wikimedia text in
English, German, and Bavarian. The calibration-
language ablation uses seed 42, while the focused
calibration-source comparison uses three seeds; the
latter is additionally evaluated across all 14 mod-
els using balanced seed-42 calibration. Finally, a
supplementary Qwen-family analysis evaluates ad-
ditional checkpoints from Qwen2, Qwen2.5, and
Qwen3 to test whether the behaviour observed for
Qwen2.5 in the main panel extends across Qwen
generations and model sizes.
3.3
Evaluation Protocol
Our primary evaluation uses multiple-choice ac-
curacy. For each question, we select the answer
candidate with the highest mean token-level condi-
tional log-likelihood given the question context.
Length normalisation reduces answer-length ef-
fects, while scoring the option text rather than A–D
labels avoids model-specific label priors. We also
evaluate answer-letter likelihood and greedy gener-
ation as diagnostics for label bias and output-format
failures, but neither contributes to the primary accu-
racy measure. We additionally evaluate free-form
generation on five representative models using all
618 BAVGROUND instances. Each quantised re-
sponse is compared with its BF16 counterpart by
a blinded pairwise LLM judge (gpt-5.6-luna).
Judging prioritises semantic correctness, allows
ties, and considers Bavarian dialect appropriate-
ness secondarily. A random 20% subset is repeated
with reversed response order to check position bias.
Full judge instructions and coverage statistics are
given in Appendix A.14.
3.4
Quantisation Effects and Statistical
Analysis
For model m, quantisation setting q, and lan-
guage ℓ, we define the BF16-relative change as
∆m,q,ℓ= Sm,q,ℓ−Sm,BF16,ℓ. We measure whether
quantisation widens the pre-existing gap between
Bavarian and comparator language c using
Am,q,c = ∆m,q,BAR −∆m,q,c,
(1)
which we term dialect-gap amplification. Neg-
ative values indicate that Bavarian degrades more
under quantisation than the comparator language,
while positive values indicate greater degrada-
tion of the comparator. We also compare BF16
and quantised predictions item by item to distin-
guish correct-to-incorrect, incorrect-to-correct, and
correctness-preserving answer changes. We use
5,000 bootstrap replicates throughout, resampling
source questions for aggregate effects and both
models and questions for dialect-gap amplification.
We treat cross-language effects as practically equiv-
alent when the 90% confidence interval falls en-
tirely within a ±2 percentage-point margin, and
check the sensitivity of this conclusion at ±1 and
±3 points. Full resampling and sensitivity results
are reported in Appendix A.3.
4
Results
We first examine the overall effect of quantisation
and whether robustness differs between Bavarian,
its closely related standard language German, and
English. We then analyse how calibration data, pre-
diction instability, and free-form generation shape
this dialect–standard-language comparison, before
considering the resulting deployment trade-offs.
4.1
Eight-bit robustness is broadly preserved,
while four-bit robustness does not follow
linguistic specialisation
Across the 14 models, GPTQ-8 remains essentially
unchanged from BF16, with a mean accuracy dif-
ference of +0.03 percentage points. In contrast,
both 4-bit methods reduce mean accuracy by about
1.4–1.5 points. The 95% confidence interval for
GPTQ-8 includes zero, whereas those for GPTQ-4
and AWQ-4 indicate a clearer aggregate loss. Full
confidence intervals are reported in Appendix A.1.
The average 4-bit loss, however, hides substantial
variation across models (Table 1). GPTQ-4 effects
range from a 2.3-point gain for Qwen2.5 to a 6.6-
point loss for GENBA-10B, while AWQ-4 ranges
from a 3.2-point gain for Gemma 2 to a 5.7-point
loss for Granite. This variation does not follow the
models’ linguistic focus.
German-oriented models such as Teuken, Le-
oLM, and LLaMmlein are not consistently more
robust, and the Bavarian-adapted GENBA-10B
is among the models most affected by GPTQ-4.
Conversely, several broader multilingual models
remain comparatively stable. Apparent gains are
checkpoint-dependent rather than family-wide, as
illustrated by the heterogeneous Qwen-family re-
sults (Appendix A.2). Taken together, these re-
sults suggest that linguistic specialisation does not
consistently protect a model from aggressive com-

4.2
Bavarian’s lower baseline does not
translate into greater quantisation
fragility
These model-level differences also raise the ques-
tion of whether Bavarian’s weaker baseline makes
it more vulnerable to compression. Bavarian starts
from a lower BF16 accuracy than German and En-
glish (56.9% vs. 60.7% and 62.4%) and is also
tokenised less compactly (Appendix A.5).
We
therefore examine whether quantisation widens the
existing gap between Bavarian and German or En-
glish. Table 2 shows that this is not the case at 8
bits. The Bavarian–German and Bavarian–English
effects both fall within our ±2 percentage-point
equivalence region, indicating that GPTQ-8 largely
preserves the relative language pattern. At 4 bits,
however, the effects are less consistent. GPTQ-4
tends towards greater Bavarian degradation relative
to German, while AWQ-4 shifts the average con-
trast in the opposite direction; neither satisfies the
equivalence criterion.
Per-model results make this heterogeneity
clearer (Table 11). Bavarian accuracy decreases in
10 of 14 models under GPTQ-4 and 8 under AWQ-
4, compared with 5 under GPTQ-8. These losses
are not consistently larger than those in German:
under GPTQ-4, Bavarian degrades more than Ger-
man for 9 models and less for four, while AWQ-4 is
almost evenly split between the two directions. Lin-
guistically specialised models show the same varia-
tion. LeoLM, LLaMmlein, and Teuken lose more
in Bavarian under GPTQ-4, whereas GENBA-
10B shows the opposite pattern, losing 3.4 points
in Bavarian, 5.3 in German, and 11.2 in English.
Adaptation to Bavarian therefore does not make
a model uniformly more robust, although it may
influence which language is better preserved under
compression. The same disconnect appears beyond
language. The harder GRD subset has lower BF16
accuracy than GEN (57.9% vs. 63.4%), yet both
show nearly identical 4-bit losses (Appendix A.6).
Lower baseline performance therefore does not
consistently predict greater quantisation sensitiv-
ity. Instead, the language most affected at 4 bits
depends more on the individual model and quan-
tisation method than on baseline performance or
linguistic specialisation.

4.4
Small accuracy losses conceal substantial
prediction instability
Beyond these cross-language differences, aggre-
gate accuracy also hides how often quantisation
changes individual predictions. GPTQ-8 changes
only 1.18% of matched BF16 predictions, com-
pared with 11.93% for GPTQ-4 and 16.00% for
AWQ-4.
For both 4-bit methods, correct-to-
incorrect changes outnumber compensating gains,
with additional wrong-to-wrong changes further in-
creasing the divergence from BF16. These changes
are strongly concentrated on questions where the
BF16 model is already uncertain. GPTQ-4 and
AWQ-4 alter roughly 40–43% of predictions in the
lowest-margin decile, but only 1–2% in the high-
est; GPTQ-8 changes 10.16% in the lowest decile
and none in the highest (Table 4). An item-level
analysis confirms that pre-quantisation decision
margin is a much stronger predictor of instability
than language or prompt length (Appendix A.11).
The small average losses at 4 bits therefore hide
substantial changes in individual predictions, par-
ticularly on questions where the model was already
uncertain before quantisation.

hidden by multiple-choice accuracy

behaviour becomes even clearer in free-form gener-

response winning or tying in 86.9% of cases. At
4 bits, BF16 responses are preferred more often,
particularly under AWQ-4 (Table 5). The differ-
ence between the two 4-bit methods is much larger
here than in multiple-choice accuracy. GPTQ-4
and AWQ-4 have similar average multiple-choice
losses, yet AWQ-4 shows substantially poorer re-
tention in free-form responses. This gap is also vis-

Bavarian, German, and English. At 4 bits, however,
the degradation is more pronounced. Under GPTQ-
4, BF16 responses are preferred more strongly
in Bavarian and German, while English remains
comparatively stable. AWQ-4 shows degradation
across all three languages, with German at least
as affected as Bavarian. This mirrors the multiple-
choice results above: 4-bit compression can affect
Bavarian substantially, but the effect is not consis-
tently stronger than in German. Because the free-
form comparison relies on pairwise judging, we
also checked sensitivity to response order, which
was generally consistent. We treat these results as
complementary evidence of response preservation
rather than as human validation of Bavarian natu-
ralness or sociolinguistic quality (Appendix A.14).
4.6
Large memory savings do not require
proportional quality loss
Despite the quality losses observed above, quantisa-
tion can still offer substantial memory savings with
little change in multiple-choice accuracy. Across
the measured configurations, 11 quantised model
configurations reduce peak reserved memory by at
least 50% while losing no more than one percent-
age point relative to BF16 (Figure 1). Mistral 7B
GPTQ-4 provides one of the strongest quality–
memory trade-offs in our measurements: memory
falls from 19.2 GB to 7.9 GB, a 58.9% reduction,
while multiple-choice accuracy remains essentially
unchanged (66.8% vs. 67.2%) (Appendix A.15).
Its Bavarian, German, and English multiple-choice
scores also remain close to their BF16 baselines
(Appendix A.4). The same Mistral configuration
also shows why this trade-off depends on how qual-
ity is measured. Although its multiple-choice accu-
racy is preserved, free-form judging gives GPTQ-4
a −3.9 point net preference relative to BF16 over-
all and −5.1 points in Bavarian (Appendix A.14).
A strong quality–memory trade-off under fixed-
choice evaluation can therefore still hide degrada-
tion in generated responses. More broadly, GPTQ-
4 matches or exceeds GPTQ-8 multiple-choice ac-
curacy for 5 of 14 models despite using fewer bits,
showing that greater compression is not uniformly
worse.
5
Discussion
The clearest distinction in our results is between
broadly stable 8-bit quantisation and less pre-
dictable 4-bit compression. GPTQ-8 largely pre-
serves average accuracy and the relative behaviour
of Bavarian, German, and English. At 4 bits, how-
ever, robustness varies more across models and
methods, with no consistent protection from lin-
guistic specialisation. This makes calibration par-

Changing the calibration language can alter the
Bavarian–German effect under GPTQ-4, while
calibration source reveals a different method-

stable across instruction-style and Wikimedia data,

Robustness
therefore depends not only on the checkpoint and
method, but also on the calibration data. These
differences are also difficult to explain from base-
line performance. Bavarian starts from lower BF16
accuracy and less compact tokenisation, yet neither
consistently predicts larger losses. Instead, pre-
diction changes concentrate on low-margin BF16
decisions, explaining why small average accuracy
changes can conceal substantial behavioural differ-
ences.
The free-form results show the same mismatch:
GPTQ-4 and AWQ-4 have similar fixed-choice
losses, but AWQ-4 preserves generated responses
less well. For closely related varieties, the ques-
tion is therefore not only whether quantisation low-
ers performance, but whether it changes the rela-
tive behaviour of the dialect and standard language.
In our aligned Bavarian–German comparison, this
depends on the model, quantisation method, cali-
bration data, and evaluation setting rather than on
baseline performance or linguistic specialisation
alone. Robustness should therefore be assessed via
language-specific changes on matched questions,
not aggregate multilingual accuracy.
6
Conclusion
We asked whether making language models lighter
through post-training quantisation also preserves
their behaviour across Bavarian, Standard German,
and English. For GPTQ-8, the answer is largely yes.
Performance remains close to BF16 and the relative
differences between the three languages are broadly
preserved. At 4 bits, this stability weakens. Bavar-
ian is not consistently more affected than German,
but the relative behaviour of the two languages
varies more across models, quantisation methods,
and calibration data. The aligned dialect vs. stan-
dard language comparison is important here be-
cause these changes are difficult to identify from ag-
gregate multilingual accuracy alone. It also shows
that lower baseline performance in Bavarian does
not by itself imply greater fragility, while free-form
evaluation reveals losses that fixed-choice accuracy
can miss. Our matrix of experiments indicates that
quantisation can therefore make models substan-
tially lighter without necessarily harming Bavarian
more than German, but linguistic preservation at
4 bits cannot be assumed from compression level
alone. For regional language varieties, lightweight
deployment and linguistic robustness need to be
evaluated together.
7
Limitations
Our study focuses on Bavarian and BAVGROUND,
so the findings may not generalise to other di-
alects or low-resource languages, and we do not
model Bavarian’s internal regional or orthographic
variation. The free-form analysis broadens the
evaluation beyond multiple choice but relies on
a single LLM judge. The response-order audit
shows high consistency, out of 9,270 planned com-
parisons, 112 were excluded due to output trun-
cation and 14 due to malformed pair identifiers.
The calibration-language ablation is deliberately
focused, covering four models and one seed per
condition. The calibration-source experiment adds
three-seed replication for the focused four-model
comparison while its broader seed-42 comparison
uses balanced calibration, with 14 valid GPTQ-4
and 13 valid AWQ-4 pairs. Broader replication
would therefore be useful for establishing how sta-
ble these calibration effects are across models and
seeds. Finally, the memory results are specific to
our hardware, software stack, and batch-size-one
inference setup.

The main experiments use translated instruction-

the 4-bit experiments with natural Wikipedia text
in English, German, and Bavarian. We use the
corresponding November 2023 Wikimedia snap-
shots and keep the calibration budget and sequence
length unchanged, packing each calibration set into
120 sequences of 2,048 tokens.
The corpus is
version-pinned and contains no detected exact or
near overlap with BAVGROUND or the instruction-
style calibration data.
We compare the two calibration sources in two
settings.
The focused experiment covers four
models, both 4-bit quantisers, all four calibration-
language conditions, and three seeds. We addi-
tionally compare balanced calibration across all 14
models at seed 42. Table 20 reports both compar-
isons.
As Table 20 shows, changing the calibration

A.10
Instability by BF16 Decision Margin
The margin-extreme results are reported in the main
text in Table 4. The analysis uses the same paired
BF16–quantised item rows as the transition decom-
position and groups examples by deciles of the
BF16 selected-answer margin.
The resulting pattern is strongly monotonic: an-
swer changes are concentrated among low-margin
predictions and become rare for high-margin ex-
amples. In the lowest decile, GPTQ-4 and AWQ-4
alter roughly 40–43% of decisions, whereas only
1–2% change in the highest decile. GPTQ-8 is
substantially more stable overall, but still changes
10.16% of the lowest-margin predictions and none
of the highest-margin predictions. This supports
the interpretation that quantisation primarily per-
turbs decisions that are already weakly separated
in BF16 rather than uniformly disrupting high-
confidence predictions.
