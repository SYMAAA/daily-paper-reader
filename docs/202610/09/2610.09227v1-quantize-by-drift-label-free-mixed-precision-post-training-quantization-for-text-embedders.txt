# Abstract 

Mixed-precision post-training quantization needs a per-module sensitivity signal, and for a text embedder the obvious one — how much retrieval quality a module costs when quantized — requires relevance labels that deployments rarely have. We measure a label-free substi-tute: quantization-induced representation drift , obtained by quantizing one module, re-encoding the corpus, and recording how far the output embeddings moved from their full-precision positions. Measuring sensitivity by interven-ing rather than by inspecting the full-precision model is not itself new; what is specific here is the quantity observed — the displacement of the deployed output representation, which is what a dense retriever ranks with — and the set-ting, encoder-weight mixed precision for retrieval rather than index or output-vector compression. Across five development embedders, configuration-level drift orders sampled mixed-precision plans against held-out retrieval quality at a macro Spearman of 0.911, the module sensi-tivity transports across calibration corpora and retrieval domains in the usable regime, module drifts compose rank-consistently but not numerically, and relevance-derived sensitivity adds no consistent value over drift. The method is a single additive allocation over measured drift under a hard packed-byte budget constraint, with no labels, no interaction model and no search. On three embedders held untouched until the method, baselines and hypothe-ses were frozen and sealed, the pre-registered directional hypothesis against the prior LieQ criterion holds (3/3 at the main budget, no collapse), drift scores above the two-sided LieQ steelman in 2/3, and the label-free signal reproduces under calibration resampling on all three; but at the main budget the drift allocation is numerically lower than same-budget uniform precision on all three (-0.99, -0.85, -1.01 points; under independent re-evaluation the paired query-level interval excludes zero on 1 of 3 mod-els), and in the one tighter-budget cell where uniform collapses (5.30) both drift (28.84) and the LieQ adapta-tion (27.99) remain usable. We report the confirmation before the pooled numbers and do not average over it. Together these results position output drift as a robust coarse sensitivity signal rather than a universally optimal allocation objective: it avoids the catastrophic failures of the transferred signed-geometry adaptation and can remain usable at stressed budgets where uniform collapses, but fine-grained redistribution around a strong uniform operating point remains unresolved. 

# 1 Introduction 

Quantizing a text embedder is not the same problem as quantizing a generative model. Dense retrievers encode queries and documents independently and rank by a simi-larity between the two vectors [13, 34, 11], and a single embedder is typically served behind every query a system answers, which makes it a natural target for low-bit weight compression. The output is a vector whose geometry is the product, and the downstream metric — ranking qual-ity — depends on relative distances rather than on any single token. Mixed-precision allocation therefore needs a sensitivity signal that tracks retrieval damage, and the tempting choice is to measure that damage directly with relevance judgements. Deployments usually do not have them. This paper reports what happens when the signal is label-free and produced by the quantization itself. For each module and bit width we apply the quantization, re-encode the calibration corpus, and measure the cosine displacement of the resulting embeddings from their full-precision positions. 

What the claim is, and what it is not. A reader can reasonably object that “embeddings that move less are embeddings that rank better” is close to a tautology, and that finding a correlation between whole-model drift and retrieval quality is not a contribution. We agree, and that is not the claim. The unit of novelty here is not the correlation but the allocation signal : whether drift mea-sured by quantizing a single module in isolation carries enough information to decide that module’s precision, and whether the resulting ordering is stable enough across mod-els, budgets and retrieval domains to be used without any downstream supervision. Those are empirical questions 1arXiv:2610.09227v1 [cs.IR] 6 Oct 2026 with a real chance of a negative answer — single-module drift could compose too badly to predict a configuration, it could be dominated by module size, or it could reorder whenever the calibration corpus changes. §5 and §4.4 test each of them. 

Two jobs for a sensitivity signal. The results sepa-rate two things a sensitivity signal is asked to do. One is to avoid catastrophic precision mistakes — to keep bits away from a module whose quantization collapses retrieval and to survive budgets at which the trivial allocation does not. The other is to resolve small trade-offs among allo-cations that are already good. Drift is much stronger at the first than at the second, and the paper’s own devel-opment analysis already says why: whole-configuration drift orders sampled allocations against held-out quality at a macro Spearman of 0.911 over the full pool, but the correlation falls markedly once the pool is restricted to its best quarter (§4.1, Table 19), and the inversion rate of the additive prediction rises in the same region (§5). The confirmation on three untouched models (§4.3) is the same fact seen from the other side: the pre-registered com-parison against a prior geometry criterion replicates, and at the budget where uniform precision is already healthy, drift does not improve on it. We treat that not as a failed method but as the boundary of what a coarse surrogate can be expected to resolve. The position this paper ends in is therefore narrower than the one it started from: output drift is a robust coarse signal for avoiding harmful preci-sion assignments across heterogeneous embedders, and it is not a universally superior fine-grained allocator — on untouched models it scores above a prior geometry-based adaptation while consistently, if not always significantly, trailing uniform precision at the healthy 3-bit operating point. 

Relation to intervention-based allocation. Measur-ing sensitivity by actually quantizing a component is an established idea. InfoQ [1] quantizes each layer at each candidate bit width and scores it by the resulting change in mutual information in the subsequent layers , then allo-cates by integer linear programming; our allocator is the same integer program over summed per-module scores, and we make no claim of novelty for it. The difference is in what is observed. InfoQ reads an internal, information-theoretic quantity — sliced mutual information between activations at downstream observer points — inside a vi-sion CNN, and validates on ImageNet accuracy. We read the final output embedding , which for a dense retriever is not one representation among many but the exact vector the ranking score is computed from; Proposition 1 makes that connection quantitative. Whether that difference in observable changes the allocation, and changes it for the better on retrieval, is an empirical claim that §4.3 settles only in part: against the prior geometry criterion, yes; against uniform precision at a healthy operating point, no. Two further differences are matters of evaluation rather than method: we measure how far the sensitivity transports across retrieval domains, and we express bud-gets in real packed bytes including scale and zero-point metadata. §7 places both against geometry-based and interaction-aware allocators. 

Contributions. 

• A retrieval-facing, label-free sensitivity signal 

(§2). Quantization-induced displacement of the out-put embedding, measured per module and bit width, requiring no relevance judgements, no gradients and no search. Proposition 1 connects it deterministi-cally to the perturbation of the cosine score a dense retriever ranks with. • An empirical study of output-drift allocation across heterogeneous text embedders and ac-tual packed-byte regimes (§3–§4), including how far module sensitivity transports across retrieval do-mains, and a pre-registered confirmation on three untouched embedders (§4.3) that characterises the signal’s strengths and its limits rather than only its wins: the comparison against a prior geometry crite-rion replicates, the comparison against uniform pre-cision at the main budget does not. # 3 Experimental setup 

Models. Five development embedders: E5-base-v2 [31], BGE-M3 [3], EmbeddingGemma-300M [25], Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B [41] and SKILLRET-0.6B, abbreviated in the tables as E5-base, BGE-M3, EmbGemma, Qwen3-0.6B and SKILLRET. Three further embedders — mxbai-embed-large-v1, all-mpnet-base-v2 [24, 28] and gte-base-en-v1.5 [19, 40], abbreviated mxbai-large, MPNet-base and GTE-base — were held out entirely until the method, the baselines, the budget grid, every threshold and the hypotheses were frozen and their pre-registration sealed by hash; they were then run once under the frozen protocol and are reported in §4.3 under the pre-registered order, before any pooled number. 

Quantizer. Weight-only group-wise asymmetric round-to-nearest [10] with a per-group FP16 scale and zero point; activations are left in full precision. Candidate bit widths are {2, 3, 4} per module and tensors with fewer than two dimensions are not quantized. Those one-dimensional pa-rameters (biases and normalisation weights) are excluded from both the numerator and the denominator of the packed-bit accounting; across the eight models they are 0.011–0.112% of all parameters, and two-dimensional ten-sors that are kept in full precision are charged at 16 bits and amount to at most 1.66% of parameters. Both rules apply identically to every arm of a model. The group size is chosen per model so that a uniform 3-bit packing is a feasible point of the budget grid, and therefore differs across models; Table 1 reports it together with the realized packed cost of each budget. 

Retrieval. Documents and queries are encoded with each model’s own pooling and prompt conventions through 

sentence-transformers [24], the resulting embeddings are ℓ2-normalized, and retrieval scores are inner products on those unit vectors — that is, cosine similarity. Search is exact rather than approximate, so no index-side ap-proximation enters the measurement. This is the setting Proposition 1 assumes. “Full precision” throughout means the released weights loaded in FP32; no half-precision infer-ence is used anywhere in the pipeline, and every quantized arm is produced by round-to-nearest from that same FP32 3copy, so the label applies identically to every arm. (Ear-lier drafts wrote FP16 for this reference; the accounting charges skipped tensors at 16 bits, which is a deploy-ment assumption, not the evaluation dtype.) Differences quoted in the text are computed from unrounded ledger values, so subtracting two displayed two-decimal cells can differ from the quoted difference by 0.01 . One software asymmetry is disclosed here rather than hidden in a foot-note: the remote modelling code of gte-base-en-v1.5 

indexes its rotary position table with uninitialised posi-tions under transformers 5.14.1 and fails before produc-ing an embedding, so that model alone is evaluated under 

transformers 4.49.0, with every arm for that model — full precision, uniform, both LieQ variants and drift — under the same pinned version; the other seven models run under release 5.14.1, which the container resolves by default. The diagnostic that isolated the version is in the released ledgers. 

Allocation units. We partition the encoder into twelve units: four attention layer-bands, four feed-forward layer-bands, and four vocabulary bands of the embedding table. Layer bands are equal splits of the layer index, so band j

holds layers ⌊jL/ 4⌋ . . . ⌊(j+1) L/ 4⌋ − 1 for an L-layer en-coder; this makes the partition comparable across models with different depths. Vocabulary bands are contiguous row slices of the embedding matrix in tokenizer-ID order ,that is, rows ⌊jV / 4⌋ . . . ⌊(j+1) V / 4⌋ − 1 of a V -row table. The partition is therefore a fixed property of the released tokenizer and is not derived from token frequencies in our calibration corpus, so the partition itself does not introduce an additional corpus-dependent grouping rule. It does not follow that the embedding bands are domain-invariant: different corpora exercise different token IDs, so the measured sensitivity of a band can still depend on the domain. That is an empirical question, and §4.4 is where it is answered rather than assumed. Bands rather than individual layers keep the configuration space enumerable while still letting the allocator separate early from late computation and low-ID from high-ID vocabulary. Deploy-ing per-band precision on the embedding table requires a row-blocked packing format; we account for its scale and zero-point overhead in the budget below but do not report kernel-level latency. Whether twelve is the right number of units is checked in Appendix G. 

Budget. Budgets are expressed in actual packed bits per weight , including the group-wise scale and zero-point overhead, because a nominal “3.5 bpw” means a different memory footprint on models with different group sizes and padding. Writing Sb for the packed cost of a uniform 

b-bit model, we evaluate at two operating points, B2↔3 =(S2+S3)/2 and B3 = S3: a stressed regime in which uniform precision is often unusable, and the healthy regime in which it is. A third point, B3↔4 = ( S3+S4)/2, appeared in early drafts of the protocol; it was never measured on any model, the frozen confirmation pre-registration names only the two points above, and the scope of the paper is fixed to them. We keep the older labels B2.5 and B3.0

in the tables for continuity with the ledger, but they are budget names , not bit widths: the realized packed cost of 

B2.5 differs across models (Table 22 reports it per cell), and reading “2.5” as 2.5 bpw is wrong. 

Corpora. All corpora are taken from BEIR [29], whose heterogeneous zero-shot tasks also make up most of the retrieval portion of MTEB [22]. Module drift is profiled on a probe corpus (BEIR ArguAna) subsampled to 1,500 documents and 150 queries; all reported retrieval quality is on a disjoint held-out corpus (BEIR NFCorpus, 3,633 documents and 323 queries). Probe and evaluation banks are disjoint throughout, and no relevance judgement from either corpus enters the drift computation. 

Configuration pools. How the pool is built matters, because a correlation measured on it inherits its composi-tion. We request 176 plans per model and keep the distinct feasible ones, which leaves the per-model counts in Table 1. The requested mix is 50% one- and two-swap perturba-tions of the uniform plan, 25% perturbations of allocator outputs (the drift plan and its neighbourhood), and 25% plans drawn uniformly at random from the feasible set at the same budget. The allocator-derived quarter means the pool is not independent of the signal being evaluated. We regard the pooled ρ as descriptive of this pool rather than of the feasible set, and §8 records a drift-independent random-only pool as required before the number is used as a headline. For the search and composition analyses each cell evaluates 100 random feasible plans plus every distinct feasible neighbour of the seeded plan, and the two groups are reported separately in Table 9. The seeded neighbourhood is not the same size in every cell: it ranges from 6 to 100 plans, so a cell holds between 106 and 200 configurations in total. 1 cell has fewer than fifty seeded neighbours, and its ∆search is correspondingly a statement about a small neighbourhood rather than a broad one; Table 21 gives the per-cell counts.

# 4.2 Allocation quality against budget-matched baselines 

Why the InfoQ-X column is empty. The second published criterion we tried is absent from Table 3 for a reason worth stating in the body rather than only in an appendix. An allocator consumes the ordering of module sensitivities, so we fixed an estimator-reproducibility cri-terion in advance and resolved it before computing any retrieval score. The InfoQ-X adaptation did not meet it: over 35 model-rungs — seven ladder settings on each of 5 models — reaching the original work’s 6,000-sample scale, the seed-pairwise rank correlation of the sensitivity scores never rose above 0.530, against the 0.9 fixed in advance. 

The adaptation was therefore rejected before out-come evaluation, not left unrun , and we do not report an NDCG comparison that would be a comparison of noise. The allocations also never reproduced, but we do 

not treat that as the load-bearing evidence: Appendix F shows that a known-good signal degraded to the same rank agreement also almost never reproduces an alloca-tion, so plan identity at this level of agreement says more about the allocator than about the estimator. Appendix B gives the criterion and the ladder, and Appendix H the implementation audit that qualifies it. 

And our own signal under an analogous audit. Agate that rejects a baseline should also be one the proposed method is asked to face. Drift has no stochastic element inside the estimator — given a calibration corpus it is deterministic — so there is no exact analogue of redrawing a projection bank. We therefore varied the one stochastic input it does have, resampling which 3 calibration subsets the sensitivity is measured on, and computed the same three statistics the stability ladder reports. On all 5 de-velopment models the seed-pairwise rank correlation is 0.9997, the median plan Hamming distance is 0.0, and the selected plan is byte-identical in 100.0% of seed pairs; 5 of 5 models satisfy the criterion that InfoQ-X failed (Table 13). The two randomness sources are differ-ent , so these numbers are not a controlled comparison of estimator variance — resampling a calibration subset perturbs less than redrawing a projection bank, and the audit is correspondingly easier. What it does establish is that the allocation our method ships is not an accident of which calibration examples were drawn.

The cliff-crossing stratum is a different and, we think, more interesting finding. Read as margins those cells say 22.697 to 29.997 points, which is why we do not read them as margins. Read correctly they say that the choice of sensitivity criterion can decide whether a same-budget allocation stays on the usable side of a quantization cliff. 

We can say what the collapses run through, and it is not arm selection. On 3 of 5 development models every one of the 15 arms falls below the threshold, including the post-hoc best and LieQ’s own two-level allocation rule. All 15 share one directional reading of the score, so this shows that no choice of allocation rule repairs the collapse — not that every reasonable reading of LieQ’s criterion fails. The immediate failure mode is the sign of the transferred score. LieQ’s ∆r measures how much more concentrated a trained projection’s output spectrum is than an untrained one, and its allocation rule treats a larger value as more sensitive. In these retrieval encoders the attention output projections give negative ∆r — the trained projection’s spectrum is flatter than the random reference — so read literally the criterion ranks every attention band least sen-sitive and the allocator gives them all the minimum width. The set of models whose attention bands are mostly nega-tive and the set whose LieQ allocation collapses coincide exactly (3 and 3 models, respectively). That is an associa-tion across five models, not yet a demonstrated cause; the two-sided steelman below tests it directly by re-running the criterion with the direction removed. The association alone could not distinguish two expla-nations — a criterion that genuinely fails to transfer, or a directional assumption meeting a sign the original setting never produced — so we tested it by intervention. We fixed a single additional score, S = |∆r|, before computing it: the untrained reference, the calibration inputs, the unit partition, the budget, the bit penalty and the allocator are all held at the signed arm’s values, and the definition together with a ban list on further variants was sealed as an amendment to the confirmation pre-registration. # 4.4 Cross-corpus transport 

Relevance-derived sensitivity was, in our earlier measure-ments, strongly dependent on which corpus produced it. Drift is much less so: module sensitivity measured on one retrieval corpus predicts the same quantity on another at 0.992, taken over the five development models. Table 7 separates the three things that could break — the sensitiv-ity ordering, the allocation decision, and the downstream score — because they can disagree. Matching sensitivi-ties with differing plans indicates an allocator threshold effect rather than an unstable signal; differing plans with matching NDCG indicates a flat optimum. Table 8 varies the probe over four corpora — SciFact, FiQA, ArguAna and NFCorpus — and evaluates every resulting plan on a fifth, SciDocs, which appears in no probe. Across the five development models the sensitivity ordering barely moves: the median pairwise Spearman over all module-by-bit cells is 0.992. Over all 10 cells, 80.0% of probe pairs select a byte-identical bit plan, with a worst-case held-out disagreement of 11.425 points; re-stricted to the 7 cells whose budget leaves a usable model, 78.6% agree exactly and the worst disagreement falls to 0.372 points. The stratification is by a criterion fixed ear-lier for the compression frontier — a cell is collapsed when the best probe retains under half of full-precision quality — and not by the domain-stability numbers themselves; both strata are reported so that the split can be checked. The one cell where plans differ appreciably is Embed-dingGemma at B3, where five of six pairs disagree yet the score spread is 0.372 points — an allocator threshold effect on a flat optimum, which is the benign shape. We read this as evidence that the sensitivity ordering does not depend on which retrieval domain produced it, within the budgets where the quantized model is usable at all. Stated as one sentence, what the three columns show is that sen-sitivity transport is stronger than plan identity, and plan disagreement need not imply retrieval disagreement : the ordering survives the change of probe almost intact, the allocator’s threshold turns small ordering differences into different plans more often than the ordering itself moves, and where the optimum is flat those different plans land on nearly the same score. 3 cells fall in the collapsed stratum and are marked in the table. Qwen3-0.6B at B2↔3 is the instructive one: probe pairs disagree by 11.425 points of NDCG@10, which looks like a large domain effect until one notes that the arms being compared score 4.02 and 15.44 against a full-precision 32.48. Both are unusable, so the disagreement is not a difference between two deployable systems. It is still informative: it says that domain robustness itself can break down once a budget has pushed a model past its utility cliff, which is why the collapsed stratum is reported rather than discarded. Summing single-module drifts predicts the drift of the whole configuration with a rank correlation of 0.939 but a median relative residual of 17.5% and a P90 near 48.0% (Table 20). That relative figure has a denominator artifact: configurations with small measured drift inflate |R|/D .Table 9 therefore reports the same disagreement on scale-free axes, and the picture holds — the mean absolute residual is 0.69 of the interquartile range of measured drift. The regression makes the shape of the disagreement precise. Fitting D = α + β ˆD + ε gives a compressive 

trend: β lies in [0 .26 , 0.77] across cells with R2 ≈ 0.84 .Composition error is therefore systematic rather than random — the sum consistently overstates how much a configuration will move, increasingly so for aggressive configurations. It is worth being precise about what this does and does not explain, because the obvious reading is wrong. The 

fitted mean trend α + β ˆD is monotone, which is why the global ordering is largely preserved; the relation itself 

is not monotone, because ε can and does reverse pairs. Measured directly, 9.9% of configuration pairs are ordered wrongly by the module sum overall, rising to 15.3% within the top quartile by retrieval quality and 12.2% inside the additive plan’s own neighbourhood. A monotone fit does not make the ordering exact, and the two facts are only consistent when stated together: the fitted mean trend is compressive and monotone, explaining why global ordering is largely preserved; non-negligible residuals nev-ertheless produce pairwise inversions, particularly among high-quality neighbouring configurations. 

The residual is largest exactly where the damage is largest: |R| correlates with the number of 2-bit modules and with predicted drift magnitude, and negatively with nominal bits per weight. The summary we take forward is that interactions are substantial in magnitude but usually insufficient to disrupt the global configuration ordering; they can still matter within narrow high-quality neighbour-hoods. We use rank-compositional for this property rather than “near-additive”, which overstates what the magnitude evidence supports. §6 shows the algorithmic consequence. We report β and R2 as descriptive statistics and delib-erately do not attach a significance test to β̸ = 1 . The configurations in each pool are not an i.i.d. sample: they are structurally generated and share module assignments heavily, so the sampling population an OLS t-statistic would refer to is not well defined here. Establishing signif-icance would require first fixing a configuration sampling design and then bootstrapping over it. 

# 6 Analysis: why is the allocator this simple? 

Appendix E asks the prior question — whether the gain comes from the signal at all rather than from the alloca-tor’s structure — with permutation and random-feasible nulls; the answer is yes on every development cell, and the subsections below take that as given. 

# 6.1 Does relevance supervision help? 

Our first design derived sensitivity from measured NDCG damage per module. On two models it was the weakest of seven signals at predicting held-out damage, its allo-cation lost to drift, and it did not clear a null built by shuffling the relevance judgements fifty times and running the allocator to completion on each. Controlling for drift, the partial correlation between label-derived sensitivity and true damage was approximately zero. We found one cell where labels produced the better allocation and no pre-allocation criterion that identifies such cells in advance. The accurate statement is therefore not that labels carry no information, but that relevance-derived sensitivity pro-vided no consistent incremental predictive value beyond drift under our tested allocator and calibration protocol ;labels are reported as an ablation and are absent from the method.

# 7 Related work 

Uniform and mixed-precision PTQ. Post-training quantization is surveyed in [10]. Its reconstruction branch optimizes rounding or weights against a layer- or block-wise output objective on a small calibration set, from AdaRound [23] and BRECQ [18] to Optimal Brain Com-pression [9]. For large transformers, weight-only PTQ is dominated by GPTQ [8] and activation-aware scaling as in AWQ [20]; LLM quantization more broadly adds outlier-, sensitivity- and incoherence-aware schemes such as LLM.int8() [4], SmoothQuant [32], ZeroQuant [36], SpQR [5], SqueezeLLM [16], OmniQuant [26] and QuIP [2]. These methods chiefly improve how a tensor is quantized at a given precision; they are orthogonal to how precision is distributed , and our quantizer is deliberately the plain round-to-nearest baseline so that the allocation signal is the only variable. Mixed-precision allocation adds a per-layer sensitivity score, classically the Hessian trace of HAWQ [7, 6] and its integer-only successor HAWQ-V3 [35], or a search policy with hardware feedback as in HAQ [30]. 

Quantizing transformer encoders. Encoder-only transformers were quantized well before generative LLMs: Q8BERT [37] applied byte-width quantization-aware train-ing, Q-BERT [27] assigned group-wise mixed precision from Hessian spectra, and I-BERT [15] made inference integer-only. All three are evaluated on classification and span-extraction benchmarks, where the task head sits on top of the encoder and absorbs part of the perturbation. An embedder has no such head: its pooled output is the product, which is why we observe it directly. 

Geometry- and information-based allocation. 

LieQ [33] allocates bits in sub-8B language models from layer-wise diagnostics. It motivates the criterion with a perplexity-drop measurement of functional saliency, but the deployed score is purely geometric and needs no per-plexity probing: for each projection it compares the spec-tral entropy of the layer’s output against the same in-put passed through an untrained weight matrix of the same initialization distribution, and reads the difference as training-induced energy concentration. Layers whose spectra training concentrated most are treated as function-ally irreplaceable and given the higher precision. This is the sharpest available contrast with our signal, because it is measured entirely on the full-precision model: nothing is quantized before the score is read. We quantize first and observe what moves in the output the retriever consumes. §4.2 puts the two criteria head to head under an identi-cal allocator and budget, so the comparison isolates the sensitivity criterion rather than the surrounding pipeline. 

Intervention-based allocation. InfoQ [1] is the closest prior work in mechanism. It quantizes each layer at each candidate bit width and scores it by the change in mutual information among the subsequent layers, then solves an integer program. Our allocator is the same program; our score is a different observable in a different setting — final embedding displacement rather than internal information flow, dense retrieval rather than image classification, and cross-domain transport of the score rather than a single calibration set. We do not claim intervention-based sensi-tivity as novel, and we regard an InfoQ-style adaptation as the baseline this paper most needs. One structural obstacle should be stated plainly rather than papered over. InfoQ’s full criterion relies on class-labelled task information and accuracy-based observer selection. Dense retrieval has pairwise relevance rather than a per-example class target, so there is no canonical one-to-one port of its 

I(L; Y ) branch. We therefore evaluate the directly trans-ferable information-flow component, and call the result an InfoQ-X adaptation rather than a reproduction. 

Interaction-aware allocation. CoopQ [42] models inter-layer dependence explicitly, estimating Shapley val-ues for layer sensitivities and their interactions and solv-ing a binary quadratic program over 2/4-bit assignments. MixQuant [21] makes the related point that a layer’s sen-sitivity depends on the bit widths of its upstream layers, and marginalizes each layer’s distortion over randomly quantized upstream configurations. Our composition mea-surement in §5 is a contrastive observation against this 12 line rather than a refutation: in the feasible region for text embedders the residual interaction is large in magnitude and systematically compressive, yet rarely reorders config-urations at the global scale, while remaining large enough to matter inside a narrow neighbourhood. We have not run an interaction-aware allocator head to head, and this measurement does not establish that we would beat one. 

Compression in dense retrieval. Most work labelled “quantization” in dense retrieval targets the output vectors or the search index rather than the encoder weights: JPQ trains the query encoder jointly with a product-quantized index under a ranking loss [38], and RepCONC learns discrete document representations via constrained clus-tering [39]. Index-side compression in libraries such as FAISS [12] and truncatable representations such as Ma-tryoshka embeddings [17] shrink the stored vectors, and late-interaction models such as ColBERT [14] move cost from the encoder to the index. Those methods compress what the encoder produces ; the setting here is weight-level mixed precision for the encoder itself, which is comple-mentary and composes with them. 

Prior work in one sentence. Existing methods pre-dominantly estimate sensitivity from intrinsic geometry, reconstruction error, or interaction-aware proxies, and where they do intervene they read an internal quantity; we measure the behavioural consequence of a quantiza-tion intervention on the embedder’s output representation, which is the object the retrieval metric is computed from. 

# 8 Limitations 

The most consequential limitations, in order: • Published baselines require adaptation, and one of them we could not accept. No published mixed-precision allocator is native to this setting: the candidates were designed for generative language models scored by perplexity, over per-layer units, with a per-example class target available. We therefore re-port a controlled LieQ adaptation, and we attempted an InfoQ-X adaptation under an estimator stability protocol fixed in advance. InfoQ’s own estimator is not substitutable — it estimates sliced mutual information with a KSG estimator over random one-dimensional projections, so a cheaper information proxy would be a weaker method wearing InfoQ’s name — and its I(L; Y ) branch has no canonical port to a setting without per-example class labels. The adaptation reached the original work’s 6,000-sample scale and did not satisfy the pre-specified criterion, so it was rejected before any retrieval metric was computed , not left unrun (Appendix B). What this costs the paper is concrete: the reader gets a con-trolled comparison against one prior criterion rather than two, and the second comparison is reported as a null on estimator reproducibility rather than on retrieval quality. InfoQ’s downstream quantization-aware training stage is omitted in any case, since every arm here is evaluated under identical PTQ-only quantization. Our own static-geometry operational-ization is not a published system, and its “post-hoc” column selects on the held-out set, so it is an upper bound for that family rather than a competitor. • LieQ-adapt is a controlled adaptation, not LieQ’s deployment format. LieQ allocates per layer and keeps precision uniform inside a layer so that standard kernels apply. We transfer its geometry criterion into our common twelve-unit packed-byte framework, which is what makes the sensitivity com-parison controlled, but it is not LieQ’s native setting. Table 17 reports its own two-level allocation rule separately for that reason. • At the main budget, drift does not beat uniform on the untouched models. The pre-registered hypotheses concerned LieQ and hold; the comparison a reader most wants, against the trivial allocation, goes the other way by about a point on all three (§4.3). The development table should be read with that confirmation next to it, not instead of it. • Domain stability is shown only on the develop-ment models. The four-probe measurement is now complete for all five development models (Table 8) and is strong: 78.6% of probe pairs agree on a byte-identical plan and the worst held-out disagreement is 0.372 points. It is nevertheless measured on the models the method was developed against; it was not part of the frozen confirmation matrix and has not been run on the untouched models, so “domain-stable” stays out of the title. • The predictability pool is not fully indepen-dent of the signal. On the three untouched models the random-only pool is the primary form and gives ρ between 0.611 and 0.908 — above the pre-registered 0.6 on all three, and weaker than the development models on two of them. • An earlier version of Equation 1 pooled queries and documents by count. # 9 Conclusion 

Quantization-induced output drift is a stable, retrieval-facing coarse sensitivity signal: it is deterministic given a calibration corpus, it reproduces under calibration resam-pling on every model we measured, on the development models it transports across retrieval domains in the usable regime, and it orders sampled allocations against held-out quality well over the broad sampled configuration pool. Under a pre-registered protocol on three embedders held untouched until the method was frozen, the allocation it produces avoids the severe misallocations that a trans-ferred signed-geometry criterion makes, and at the main 

B3 operating point it never collapses. The same protocol did not reproduce the development advantage over uni-form precision: at the healthy 3-bit operating point drift is numerically lower on all three models, and the decompo-sition shows it is lower having reduced module, held-out and whole-model drift exactly as designed. Drift is much better at identifying dangerous allocations than at discrim-inating among already-good ones. The result suggests that the next problem is not to find another global sensitivity proxy, but to decide when coarse representation preserva-tion is sufficient and when fine-grained, ranking-aware or configuration-level refinement is required.

# B InfoQ-X adaptation: estimator stability validation 

InfoQ’s full criterion combines the information flow from the input to an observer point with the information the observer carries about a per-example class target, and selects observer points by correlating each layer’s low-bit accuracy drop with its change in sliced mutual informa-tion. Dense retrieval supplies pairwise relevance rather than a per-example class label, so the I(L; Y ) branch and the accuracy-based observer selection have no canoni-cal one-to-one port; we evaluate the directly transferable information-flow component and refer to the result as an 

InfoQ-X adaptation rather than a reproduction. Every-thing outside the sensitivity metric — quantizer, allocation units, candidate bit widths, packed-byte budget, calibra-tion corpus and integer program — is byte-for-byte the configuration used by our own method, and the estimator is InfoQ’s own SMI with a KSG estimator over random one-dimensional projections. Projections are drawn from a shared bank across the full-precision and quantized passes, so the two SMI estimates are positively correlated and their difference has lower variance than independent sampling would give. An allocator consumes the ordering of module sensitivi-ties, so an estimator whose ordering does not reproduce across projection draws cannot produce a meaningful al-location regardless of how the resulting bit assignment scores. We therefore fixed a convergence criterion before running: two consecutive rungs of the ladder in Table 11 must each show a seed-pairwise Spearman median of at least 0.9 together with either byte-identical selected plans or a median plan Hamming distance of at most 1. The ladder increases projection count first at fixed sample size, then sample count at the largest projection setting, and stops at 6,000 calibration samples — the search budget reported in the original work. No retrieval metric is computed during this stage : the stability gate is re-solved first, and only an accepted estimator proceeds to a held-out NDCG comparison. This ordering exists so that a poor baseline score cannot be explained away after the fact as estimator noise, and equally so that a stable estimator that simply loses is reported as a proper baseline result. It did not satisfy our pre-specified stability crite-rion up to the original work’s 6,000-sample scale: changing the projection seed continued to alter a median of 4–9 of the 12 allocation decisions, and no rung produced a byte-identical plan for any seed pair. We therefore do not report unstable NDCG comparisons, in either direction. 

Two qualifications from the implementation au-dit. First, the pre-registered protocol states that a seed redraws only the projection bank. In the code that pro-duced Table 11 the seed also redrew which N of the cached calibration examples entered the estimate, so the rows with N < 6,000 measure projection noise and calibration-resampling noise together, and their downward trend as 

N grows should not be read as a clean statement about projections. The N = 6 ,000 row is immune — it uses every cached example, so the seed there changes the projection bank and nothing else — and it is the row the rejection rests on; the reported ceiling of 0.530 is a maximum over all rows, so the immune row’s own value cannot exceed it. The code was corrected to the pre-registered protocol, now stores the per-seed sensitivity vectors, and the full ladder was re-run on one development model (E5-base) as a development-only audit that does not reopen the pre-registered decision: the maximum seed-pairwise ρ over the seven rungs moved from 0.276 to 0.358, the N = 6 ,000 row reproduced its original value exactly (0.126 against 0.126), and no rung produced a byte-identical plan across seeds. Second, the estimator receives the one-dimensional projec-tions unstandardized. KSG’s joint radius is a Chebyshev distance, so a scale mismatch between the full-precision and quantized projections can move the estimate for rea-sons unrelated to information. The same audit re-ran the ladder with the projections z-standardised before the estimator: the maximum ρ is 0.324 and the N = 6 ,000 row 17 0.157, again with no reproduced plan. Neither correction approaches the pre-registered 0.9, so both qualifications are now closed: what was rejected is our adaptation of InfoQ’s estimator under this protocol, the rejection is not an artefact of either implementation choice, and we still do not claim that the estimator family is unusable here. The more precise reading of the ladder is that the true perturbation of information flow at three to four bits is small relative to the estimator’s finite-sample noise floor at the sampling scale the original work used.

Equation 1 weights the query and document sides equally. # D Reproducibility of our own sig-nal

# E Does the gain come from the sig-nal or from the allocator? 

Spending a fixed byte budget unevenly is itself a choice, and it is fair to ask how much of the improvement over uniform allocation comes from the drift signal rather than from the mere act of allocating non-uniformly. We answer it with two nulls, both computed at B3 on all 5 develop-ment models and both already part of the pre-registered protocol. The permutation null keeps the exact multiset of module sensitivities and reassigns them to modules at random, 200 times: the score distribution, the allocator, the budget and the candidate bit widths are untouched, and only the correspondence between score and module is destroyed. The random null draws 100 plans uniformly from the feasible set. Table 14 places the drift allocation in both distributions, and the two nulls should be read separately rather than merged into one claim. Against the random null the result is uniform: the drift allocation is at the 100.0th percentile on every one of the 5 models. Against the permutation null it is strong on four and ambiguous on one. The ambiguous model deserves a sentence in the body rather than a footnote. On EmbeddingGemma the per-mutation null’s median is itself positive, so on that model reallocating bytes unevenly beats uniform allocation even after the scores have been scrambled. The drift alloca-tion is still at the 89.5th percentile there, but some of the gain comes from heterogeneous spending itself rather than from identifying the correct sensitive modules , and we do not count that model as evidence that the criterion is doing the work. 1 of 5 models behaves this way. This is the same caution the compression-frontier analysis raises from the other direction: where a model is far from its utility cliff, many allocations are adequate and the choice of criterion matters less. 

# F How much score agreement does a reproducible allocation need? 

A gate that rejects a baseline has to be a gate something can pass. Our stability criterion asks for both a rank correlation and an agreeing allocation, and the second half of that turned out to need a control: an integer program over twelve units can be brittle, and if it is, “the plans never agreed” is a statement about the allocator rather than about the estimator that fed it. We therefore measured the allocator’s own response. Taking the measured drift scores from 4 development cells — a signal that is not in question — we added noise calibrated to reach a target rank correlation, drew two independent perturbations, ran both through the unchanged allocator at the same budget, and counted how often the two plans agreed. Table 15 is the result. Agreement is essentially guaranteed above ρ ≈ 0.9 and collapses below it: at 0.875 the two plans are identical 18 56.2% of the time, and by 0.730 exact agreement has already fallen under one in ten. Two things follow. First, the pre-registered threshold of 

0.9 was better calibrated than we knew when we fixed it — it sits at the knee of this curve, which is where a sensitivity signal stops being able to determine an allocation at all. Second, and against our own earlier framing, the InfoQ-X exact-plan rate of zero is not distinctive. The best rank agreement that adaptation reached is 0.530; at the control point nearest to it (0.589, the closest row of Table 15) the control signal itself agrees on only 6.2% of plan pairs, with a median Hamming distance of 4.0. The InfoQ-X rejection rests on the rank correlation, which never came close to the threshold; the plan statistic is a consequence of operating in a regime where no signal would reproduce an allocation. 

# G Is twelve allocation units an ar-bitrary choice? 

The twelve units — four attention layer bands, four FFN layer bands, four vocabulary bands — were chosen to keep the configuration space enumerable, and a reader is entitled to ask whether the results are an artifact of that choice. LieQ is natively layer-wise, interaction effects can be hidden by aggregation, plan stability is easier to achieve over fewer units, and the local-search topology de-pends on unit count, so the question touches four different claims in the paper. We therefore re-ran the whole mea-surement at six, twelve and twenty-four units on E5-base, holding every other setting fixed. This is a robustness check on one development model, not a tuning sweep, and it is deliberately not run on the untouched confirmation models. The sensitivity ordering is essentially granularity-invariant. Projected onto a common two-bands-per-family frame so the three settings are comparable, the pairwise rank correlation between granularities is at least 0.996. Whatever the criterion is measuring, it is not an artifact of how finely the model is cut. Retention does depend on the unit count, but not in the direction that would embarrass the choice: six units is too coarse to beat uni-form allocation at all (-0.072 points), twelve gains 0.397 points, and twenty-four gains 0.276 — less than twelve, at 3.7 × the six-unit probe cost, because the budget is spread over units too small for the integer program to place use-fully. Probe-to-probe transport is above 0.99 at all three settings. Twelve is therefore not a tuned optimum but the coarsest setting that is fine enough to help, and the criterion’s ordering would survive a different choice. 

# H Implementation audit 

Two passes over the implementation were made after the development results were in and before the untouched models were opened; the second pass covered the statisti-cal primitives, the monotone repair applied to sensitivities before allocation, and the InfoQ-X estimator. It found three genuine bugs, all in rank statistics, and none that changes a reported conclusion. The Spearman routine broke ties by array position rather than by average rank, which also returns a perfect correlation for a constant vec-tor; the Kendall routine used a tie-corrected denominator that is wrong whenever a pair is tied in both arguments; and the plan Hamming distance iterated over one plan’s keys only. All three were corrected to agree with the reference implementations, and every correlation in this paper whose inputs are stored in a ledger — 146 statistics across the predictability pools, the random-only pools, the pooling and transport agreements and the granularity checks — was recomputed with the corrected routines. The largest change is 0.0008 in ρ, none of them cross a pre-registered threshold, and no input vector is constant. The InfoQ-X ladder’s seed-pairwise correlations cannot be recomputed because the original runs stored the statistics rather than the per-seed vectors; the old routine inflates agreement when both vectors are tied at the same posi-tions and does not deflate it otherwise, so the corrected values can only be lower. The criterion-agreement statistic 

A(c) is defined on the raw module drift while the allocator consumes the monotone-repaired scores; recomputing A(c)

on the repaired bit-3 slice gives the same value in all 10 development cells, because the repair never touched that slice. The qualifications to the InfoQ-X result are stated in Appendix B. After the confirmation results were in, the three untouched B3 cells were re-evaluated with the ledger’s own bit plans on a different machine (Table 6); every arm agreed with the ledger to within 0.060 points, and on mxbai-large the LieQ-abs pipeline — a separate script — had already produced the uniform plan byte-for-byte with an NDCG identical to the uniform arm of the search pipeline to four decimals, which is the cross-path control for the uniform evaluation. The audit ledger, the corrected routines and their tests are in the released code.
