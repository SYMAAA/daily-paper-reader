## ABSTRACT 

The best two-bit weight quantizers for large language models, such as QTIP and Proteus, share a recipe. Each weight matrix is first rotated by a random orthogonal transform so that its entries look Gaussian, and this rotation has to be undone at every decoding step. The rotated weights are then encoded with a trellis or lattice code under a Euclidean search, and the layer Hessian is used only in the error-feedback step between coding blocks. We show that this recipe leaves part of the Hessian unused. Error feedback turns the loss into a weighted sum of per-coordinate rounding errors, and the weights, the diagonal of the Hessian’s LDL factorization, are computed by existing quantizers but never read. We put these weights into the search’s distance, the Viterbi branch metric, so that the search follows the curvature within each coding block. This also explains what the rotation is for. The rotation removes this within-block variation, so after it the weight has little left to exploit. Weighting in the native basis and rotating are therefore substitutes. We confirm this on three models: the weighted native search matches the baselines’ rotation, a full-dimension randomized Hadamard, to within about one point of downstream accuracy, and weighting after the rotation gains little. Around this search we build C URVE TQ, a trellis codec with no rotation. The rotation also flattened the amplitude and the marginal shape of the weights, which we handle directly with a factored scale field and a closed-form quantile table. In either basis, error feedback passes each block’s residual on to the next, so we store a start state per coding block that lets the trellis adapt to it. At two bits CURVE TQ is 1–3 points higher in mean downstream accuracy than QTIP and Proteus on three 4–8B Instruct models, even after both are given our start state, which by itself lifts either baseline by 1–3

points. It also leads on a 35B mixture of experts, to our knowledge the first trellis-coded result on such a model. With no rotation to undo, our decoder is the fastest of the three at all tested batch sizes and bit widths. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

When a large language model (LLM) generates text one token at a time, the cost of each step is dominated by reading the model’s weights from memory. Storing fewer bits per weight therefore directly translates into faster decoding. At four bits per weight, post-training quantization (PTQ) is by now routine (Frantar et al., 2023; Lin et al., 2024). The open frontier is two to three bits, where quantized models still lose a large amount of accuracy, and where the choice of the code , how groups of weights are jointly mapped to a small set of stored indices, matters more than the choice of the scalar grid (Tseng et al., 2024b; Egiazarian et al., 2024). The strongest two-bit trellis and lattice codecs today follow the same three-step recipe, which we call transform-and-flatten (Figure 1a). First, each weight matrix is rotated by a random orthogonal transform, so that its entries look like independent Gaussian samples (Chee et al., 2023; Tseng et al., 2024a). Second, the rotated weights are encoded with a high-dimensional code, a trellis or a lattice, using a Euclidean search and a Gaussian-shaped reconstruction. Third, the layer Hessian, which says how sensitive the layer output is to an error in each input coordinate, enters only through an error-feedback step between coding blocks (LDLQ). QTIP (Tseng et al., 2024b) is the canonical instance; Proteus (Yang et al., 2026) replaces its lookup table by a computed code but keeps the rotation and the Euclidean search. The recipe has a cost at every decoding step: the rotation has to be undone at inference time, either on the activations or on the weight-decoding path.

1arXiv:2610.09212v1 [cs.LG] 6 Oct 2026 What error feedback leaves unused. It is tempting to think that the feedback step has taken care of the Hessian, so that the search inside a block can safely be Euclidean. In fact, feedback only rearranges the loss of a row into a weighted 

sum of its per-coordinate rounding errors (Eq. 1). The weights are the diagonal D of the Hessian’s LDL factorization, the same quantity GPTQ divides its residuals by (Chee et al., 2023; Frantar et al., 2023). In the native basis these weights differ by orders of magnitude between input channels, because a few channels carry outlier activations. Yet, to our knowledge, every trellis and lattice quantizer ignores them and minimizes the unweighted error inside the block; QTIP computes their block form as part of its feedback step and never reads it. Our first step is simply to use them. Placed in the Viterbi branch metric, they change neither the code nor the decoder, and, as we show next, they make the rotation unnecessary. 

Why the rotation then becomes unnecessary. Once the weight is in the search, the random rotation turns out to act on the same quantity. The rotation is meant to make all coordinates look alike, and for the raw Hessian diagonal it does. For the LDL diagonal it does not: Figure 2(a) shows that it turns the jagged native weights into a smooth ramp along the coding order, nearly flat within one 64-column block. A weight that is constant within a block does not change which path the block’s search selects, so after rotation the weighted and the Euclidean search coincide: the rotation removes the structure the weight exploits . Proposition 1 relates the weighted search’s gain over the Euclidean one, in the high-resolution limit, to the within-block variation of these weights, which is large in the native basis and near zero after rotation. Weighting and rotating are therefore substitutes, which Section 4.2 confirms on three models. A rotation also flattens the local amplitude and the marginal shape of the weights; we handle both directly, with a factored group scale and a closed-form quantile table (Section 3.3), so the decoder needs no transform (Figure 1b). 

What else the block boundary changes. Taking the feedback block seriously has a second consequence, for the trellis termination. Under feedback, the first columns of each block carry the residual pushed forward from the previous block, so the boundary is where the search needs the most freedom. QTIP’s tail-biting trellis ties each tile’s first coordinates to its last, constraining exactly this boundary, and stores no start state; we instead let each block start in any trellis state and store the choice (Section 3.1). The 14 stored bits per block are 0.22 bpw, our largest single component, and not specific to our codec: given to QTIP and Proteus, the same state is worth 1–3 downstream points at two bits. Removing the state costs 2–4 perplexity and 5 downstream points, ten times the cost of a group-scale field of the same size. At decode time it is one load per block, 1–3% of throughput. 

Contributions. (1) We show that the curvature weight and the random rotation are substitutes: the LDL diagonal in the branch metric exploits what a rotation removes (Sections 3.1–3.2). On three models the weighted native search matches, to within about a point of mean accuracy, the full-dimension rotation QTIP deploys at equal rate (Section 4.2). The loss identity (Chee et al., 2023) and weighted trellis search (Fischer & Wang, 1992) are both known; what is new is that together they make the rotation unnecessary. (2) We replace QTIP’s tail-biting termination by a block-local trellis with a stored start state per block, our largest single rate component and the one that creates the two-bit downstream margin (Sections 3.1, 4.3). It is not specific to our codec: transferred unchanged to QTIP and Proteus, it lifts each by 1–3 points (Section 4.1). (3) Using (1), we build C URVE TQ, a rotation-free trellis codec; in the native basis, its per-module quantile table and factored scale field handle the marginal shape and the local amplitude that a rotation would have flattened (Section 3.3). Against QTIP and Proteus given our start state it is 1–3 points higher downstream at two bits on three Instruct models (Section 4.1). It also leads on a 35B mixture of experts, to our knowledge the first trellis-coded result on one; at 32B the start state alone closes most of the baselines’ gap. (4) With no rotation to undo, our decoder is faster than both baselines’ kernels at batch 1 to 128 (Section 4.4). 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Incoherence processing. QuIP (Chee et al., 2023) introduced random orthogonal preprocessing that makes weights and Hessians incoherent, together with LDLQ, the error-feedback rounding whose loss identity we use (Eq. 1); QuIP# (Tseng et al., 2024a) made the transform a randomized Hadamard and added the E8 lattice codebook, block LDLQ and a fine-tuning stage. Later work moves rotations into the activation path or makes them data-aware (Ashkboos et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025b; Sun et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2026a). Our view is that a rotation flattens three separate quantities at once, the local amplitude, the marginal shape and the block-local LDL diagonal, and that each can be flattened explicitly in the native basis. 

Trellis, lattice and vector codes. Two lines of work are closest to ours. The first is trellis-coded quantization. Its weighted and feedback variants (Marcellin & Fischer, 1990; Fischer & Wang, 1992; Atal & Schroeder, 1979; Eyuboglu & Qureshi, 1988) are classical; QTIP (Tseng et al., 2024b) brought TCQ to LLM weights with the bitshift trellis and two decoders, a lookup code and a computed one. For mixtures of experts, QMoE (Frantar & Alistarh, 2023) quantizes with one Hessian per expert, which we adopt; no trellis or lattice codec has been reported on such a model. Finally, post-quantization fine-tuning is worth more at two bits than the spread between any two codes (Malinovskii et al., 2024), and QuIP#, QTIP, AQLM and Proteus all publish their headline numbers after such a stage; to isolate the code, we run every method without it (Section 4). Appendix C discusses this literature in more detail.

Block-local trellis with an explicit start state. A trellis code must decide how one coding block’s path relates to the next. We let each block’s Viterbi start in any of the 2L states and store the chosen state, the boundary-state field bg

(the discrete counterpart of the amplitude field sg of Section 3.2), as L bits per (row, block). QTIP instead codes 256-weight tiles as tail-biting sequences: the first state is a function of the tile’s own last bits, so no start state is stored and the rate is exactly k (Tseng et al., 2024b). That construction is right for a long tile over a rotated, near-i.i.d. source. Under error feedback, however, the block boundary is where the inherited residual lands, and tying the first ⌈L/k ⌉ coordinates of a 64-column block to its own last ones is the wrong constraint. Group scale and quantile table. The scale of a 64-group is its absmax sg , stored on the factored grid described below as ˆsg ≥ sg ; the coder operates on zi = wi/ˆsg , so encoder and decoder share the normalization. Each coded linear layer has its own alphabet of N = 128 int8 values at the equiprobable quantiles of a sample of its normalized weights, indexed by a 7-bit hash of the emission window, with no interpolation at decode. The sample is a deterministic stride over the layer’s 64-groups, so the table is closed-form and the build is a deterministic function of the calibration set. A quantile grid is a heuristic rather than the rate–distortion-optimal reproduction law, but it matches an EM fit of the same 128 entries (Section 4.3). 

Emission window. QTIP emits from a 16-bit state and searches 216 survivors; we instead emit from the edge (st, a t)

of a 14-bit state (the same code family, and at k = 2 the same 16-bit window) and search 214 . This is the Mealy form (outputs on transitions rather than on states) of QTIP’s node-emitting trellis; an exact Viterbi run confirms identical costs and reconstructions on every tested row, with a quarter of the survivors. 

Table-free emission. The 128-entry table can be removed altogether: in the table-free variant the reconstruction is 

computed from the emission window, like QTIP’s 1MAD code, with one global scale in place of the table (details in Appendix I). Nothing else changes, so the difference between the two variants isolates the table (Appendix D). 

Factored scale field. A fp16 scale per 64 weights costs 0.25 bpw. Second-level quantization stores first-level scales on a low-bit grid with a fp16 constant per block (Dettmers et al., 2023; 2024), treating them as a flat sequence. We treat them instead as a two-dimensional field over rows and column groups, whose row and column-group effects carry much of the variance (about half over the linear layers of Llama-2-7B, by a two-way decomposition log 2 sr,g =

Table 1 reports the trellis methods at two and three bits and, at two bits, HIGGS and GPTQ, which fall far behind the trellis codecs (other rows and baselines in Appendix D; confidence intervals for every difference below in Appendix E). GPTQ is the only other rotation-free row: its scalar grid collapses at two bits, and its gap to our build is what the code is worth without a rotation. 

Two bits. At 2.29 bpw C URVE TQ has lower perplexity than QTIP and Proteus at 2.00 and 2.25 bpw on all four Instruct models. Downstream it leads them by +2 .5 to +5 .2 points on the three 4- and 8B Instruct models, all twelve contrasts excluding zero, and by +0 .5 to +1 .3 on Qwen3-32B (Table E.1). The +start rows add our per-block start state to the baselines’ own trellis. It is worth +1 .0 to +3 .3 points to them, more than the group scale, and it closes the perplexity gap on the Llamas and Qwen3-32B. The downstream margin survives: +0 .9 to +3 .1 points against QTIP +start and Proteus +start on the three models, five of six contrasts excluding zero. It is concentrated on generative tasks (GSM8K, IFEval), while multiple-choice tasks move by about a point. We implemented the decoder of Section 3.3 (row Decode + GEMM of Figure 1b) as a fused dequantize-and-multiply CUDA kernel and measured decoding on an RTX 3090 in QTIP’s own benchmark setting, at three bit widths and three batch sizes (Table 5; full table and setup in Appendix I, Table I.1). Our harness reproduces QTIP’s published fp16 figure on Llama-2-7B ( 52 .25 against 52 .5 tokens/s), so at batch 1 we quote their published QTIP numbers there; QTIP publishes no batch 64/128 figures, so those, and all of Qwen3-4B, we run with its released kernels; Proteus, which has no public kernel, is our implementation throughout. Three things follow. First, our decoder is the fastest of the three in every cell: at batch 1 it is 1.05 –1.09 × QTIP and 1.12 –1.36 × Proteus, and at batch 64 and 128 it stays ahead by 

1–13% . Second, the rotation is expensive at batch 1. Adding QTIP’s activation-side Hadamard to our own decoder, so that only the transform changes, costs 5–9 tokens/s on Llama-2-7B and 13 –17 on Qwen3-4B, 4–11% of throughput. That is most of, or more than, our whole margin over QTIP: with the rotation added our decoder is about as fast as QTIP’s ( 0.93 –1.02 ×), so at batch 1 most of the lead is the transform a rotation-free decoder does not pay for. Third, the stored start state is cheap to decode: removing it gains only 1–5 tokens/s, 1–3% , so the 0.22 bpw it costs returns 

1–3 downstream points (Section 4.1) at almost no cost in speed. 

## 5 CONCLUSION 

We showed that the curvature weight and the random rotation are substitutes: the LDL diagonal that error feedback already computes, placed in the Viterbi branch metric, exploits the within-block variation a rotation removes, and on three models the weighted native search matches the baselines’ rotation to within about a point. On this basis we built CURVE TQ, a trellis codec with no rotation to undo at inference, whose factored group scale and quantile table handle the amplitude and the marginal shape directly. Its block-local trellis with a stored start state also lifts QTIP and Proteus by 1–3 points; against them so strengthened, C URVE TQ is still 1–3 points higher downstream at two bits, leads on a 35B mixture of experts, and decodes faster at every batch size we tested. 9REFERENCES

What the native basis carries. The native basis is heterogeneous in three ways, and the codec meets each where it lives: the amplitude field sg carries local magnitude, the branch weight Di carries local sensitivity, and the boundary-state field bg of Section 3.1 carries the local non-stationarity that feedback itself creates at a block boundary. The last is not something a rotation removes: it comes from the feedback, which the baselines use as well, and the start state lifts them in the rotated basis too (Section 4.1). 

## C EXTENDED RELATED WORK 

What a rotation repairs. QuIP’s Table 2 shows that for a scalar grid at two bits, removing incoherence is a cliff: outlier input channels overwhelm a grid that has no local scale. That cliff is the amplitude and shape job of the rotation, which our group scales and quantile table do in the native basis; it is not evidence about the curvature job. Leech-lattice VQ (van der Ouderaa et al., 2026) reports the same from the other side: with a norm-preserving correction in place of plain GPTQ, dropping the rotation moves Llama-2-7B at two bits from 191 .9 to 6.9 perplexity, leaving the Hadamard worth about 0.2: part of what a rotation buys is compensation for a mismatch between solver and code, not a property of the weights. 

Trellis successors. Proteus (Yang et al., 2026) keeps QTIP’s rotation, block LDLQ and Euclidean Viterbi but re-places the lookup table with a computed Gaussianizing code, chosen per layer from a small candidate pool by a 16 Hessian-weighted selection objective (curvature enters the choice of code, not the branch cost), and then learns per-channel scales and distills per-layer affines. Its throughput advantage at large batch is attributed to codebook shared-memory traffic rather than to the rotation. Q-Palette (Lee & Song, 2025) derives the optimal bit allocation for Gaus-sianized weights and provides fractional-bit trellis, vector and scalar quantizers; BCJR-QAT (Iyengar, 2026) replaces the Viterbi argmax by forward–backward marginals so that the trellis can be trained through, and puts the curvature in the training objective. 

Fine-tuning, and what a published “two bits” contains. PV-Tuning (Malinovskii et al., 2024) holds the code fixed and varies only the fine-tuner: AQLM at 2.01 bpw on Llama-2-7B moves from 7.38 WikiText-2 perplexity with calibration alone to 6.69 , 6.41 and 5.90 as the fine-tuner strengthens. QTIP’s own end-to-end stage is worth about one perplexity point at 7B and about 0.2 at 70B. Converted through the no-fine-tuning rate slope this is 0.7–1.1 effective bits at 7B, more than the spread between any two codes we compare. Even tuning per-column scales alone moves QuIP# and QTIP by more than a perplexity point in a common pipeline (van der Ouderaa et al., 2026). QuIP#, QTIP, AQLM and Proteus all publish their headline low-bit numbers after such a stage, and the rates they publish differ in what they charge for its parameters and for metadata. We therefore fix the fine-tuning protocol at none for every method we run and compare at matched rate; how the margins change under a fine-tuning stage is left to future work. 

Other relevant lines. GPTQ’s column-wise feedback has recently been identified with Babai’s nearest-plane algo-rithm on the Hessian lattice (Chen et al., 2026b), whose error bound is a quadratic form in the same diagonal D that we place in the search. YAQA (Tseng et al., 2025) improves which Hessian is used and measures quantized models by their KL divergence to the original. ICQuant (Li et al., 2025) codes outlier positions by their index gaps; HIGGS (Malinovskii et al., 2025) derives MSE-optimal grids for Hadamard-rotated weights; NF4 is the Gaussian-quantile construction that our empirical quantile table generalizes. For mixtures of experts, MoEQuant (Hu et al., 2025) studies calibration imbalance across experts and recent work allocates bits across experts. Calibration-set sensitivity is docu-mented by Williams & Aletras (2024); for a deterministic codec its consequence is sharper, since the calibration slice is the only replicate axis. Quantization is known to hurt multi-step reasoning first (Li et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025a), which is where our two-bit gains concentrate.

How much of the margin is the extra rate? The T =256 variant also bounds how much of our margin over the baselines is bought with rate rather than designed, without any assumption about how perplexity trades against bits. It is our recipe with QTIP’s termination and QTIP’s tile length, storing no start bits and carrying a 14 -bit state against QTIP’s 16 , and it sits at 2.0685 bpw, 0.066 above the baselines’ published 2.003 and 0.219 below ours. At that rate it reaches 13 .38 perplexity against QTIP’s 15 .36 and Proteus’s 14 .21 . Our perplexity margin over QTIP on this model therefore splits into 1.98 from the weighted search, the quantile table and the factored scales, spent at 0.066 bpw, and 1.84 from the block-local start state, spent at 0.219 bpw (both halves measured against the same rebuild; 0.04 

of the 3.86 in Table 1 is the rebuild’s GPU difference). The two halves are comparable, so on perplexity the stored state is not what creates the gap—it is what doubles it. Downstream the split is one-sided in the other direction: at 

47 .7 points the variant is +0 .8 [−0.9, +2 .2] over QTIP and +0 .4 [−1.4, +2 .0] over Proteus with per-group scales, neither measurable, while the start state is worth +4 .2 [+2 .5, +6 .4]. Without a stored start state the other components recover perplexity at this rate but not accuracy; with one they deliver both. We report the variant as a control, not a configuration to use (it is 1.84 perplexity and 4.2 points worse than the build we ship), and it is run on Qwen3-4B only.

QTIP reports batch 1 only, so its batch 64/128 rows, and all of its Qwen3-4B rows (a model QTIP does not report), are our runs of its released kernels. Proteus has no public kernel, so all of its rows use a kernel written from its description, and its published throughput is measured on an H800, which we do not have. Its rows here should therefore be read as what that description yields in our harness on this GPU, not as a reproduction of Proteus’s published results; the anchored comparison in this table is the one against QTIP, whose released kernels we run and whose published fp16 figure our harness reproduces. QTIP runs its released HYB code ( L = 16 , V = 2 ), the code of its published results and kernels, for accuracy and throughput alike. QTIP and Proteus include the Hadamard transform on the activations that incoherence processing requires at inference; our decoder does not need one. Our decoder is a fused dequantize-and-multiply kernel: one warp per output row loads the block’s start state, walks the trellis, reconstructs each weight by one table read from the emission window (in the table-free variant, the (14+ k)-bit window is mixed by one multiply and one xor-shift and the four bytes of the result are summed, centred and divided by the exact standard deviation p4(256 2 − 1) /12 , giving a unit-variance code value; one global scale is then chosen by a nine-point line search on 16 calibration rows, so the stored table of 0.0001 bpw disappears), applies the group scale reconstructed from the factored field, and accumulates against the activation. The “ +rot.” arm of Table I.1 runs the same decoder with an activation-side Hadamard inserted before each quantized layer, following QTIP’s transform, so that its cost measures what incoherence processing adds to a decoder that would otherwise not need it; the “ −start” arm runs it with every block started from a fixed state, so that its gain measures the decode-time cost of the stored start state. Both arms are decode-only measurements: the resulting models are not the ones evaluated for accuracy. 

## J RATE ACCOUNTING AND STREAM AUDIT 

Rate accounting. Every bits-per-weight figure in this paper counts the k code bits, all scale side information, the emission table and the L-bit start state of every block; for QTIP and Proteus we add their reported 0.003 bpw of sign bits, marked +. The per-tensor arithmetic and a byte-level audit of the serialized stream follow. 

Why the start state and not a tail-biting block. QTIP instead codes 256-weight tiles as tail-biting sequences, whose first state is a function of the tile’s own last bits, so no start state is stored and the rate is exactly k (Tseng et al., 2024b). The construction is right for its setting: a long tile over a rotated, near-i.i.d. source, coded without weights, where the tile boundary falls on nothing in particular and the constraint removes only L/ 256 of the path freedom. Under error feedback it has a cost that the i.i.d. setting hides. The block is the 64-column feedback block, 31 so the constraint removes L/ 64 of the freedom; and the block is not stationary: its target ˜z carries the residual pushed forward from the previous block, concentrated on the first coordinates, and ωi varies across it. Tail-biting ties the first ⌈L/k ⌉ coordinates of a block to its last ones, so the coordinates that carry the inherited residual are the ones the search may not choose freely, and their error is passed on again. We measure this directly: with a tail-biting block the reconstruction error at the first block position is 2.6–2.9× the within-block mean, and lengthening the sequence by stacking rows improves it monotonically but has not reached the stored-state result by QTIP’s own tile length (15 .60 → 14 .62 → 13 .38 at T = 64 , 128 , 256 , against 11 .54 ; Table J.3): block length is part of the problem, but where the constraint falls matters as much as how much freedom it removes. The freedom is spent where the feedback puts the difficulty: on the built models the error at the first block position is 0.55 × the within-block mean and recovers to the mean by position eight. 

What is counted. For the +start baselines the stored stream of a 64-weight segment is the L-bit start state followed by the code bits; a node-emitting trellis emits from the state itself, so its last KV code bits are never emitted and the segment costs L − KV bits over tail-biting ( 12 per block for QTIP, V = 2 ; 14 for Proteus, V = 1 ; 0.19 and 0.22 bpw at two bits), whereas our edge-emitting trellis uses every code bit and is charged the full L = 14 . The reported rate splits into a payload term and the side information the decoder needs, R = Rpayload + Rscale + Rstate + Rtable , with the boundary adaptation rate Rstate = L/G (Section 3.1). Term by term: for a tensor of R rows and C columns coded at k bits with blocks of G = 64 , the reported rate is the sum of: k bits per weight for the trellis path; L/G = 14 /64 

bits per weight for the L-bit start state of every (row, block) pair (Section 3.1); 4/G bits for the int4 residual of the factored scale field, 16 /(G · 256) for its fp16 block absmax, 16 /C for the fp16 row factor and 16 ⌈C/G ⌉/(RC ) for the fp16 column factor; and 128 · 8 + 32 bits per tensor for the emission table. Both levels of the scale field are rounded up against the stored fp16 factors, so ˆs ≥ s holds exactly for every group; the builder checks that no normalized weight leaves [−1, 1] . The fp16-scale variant replaces the four scale terms by 16 /G . Summed over the coded tensors of each model this gives the “Reported” column of Table J.1; the start-state term is 0.219 bpw for every model, the rest varies with tensor shapes in the fourth decimal. Embeddings, output head, norms, router and vision tower stay in bf16 and are outside the reported rate, which covers the coded linear layers. For reference the table also lists the rate a row-long trellis with one stored state per row would have; that construction is evaluated in Section 4.3. The start state is stored in full: a variant that keeps the top L − b bits of the previous block’s end state and spends only the low b = 8 freely, at 0.125 instead of 0.219 bpw, saves 0.094 bpw and costs 0.51 , 0.43 and 0.44 perplexity at two bits on Qwen3-4B, Llama-3-8B and Llama-3.1-8B respectively. Every reported build stores all L bits.

Stream audit. To check the arithmetic against bytes, we instrumented the builder to serialize, for every tensor, exactly what a decoder needs: the k-bit symbol stream, the int4 residual codes packed two per byte, the fp16 block absmax, row and column factors, the 128 int8 knots with their fp32 step, and the start states. A separate routine reads those bytes back, regenerates the emission index through the mixer of Section 3.3, walks the trellis and rescales, and compares the result with the tensor the coder wrote into the checkpoint. Table J.2 shows the first decoder layer of Qwen3-4B at k = 2 : every component matches its formula to the byte, the full model is recovered bit-exactly from the stream, and the two decoding rows show that the per-block state is what a decoder needs: given one state per row instead, 10 .2% of the weights differ. The same check was run on the whole model and on the chained and tail-biting variants of Section 4.3 (Table J.3); all decode bit-exactly at their stated rates. Two further variants lengthen the tail-biting sequence by coding two and four consecutive rows of one 64-column block as a single sequence, leaving 32 feedback and scales per row; the second is QTIP’s length of 256. Both decode bit-exactly with zero stored start bits, their first and last states agree in all 252 modules, and both pack to the same 2.0685 bpw as the 64-length block. Perplexity falls monotonically with sequence length ( 15 .60 , 14 .62 , 13 .38 at T = 64 , 128 , 256 ), so roughly half of the tail-biting penalty is the shortness of the sequence. The remainder is not: at T = 256 (QTIP’s own tile length, and the length at which its rate argument is stated) the construction still trails a stored start state by 1.84 ppl. The two doublings return −0.98 and −1.24 , so we cannot claim the curve has saturated, only that it has not reached the stored-state result by the length the comparison method uses. With a 64-length tail-biting block the error at the first position of a block is 2.6–2.9× the within-block mean (above); lengthening the sequence dilutes that boundary term across more coordinates without removing it. Downstream the ladder does not halve: the two doublings return +0 .1 [−1.1,

+1 .2] and +0 .8 [−0.8, +2 .2] suite-mean points, neither measurable, against the start state’s +4 .2 [+2 .5, +6 .4]; at 

T = 256 the construction is still −4.2 points behind and loses on 31 of 34 tasks. The perplexity recovery is therefore not evidence that sequence length can substitute for the stored state, only that perplexity is the less demanding of the two measures here (Section 4.3).
