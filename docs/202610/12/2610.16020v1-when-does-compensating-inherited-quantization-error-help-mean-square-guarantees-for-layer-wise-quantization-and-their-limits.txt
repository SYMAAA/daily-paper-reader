When Does Compensating Inherited Quantization Error Help?
Mean-Square Guarantees for Layer-Wise Quantization and Their
Limits
Tianhao Guo∗
12 October 2026
Abstract
Layer-wise post-training quantization methods let each layer compensate for the quantiza-
tion error inherited from earlier layers. We ask under what assumptions such compensation
guarantees a network output error no larger than without it. For linear networks of any depth,
with the required calibration Gram matrices invertible, if the rounding error of each layer has
zero conditional mean and a deterministic second-moment map common to both schemes, as
under subtractive dither on an unbounded grid, then compensation with strengths in [0, 1] fixed
in advance gives a mean-square output error no larger than no compensation, each scheme be-
ing followed along its own trajectory. The exact gain separates error removed directly from
reduced excitation of later rounding noise. Outside this setting the comparison can fail. With
rectified linear activations it fails for two independent reasons: noise changes activation states,
and, even when no state changes, the correction moves error from samples on which a unit is
inactive to samples on which it is active. With stable activations, commutators between the
calibration projection and the activation patterns bound the excess. With a bounded codebook,
even unique exact layer-wise minimizers with no overload can give a larger output error. For
fixed rounding errors it already fails for two scalar linear layers, refuting a published universal
guarantee. An exact residual identity gives the necessary and sufficient condition on a common
layer input. All these comparisons hold the calibration input fixed. On two 8B models at 4 bits,
fixed half-strength compensation beat uncompensated rounding in every model, text and draw
tested, yet no rule for choosing the strength beat a layer-wise rule consistently, and lowering the
full-network loss on the data used to choose did not reliably lower it on new text.

Introduction
Layer-wise post-training quantization processes the linear maps of a network one at a time, choosing
for each a quantized weight matrix that reproduces the layer output on calibration data [2, 3, 4, 5].
Because every layer is treated separately, the error made in early layers reaches later layers through
their inputs. Several recent methods change the layer objective so that a layer matches the output
of the original network on the original input, while being fed the quantized input; the layer then
compensates for the inherited error. The same objective appears under the names quantization
error propagation [1], asymmetric calibration [6] and activation drift correction [17], and in [7, 16].

These methods share the motivation of reducing errors inherited from earlier quantized layers.
This raises a stronger question: under what assumptions does layer-wise compensation guarantee
that the network output error is no larger than without compensation? The answer is not obvious.
Each layer solves a least-squares problem, and the solution is closer to the target than the uncor-
rected one before rounding. After rounding this need not be so, and a comparison on a common
input says nothing yet about two schemes that, from the second layer on, see different inputs. That
is the question of this paper. The comparison requires us to follow both the inherited error and
the activation that multiplies the next rounding error. Reducing the first is not enough; the second
must be controlled along the two trajectories as well. Throughout, the calibration input is fixed
and the randomness is that of rounding; whether an improvement on calibration data carries over
to other inputs is a separate question, on which Section 9 reports experiments.
Related analyses exist. SPFQ [14] gives high-probability full-network error bounds for a stochas-
tic path-following quantizer. The analysis of OPTQ and Qronos [8] gives layer-wise bounds for both
algorithms and compares their error decompositions and upper bounds. These results do not es-
tablish an ordering of the realized total errors of a compensated and an uncompensated scheme.
We establish a network-level comparison under explicit noise assumptions, give the gain exactly,
and determine where the comparison fails. The fixed-error guarantee of [1] is one such failure. Its
mechanism is simple: the inherited error and the new rounding error can cancel, and removing
the first leaves the second without its counterpart. Whether compensation helps is therefore a
question about the correlation between the two errors, and the positive results below are obtained
by controlling it. We use the formulation of [1] to study a family of compensated layer objectives
parameterized by the strength. The related methods motivate the analysis; their quantizers, solvers
and regularization choices require separate treatment. QEP denotes the compensated scheme and
BASE the uncompensated one in the model studied here.
Contributions.
1. A comparison along two trajectories, and the gain it yields (Section 4). For linear
networks of any depth, with rectangular layers allowed, Theorem 8 gives mean-square output
error no larger with compensation than without it, for strengths in [0, 1] fixed in advance, under
the required Gram condition, zero conditional mean and a deterministic second-moment map
common to both schemes. The proof carries two moments through the layers, that of the error
and that of the quantized activation; the second governs how much the next rounding error is
amplified. Proposition 9 separates the gain into error removed directly and reduced excitation of
later noise. These compare compensation with no compensation. Ordering two non-zero strength
schedules requires additional row-space conditions (Corollary 10); the closed form of Example 11
gives a ratio of expected errors in the specified random-teacher model, not a prediction for every
fixed network.
2. Two obstructions for ReLU networks, and their geometric cost (Section 5).
The
comparison fails under the same dither when noise changes activation states (Example 12), and
also when no state changes (Example 14). In the latter case the correction mixes calibration
samples across activation patterns. Proposition 13 characterizes when this mixing is harmless
for all inherited errors.
Under stable activations, Theorem 15 bounds the excess by a term
controlled by commutators of the calibration projection with the sample groups; Corollary 16
gives its small-error order.
3. The local condition and the limits of the network comparison (Sections 3, 6 and 7).
An exact residual identity gives the necessary and sufficient condition for compensation to help

published guarantee (Section 6) and actual quantizers (Section 7).
Scope.
The tools are standard: orthogonal projections, dithered rounding and order relations
between second-moment matrices. The contribution is the comparison they yield and its limits.
We propose no new quantizer and do not dispute reported empirical gains. We have not established
stable activations in trained networks. The experiments of Section 9 do not satisfy our hypotheses
and are not tests of them. The projection contraction, continuous optimum and each scheme’s own
bound in [1] remain valid; Section 6 isolates the comparisons that fail.

Fixed rounding errors and the published guarantee
Sections 4 and 5 establish stochastic network comparisons under explicit assumptions. In contrast,
the guarantee published with QEP is asserted for arbitrary fixed perturbation matrices shared by
the two schemes. Under this deterministic convention, the comparison fails already for two scalar
linear layers. Here the corrected scheme denotes the idealized correction in that guarantee; actual
codebook minimizers are treated separately in Section 7.

To our knowledge the only published statement of this kind is the guarantee given with Quan-
tization Error Propagation (QEP) [1], which adds a closed-form correction to the weights before
quantizing them, scaled by a propagation strength αl ∈[0, 1]. Theorem 5.2 of [1] states that the
output error of the propagated scheme is bounded by that of the uncorrected scheme; its formal
version, Theorem B.5, states that a Lipschitz upper bound UQEP on the output error satisfies
UQEP ≤UBASE for any fixed perturbation matrices and every choice of strengths. Proposition 5.4
adds that the output error decreases monotonically as each αl approaches one. In the published
discussion of the paper the authors describe Theorem 5.2 as “a rigorous guarantee that solving
the layer-wise local optimization problem . . . reduces the final quantization error”1 and use it to
distinguish QEP from a concurrent method with the same local objective.

Relation to experiments
The comparisons of Sections 3–7 hold the calibration input fixed; the randomness is that of round-
ing. A quantized model is used on other inputs, and whether an improvement obtained on cali-
bration data carries over to them is a different question, on which those results say nothing. This
section reports what we observed on both questions: published observations, a layer-wise diagnostic
on two small models, and experiments on two 8B models. None of the experiments satisfies the
hypotheses of our theorems, and they are not tests of them.
9.1
Published observations
Table 1 of [1] reports WikiText-2 perplexity for 60 pairs (four base methods, five models, three
bit widths) with and without QEP. In 53 pairs QEP is better. In 7 pairs it is worse, all at 2 bits:
RTN on Llama-2-7B and Llama-2-13B, GPTQ on Llama-2-13B and Llama-3-8B, and AWQ on
Llama-2-7B, Llama-2-70B and Mistral-7B; in all seven the perplexity is above 103 both with and
without QEP. These experiments use αl = 1

reports that it becomes “increasingly unstable in deeper layers” of larger models, and chooses a per-
layer coefficient between drift-corrected and original calibration statistics by search; in most deeper
layers of the Qwen3-8B configuration they report, the selected coefficient is close to the uncorrected

9.3
Choosing the strength on two 8B models
Setting.
We quantized Qwen3-8B and Meta-Llama-3-8B with our own implementation of a QEP-
style correction on top of round-to-nearest; it is not a reproduction of QEP, GPTQ, AWQ or QuIP#.
The seven linear maps of each transformer block are quantized to 4 bits with an asymmetric
uniform codebook (integer codes 0–15, a scale and zero point per row and group of 128 input
channels); embeddings, normalizations and the output head stay in BF16, and the dequantized
weights are executed in BF16, so no speed is claimed. The maps are processed in dependency order
in four groups (query–key–value, attention output, gate–up, down), maps that share an input being
quantized together; each policy follows its own quantized trajectory, and the inputs of every group
are taken from the actual modules. For a group with quantized input Z and teacher input X,
the correction is fitted by ridge least squares with λ = tr(ZZ⊤)/d, the mean eigenvalue of the
Gram matrix, and every corrected candidate is re-quantized with recomputed scales and rounded
deterministically. The ridge matters for reading the results: the matrix Pλ = Z⊤(ZZ⊤+ λI)−1Z
that replaces P is not a projection, and a direction in which the Gram matrix has eigenvalue s is
compensated in proportion αs/(s+λ). “Strength 1” below means external strength 1 with the ridge
retained; on the fitting inputs and before re-quantization, in a direction with s = λ it compensates
half of the inherited error.
For each model we drew, three times, twelve disjoint 512-token windows from WikiText-2 train-
ing text: four (2048 tokens) to fit the corrections and eight (4096 tokens) to choose strengths. Eval-
uation uses the first 16384 tokens of the WikiText-2 test text and of Pride and Prejudice (Project
Gutenberg 1342, with the wrapper removed and the front matter kept), in non-overlapping 512-
token windows with the context reset for each window; neither enters any decision. Each model
uses its own tokenizer. The policies are: no correction (RTN); strength 1
2 in every group; strength
1
2 with the down projections uncorrected; and four rules that choose, for each group, a strength
in {0, 1
2, 1} on the selection data, by the output error of the group (local), the error after the next
block kept at RTN (block), the loss of the full network with the remaining blocks kept at RTN
(network), or a first-order estimate of that loss from its gradient with respect to the block output,
applied to the re-quantized candidates (gradient). The replication protocol, including all seven
policies, was fixed after an exploratory round on validation text and before any of the reported
evaluations.

time of the local rule in our prototype.
More selection data.
To see whether the selection data were simply too few, we kept the 2048
fitting tokens of two of the draws, enlarged the selection data in nested sets of 4096, 16384 and
32768 tokens, added a separate 8192-token diagnostic set from the same source that enters no
decision, and evaluated on new windows (tokens 65536 to 81920) of both texts. These runs record
the loss of both final policies on every data set, so the same pair can be compared with the same
metric on different data. On Llama-3-8B with 4096 selection tokens, averaged over the two draws,
the network policy has a lower loss per token than the local policy on the selection data, by 0.0210,
and a higher one on the diagnostic data (+0.0084), on WikiText-2 (+0.0069) and on the novel
(+0.075): the ordering of the two policies reverses when the data change. With more selection
data the advantage on the selection data shrinks (−0.0116 at 16384 tokens, −0.0087 at 32768) and
so does the deficit elsewhere, to +0.0025 on WikiText-2 and +0.030 on the novel, behind in every
draw on the novel. On Qwen3-8B the network rule is ahead at every size, most at 16384 tokens
(−0.033 and −0.018) and less at 32768. Selection by the output error of the current block does not
help consistently either. The candidates themselves were always fitted on the same 2048 tokens,
so these results do not separate the quality of the candidates from the accuracy of choosing among
them.
Where the overestimate lies.
Measuring each policy against RTN separates the two rules. For
a policy, take its gain over RTN on the selection data minus its gain over RTN on the diagnostic
data; the difference between the two policies’ values is the change of their ordering reported above.
With 4096 selection tokens, averaged over the two draws, this overestimate is +0.022 on Llama-3-
8B and +0.034 on Qwen3-8B for the network rule, and −0.007 and −0.006 for the local rule. A
further run with the fitting data of the first draw, two new selection draws and 32 source windows
used by no policy gave the same ordering in all four cases, with 0.029 to 0.049 for the network rule
and 0.008 to 0.022 for the local rule. The network rule chooses by the loss of the full network on the
selection data, which is also the quantity reported on those data; the local rule chooses by a linear
output error. A small standard error does not exclude the overestimate: on Llama-3-8B in the first
draw the network rule is ahead of the local rule on the selection data by 0.025, about six standard

errors over the eight windows, and behind on the diagnostic data. The overestimate is comparable
in the two models; the gains are not. On the selection data the network rule is ahead of the local
rule by 0.052 on Qwen3-8B and by 0.021 on Llama-3-8B, and only the first survives. Fixed strength

for every decision is not the explanation. In the same run, giving each decision eight windows not
used before in its path, with everything else fixed, did not reduce the overestimate of the network
rule: summed over the decisions and measured on a fixed set of eight held-out source windows, it
went from 0.031–0.043 with reused windows to 0.070–0.083 with fresh ones, and the mean final loss

not the choice, at each decision, of the candidate that scores best on the very windows on which
its gain is then measured. This run used a protocol, predictions and a stopping rule fixed before
inference; fresh windows also change the amount of text seen, so it does not isolate reuse at an
equal data budget.
Changing the suffix.
Choosing group by group, each rule above scores a candidate with the
later blocks still at RTN, and then changes those blocks. To see whether this explains the failures,
we replayed 48 recorded decisions (two draws per model; three blocks per draw, four groups each;
the groups of a fourth, final block serve as a control) with the earlier state and the three candidates
fixed, scoring them once with the RTN suffix and once with the corrected suffix reconstructed
from the recorded decisions. In 11 of the 48 the preferred candidate on the selection data reverses
by more than 10−4 in loss; none of these 11 reversals recurs, for the same pair and in the same
direction, on the diagnostic data. Dependence on the suffix exists, but we found no evidence that
it accounts for the losses on new text.

9.4
What the experiments do and do not show
On a fixed calibration input, Sections 4–7 give conditions under which compensation cannot hurt
and mechanisms by which it can. In the experiments, compensation helped on average relative
to no compensation, and on the small models the cancellation of Section 6 was not observed
with the rounding error held fixed; we did not measure the cross term on the 8B models, so
we neither confirm nor exclude it there.
The held-out comparisons reveal a further limitation:
24


gains measured on fitting or selection data need not persist on other inputs. A correction that
lowers a layer residual on calibration tokens raises it on others, and a policy that is better than
another on the selection data can be worse on new text. This is separate from the two increases
on calibration tokens observed after re-rounding with dither in the small-model diagnostic, and
from the fixed-input failures of Sections 6 and 7. We have not identified the cause of the held-
out losses.
The overestimate is larger for the rule that chooses by the reported quantity, and
reusing the selection data across decisions does not explain it; the finite fitting data, the size and
domain of the selection data, the ridge and the interaction between layers are not separated by
these experiments. Calibrating quantized weights against the output of a whole block rather than
a single layer is not new [28, 29]; our network rule goes further and scores candidates by the loss
of the full network. What these experiments add is a measurement, in one setting, of how far
such choices carry over to new text. The scope is narrow: two models, two or three draws, two
texts that may appear in pretraining data, windows rather than independent documents, our own
RTN-backed implementation and dequantized execution.

Limitations and open questions
• The theorems compare the two schemes on a fixed calibration input. They say nothing about
new inputs, where the largest losses we observed occur (Section 9); the overestimate we measured
is larger for the rule that selects by full-network loss, but reuse of the selection data does not
explain it and we have not established the cause. The cross term c/d was measured only on two
small models with single-layer interventions; how often 2c > (2 −α)d occurs in larger models, at
lower bit widths or along each scheme’s own trajectory is not known to us.

• The composite inequality ∥δQEP

• At the network level, Theorem 8 covers linear networks and Example 12 shows that the mean-
square comparison fails for ReLU networks. Theorem 15 bounds the cost of non-commuting
projections for ReLU networks but requires stable activations, which we have not established
for any trained network; a statement that tolerates a small probability of activation changes is
open, and other activations are not treated. How E(UQEP)2 and E(UBASE)2 compare for linear
networks under uniform dither is not settled in this paper.
• We have not analyzed damping, although the experiments of Section 9 use it. With a ridge
term, T −X = (Z −X)(I −αPλ) still contracts the inherited error on the fitting inputs, before
re-quantization, but the inequality for the activation moment in the proof of Theorem 8 does

Related work
RTN, GPTQ [2], AWQ [3] and QuIP [4] are the layer-wise methods to which QEP is applied in [1];
QuIP# [5] extends QuIP with lattice codebooks. GPTAQ [6] adopts an asymmetric calibration
objective of the same form and is the method from which the authors of [1] distinguish their
work by the guarantee examined here.
Qronos [7], NestQuant [16] and WaterSIC [17] use the
same propagated objective, and [15] studies the residual introduced by compensation itself. Error
analyses that account for the quantization of earlier layers exist: SPFQ [14] gives full-network
error bounds for a stochastic path-following quantizer, and [8] gives error bounds for OPTQ and
Qronos and compares the two bounds. These are high-probability or worst-case bounds for specified
algorithms; they do not assert that, realization by realization or for the whole network, correction
is never worse than no correction, which is the statement examined here. For a single layer, the
information-theoretic limits of quantizing for a matrix product are characterized in [18], with nested-
lattice constructions, and under an i.i.d. Gaussian weight model at high rate [17] reports a rate gap
of about 0.255 bit to the information-theoretic limit. The independence of dithered quantization
error from the input goes back to [19]; see [20, 21]. Subtractive dither has been applied to the
compression of pretrained network weights [9], and stochastic rounding to low-precision training
[10]; neither work compares corrected and uncorrected layer-wise quantization. Treating active
and inactive ReLU samples differently in layer-wise reconstruction, with a cascade analysis, goes
back to Net-Trim [11]. Quantizing a residual after predicting from an already quantized past is
the structure of predictive coding and noise shaping, where the effect of noisy prediction and the
limits on feedback gain are classical [12, 13]. Closer still are the classical analyses of finite-word-
length effects. In roundoff-noise analysis of digital filters the output noise is computed through
Gramians and minimized over realizations [24], and error feedback is designed to reduce it; the
noise gain is then a quadratic function of the feedback, with a gain term and a cross term and an
optimal strength [25]. The per-layer identity of Section 3.1 has this form and is elementary. The
second-moment recursion of the uncorrected scheme is that of a linear system with multiplicative
noise, and the propagation of orderings by positive maps is standard in that theory [26].
The
27


theory of dither is surveyed in [27]. What is specific here is that the correction is a projection
determined by the realized activations of the corrected scheme, so that it is neither a fixed feedback
filter nor a fixed state transition, and that two trajectories are compared; the step to be proved
is that this correction reduces both the error moment and the moment that excites later noise.
That error feedback can amplify correlated errors is therefore not new; what we establish is a joint
comparison of error and activation moments along two realized trajectories, its exact gain, and the
limits imposed by nonlinear geometry and finite codebooks.
13
Conclusion
Under the hypotheses of Theorem 8, compensating inherited quantization error with strengths
fixed in advance does not increase the mean-square output error of a linear network. The proof
compares the schemes along their own trajectories through both error and activation moments; the
exact gain separates direct error removal from reduced excitation of later rounding noise. Stronger
compensation is no worse under additional row-space conditions, with a closed-form depth gain
in a specified model. Outside that setting we exhibit several ways in which the comparison fails.
For ReLU networks, stable activations alone are insufficient; commutators between calibration
projections and activation-pattern partitions bound the excess. For fixed perturbations, the per-
layer comparison depends on the error cross term.
With actual finite codebooks, even unique
exact layer-wise minimizers without overload can yield a larger output error. Local improvement is
therefore not a network guarantee. All of this holds the calibration input fixed; on two 8B models,
the harder part was carrying an improvement on calibration data over to new text.
