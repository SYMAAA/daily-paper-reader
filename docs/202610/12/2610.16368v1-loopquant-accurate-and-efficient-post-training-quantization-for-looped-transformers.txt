ABSTRACT
Looped Language Models (LoopLMs) improve parameter efficiency by recur-
sively reusing shared Transformer modules, yet their repeated computation over
evolving recurrent states poses new challenges for low-bit deployment. Exist-
ing post-training quantization (PTQ) methods, largely designed for feed-forward
Transformers, fail to account for cross-loop activation dynamics, heterogeneous
loop sensitivity, and recursive error propagation, leading to substantial perfor-
mance degradation at low precision. To address these challenges, we propose
LoopQuant, a loop-aware PTQ framework for accurate and efficient low-bit
quantization of LoopLMs.
LoopQuant is built on three core components: 1)
Cross-Loop Distribution Canonicalization (CLDC) learns a shared quantization-
friendly representation across recurrent states while preserving parameter shar-
ing; 2) Interaction-Aware Loop-wise Precision Allocation (ILPA) allocates ac-
tivation precision under a fixed bit budget by modeling task-level loop sensi-
tivity and cross-loop interactions; and 3) Loop-Conditioned Task Calibration
(LCTC) further aligns quantized inference with the low-bit recursive trajec-
tory through lightweight task-space calibration.
Extensive experiments across
LoopLM architectures demonstrate that LoopQuant consistently outperforms ex-
isting low-bit PTQ methods. On Ouro-2.6B, LoopQuant under W4A4 surpasses
all W4A8 baselines in performance, while reducing model memory by 65.3%
with only 3.4% additional storage overhead over naive INT4 and achieving a
1.7× decoding speedup over BF16. These results demonstrate a strong balance
among accuracy, compression, and inference efficiency, providing a practical and
deployment-friendly solution for low-bit LoopLM inference. The code is avail-
able at https://github.com/Kishon-zzx/LoopQuant.

INTRODUCTION
Transformer-based large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated strong capabilities in lan-
guage understanding, code generation, and complex reasoning. However, these gains come with
larger model sizes, higher inference costs, and increased memory access, making deployment chal-
lenging in memory- and bandwidth-constrained environments. Rather than simply scaling model
size, Looped Language Models (LoopLMs) recursively reuse the same Transformer modules across
multiple loops (Zhu et al., 2026), increasing effective computation depth under a nearly fixed pa-
rameter budget. This parameter sharing reduces storage while maintaining strong representation and
reasoning capabilities, making LoopLMs attractive for constrained deployment. However, shared
modules are repeatedly executed, with weights and intermediate activations frequently accessed
across loops. Low-bit quantization of both weights and activations is therefore important for reduc-
ing computation and data movement costs. Achieving efficient low-bit quantization while preserving
recursive reasoning capabilities remains a key challenge for practical LoopLM deployment.
Post-training quantization (PTQ) enables efficient LLM deployment by reducing storage and compu-
tation costs without expensive retraining. Existing methods employ weight reconstruction, activation
clipping, channel scaling, rotation transformations (Xiao et al., 2023; Ashkboos et al., 2024), and
mixed-precision allocation (Saxena et al., 2024) to enable low-bit weight and activation quantiza-
tion for conventional Transformers. However, most target feed-forward Transformers, where layers

Transformer modules are repeatedly invoked across loops, while evolving recurrent states expose
them to different activation distributions. Quantization perturbations from earlier loops also prop-
agate through recursive computation, altering subsequent hidden states and quantization behavior.
LoopLM quantization must therefore account for cross-loop activation dynamics, loop-dependent
quantization sensitivity, and recursive error propagation. As shown in Fig. 1, directly applying ex-
isting PTQ methods can cause substantial performance degradation at low bit-widths.
To further understand the core challenges of low-bit PTQ for LoopLMs, we identify three key is-
sues. ❶Activation distributions vary substantially across loops. As hidden states evolve, the
same shared module receives different activation distributions across loops, with varying dynamic
ranges, outlier magnitudes, and outlier channels. Static quantization parameters calibrated on a sin-
gle distribution may fail to accommodate all recurrent states, while separate configurations for each
loop introduce additional storage and deployment overhead. ❷Loops exhibit heterogeneous im-
portance and quantization sensitivity. Although all loops share the same Transformer parameters,
they operate at different recurrent stages and contribute differently to the final prediction. Quantiza-
tion errors introduced in earlier loops can propagate through later computation. Uniform activation
precision may therefore waste the bit budget, while allocation based only on local quantization errors
cannot reflect the true impact of each loop on the final output. ❸Local quantization objectives are
misaligned with recursive task behavior. Even when the local quantization error is small at one
loop, low-bit computation can shift the hidden state and alter the input distributions of subsequent
loops. Such deviations can accumulate along the recursive trajectory and cause substantial output
errors. Accurate LoopLM quantization therefore requires joint modeling of cross-loop activation
dynamics and loop sensitivity, followed by calibration along the actual low-bit recursive trajectory.
To this end, we propose LoopQuant, a loop-aware PTQ framework for accurate low-bit LoopLM
inference. ❶Cross-Loop Distribution Canonicalization (CLDC) learns a shared quantization-
friendly representation across recurrent states through orthogonal-scaling transformation, robust
cross-loop synchronization, and group-aware channel reordering, while preserving parameter shar-
ing. ❷Interaction-Aware Loop-wise Precision Allocation (ILPA) allocates A4/A8 activation pre-
cision under a fixed bit budget by estimating task-level loop sensitivity and modeling cross-loop in-
teractions induced by recursive error propagation. ❸Loop-Conditioned Task Calibration (LCTC)
further calibrates quantization behavior along the actual low-bit recursive trajectory through task-
space defect transport and Fisher-weighted trajectory calibration, while optimizing only lightweight
loop-conditioned clipping, gain, and bias parameters. Together, they act on activation distributions,
precision budgets, and recursive quantization behavior to enable accurate and deployment-friendly
low-bit inference for LoopLMs. Our key contributions are summarized as follows:
• We identify three key challenges in low-bit PTQ for LoopLMs: cross-loop activation dy-
namics, heterogeneous loop sensitivity, and the mismatch between local quantization ob-
jectives and recursive task behavior.

RELATED WORK
2.1
POST-TRAINING QUANTIZATION FOR LARGE LANGUAGE MODELS
Post-training quantization (PTQ) compresses pretrained models without expensive end-to-end re-
training, reducing the storage, memory access, and inference costs of LLMs (Zhao et al., 2026a;c).
Recent advances have further explored aggressive low-bit compression and quantization-based ac-
celeration across diverse Transformer architectures (Zhao et al., 2026d; Xu et al., 2026; Zhao
et al., 2025b). For low-bit weight–activation quantization, activation outliers remain a major ob-
stacle to accurate quantization, particularly under stringent precision budgets (Zhao et al., 2026b).
SmoothQuant (Xiao et al., 2023) applies channel-wise scaling to shift quantization difficulty from
activations to weights, while QuaRot (Ashkboos et al., 2024) uses orthogonal rotations to redis-
tribute hidden-state outliers while preserving full-precision outputs. SpinQuant (Liu et al., 2025)
further learns quantization-friendly rotations. More recent methods strengthen these transforma-
tions: FlatQuant (Sun et al., 2024) learns flexible affine transformations for weights and activations,
while DartQuant (Shao et al., 2026) performs distribution-aware rotation calibration with efficient
orthogonal optimization. Despite strong performance on standard Transformers, these methods typ-
ically calibrate each layer from local activation distributions and do not explicitly model evolving
cross-loop activation dynamics or loop-dependent sensitivity of recursively shared modules.
2.2
LOOPED LANGUAGE MODELS
Looped Language Models (LoopLMs) increase effective computation depth by recursively reusing
shared Transformer modules, decoupling model capacity from parameter count and enabling
parameter-efficient scaling (Dehghani et al., 2018). This paradigm has been adopted by modern
LLMs. Relaxed Recursive Transformers (Bae et al., 2025) reuse shared Transformer layers with
lightweight adapters to mitigate the expressiveness limits of strict parameter sharing. Huginn (Geip-
ing et al., 2026) adopts a recurrent-depth architecture and scales test-time latent computation through
recursive applications of shared modules. Ouro (Zhu et al., 2026) extends LoopLMs to large-scale
pretraining, iteratively refining latent representations to improve reasoning under a fixed parame-
ter budget. Recently, Nanbeige4.2-3B (Lab et al., 2026) employs a Looped Transformer trained
from scratch, reusing a shared layer stack to increase effective depth and showing strong perfor-
mance on reasoning, coding, and agentic tasks. Low-bit LoopLM quantization has also received
attention. LoopQ (Fang et al., 2026) provides an initial study of cross-loop distribution shifts and re-
cursive quantization errors in Recursive Transformers. Nevertheless, evolving cross-loop activation
dynamics, heterogeneous loop-wise quantization sensitivity, and error propagation along recursive
trajectories remain distinct challenges for LoopLM quantization and motivate our work.

Overview.
In this section, we first formulate low-bit quantization under recursive computation
(Sec. 4.1), decomposing task degradation into loop-dependent local errors, cross-loop sensitivity and
interactions, and trajectory-dependent errors. Building on this formulation, LoopQuant consists of
three stages (Fig. 2): CLDC learns a shared quantization-friendly representation across recursive
states (Sec. 4.2); ILPA allocates activation precision by modeling loop sensitivity and cross-loop
interactions under a fixed bit budget (Sec. 4.3); and LCTC calibrates quantization behavior along
the low-bit recursive trajectory (Sec. 4.4). Pseudocode is provided in Appendix A.1.

EXPERIMENTS
5.1
EXPERIMENT SETUP
Models and Datasets.
We evaluate LoopQuant on representative LoopLMs, including Ouro
(with thinking-enabled models) (Zhu et al., 2026), Huginn (Geiping et al., 2026), and Nanbeige4.2-
3B (Lab et al., 2026). We report WikiText2 and C4 perplexity (PPL) (Merity et al., 2016; Raffel
et al., 2023), together with seven zero-shot tasks: ARC-Challenge/Easy (Clark et al., 2018), Hel-
laSwag (Zellers et al., 2019), LAMBADA-openai/standard (Paperno et al., 2016), PIQA (Bisk et al.,
2019), and WinoGrande (Sakaguchi et al., 2019). We further evaluate on reasoning and genera-
tion benchmarks, including MMLU (Hendrycks et al., 2021), GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021), and
HumanEval (Chen et al., 2021).
Baseline Methods.
We compare LoopQuant with representative low-bit PTQ methods, including
SmoothQuant (Xiao et al., 2023) with channel scaling, QuaRot (Ashkboos et al., 2024) with orthog-
onal rotation, SpinQuant (Liu et al., 2025) with learnable rotation, FlatQuant (Sun et al., 2024) with
affine transformation, and DartQuant (Shao et al., 2026) with distribution-aware rotation calibration.
BF16 serves as the full-precision reference. We further compare with LoopQ (Fang et al., 2026);
since its code is not publicly released, we report the results in its original paper in Appendix A.3.5.
Implementation Details.
We use per-channel symmetric quantization for weights and per-token
symmetric quantization for activations.
For fair comparison, all methods use GPTQ (Frantar
et al., 2022) as the weight quantization backend and 128 sampled WikiText2 sequences of length
1024 for calibration (Appendix A.3.6). CLDC optimizes loop-shared parameters with Adam (Ap-
pendix A.3.1), and LCTC details are provided in Appendix A.3.2. ILPA uses exact enumeration for
fewer than 5 loops and 100 Monte Carlo permutations otherwise. All experiments are conducted on
NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 GPUs, and accuracy is evaluated with lm-eval (Gao et al., 2024).

5.4
EFFICIENCY ANALYSIS OF LOOPQUANT
Memory Savings.
We evaluate the storage efficiency of LoopQuant against BF16 and naive INT4
RTN across three LoopLMs, as shown in Fig. 3 (left). LoopQuant reduces overall model memory
by 53.7–65.3% compared with BF16, while the quantized linear layers alone achieve approximately
74% memory savings. Compared with naive INT4 RTN, LoopQuant introduces at most 3.4% ad-
ditional memory overhead, largely preserving the storage efficiency of standard 4-bit quantization
(Appendix A.3.3). Despite this negligible overhead, LoopQuant substantially mitigates C4 perplex-
ity degradation caused by naive INT4 and achieves performance close to BF16.

Inference Speedup.
We evaluate LoopQuant inference efficiency with PyTorch on NVIDIA RTX
PRO 6000 GPUs, using one GPU per model. With batch size 4, we measure decoding throughput
by generating 1,024 tokens after prefilling 4,096 tokens. As shown in Fig. 3 (right), LoopQuant
improves throughput by 1.5–1.7× over BF16 across three models. Although naive INT4 attains
slightly higher throughput, it suffers severe accuracy degradation; LoopQuant preserves most INT4
efficiency gain while maintaining zero-shot accuracy close to BF16.

CONCLUSION
We present LoopQuant, a loop-aware PTQ framework for low-bit LoopLM quantization.
LoopQuant addresses cross-loop activation dynamics, heterogeneous loop sensitivity, and misalign-
ment between local objectives and recursive task behavior. CLDC learns a shared quantization-
friendly representation, ILPA allocates loop-wise activation precision under a fixed bit budget, and
LCTC calibrates quantization along the low-bit recursive trajectory. Across LoopLMs, LoopQuant
consistently outperforms low-bit PTQ methods; W4A4 matches or surpasses W4A8 baselines while
preserving reasoning capability. It delivers substantial memory savings and decoding speedup with
negligible storage overhead, enabling accurate and deployment-friendly low-bit LoopLM inference.

ETHICS STATEMENT
This work studies LoopQuant, a post-training quantization framework for improving the deploy-
ment efficiency of Looped Language Models. Our experiments are conducted on existing pretrained
models and publicly available calibration and evaluation benchmarks. Reducing memory and infer-
ence costs may broaden access to LoopLMs, but greater deployment efficiency can also facilitate
unintended or harmful uses. Quantized models may inherit biases, factual inaccuracies, and unsafe
behaviors from their full-precision counterparts, and low-bit quantization may alter model behavior
in ways not captured by standard accuracy benchmarks. Our experiments evaluate predictive per-
formance and system efficiency rather than safety, fairness, privacy, or robustness guarantees. We
therefore recommend application-specific evaluation and appropriate safeguards before real-world
deployment, together with compliance with the licenses and usage policies of the underlying models
and datasets.

containing the LoopQuant implementation, quantization and evaluation scripts, and experimental

A.1
PSEUDOCODE OF LOOPQUANT
This section provides the detailed pseudocode of LoopQuant. The complete quantization proce-
dure consists of three stages: Cross-Loop Distribution Canonicalization (CLDC), Interaction-Aware
Loop-wise Precision Allocation (ILPA), and Loop-Conditioned Task Calibration (LCTC). CLDC
learns a shared quantization-friendly representation across recursive states, ILPA determines loop-
wise activation precision under a fixed bit-width budget, and LCTC further aligns the quantized
model with recursive task behavior through task-space calibration.

recursive activation distributions through CLDC, then allocates loop-wise activation precision with
ILPA under a fixed bit-width budget, and finally performs task-aligned calibration along the low-bit
recursive trajectory through LCTC.

A.1.2
CROSS-LOOP DISTRIBUTION CANONICALIZATION (CLDC)
CLDC aims to construct a shared quantization-friendly representation across recursive states while
preserving the parameter-sharing structure of LoopLMs. It first learns a shared canonical geometry
from all loop-wise calibration states, then reduces cross-loop distribution mismatch through robust
synchronization, and finally performs group-aware channel reordering to improve group-wise quan-
tization compatibility.
Algorithm 2 summarizes the CLDC procedure. CLDC first learns a shared canonical geometry
across recursive states, then aligns cross-loop activation distributions through robust synchroniza-
tion, and finally improves group-wise quantization compatibility with channel reordering. The re-
sulting transformation is shared across all loops and remains fixed for subsequent ILPA and LCTC
stages.
A.1.3

ILPA determines loop-wise activation precision under a fixed bit-width budget. It first evaluates the
task impact of different precision configurations along the recursive trajectory, then estimates loop
sensitivity and cross-loop interaction, and finally allocates activation precision through a budget-
aware optimization procedure.
Algorithm 3 summarizes the ILPA procedure. ILPA first evaluates precision configurations on the
complete recursive trajectory, then identifies loop-wise sensitivity and cross-loop interaction, and
finally allocates activation precision under the target bit-width budget. The obtained precision con-
figuration remains fixed during inference and is used by the subsequent LCTC stage.

A.1.4
LOOP-CONDITIONED TASK CALIBRATION (LCTC)
LCTC performs the final task-level calibration after CLDC and ILPA. It keeps the quantization struc-
ture and precision assignment fixed, and only optimizes lightweight loop-conditioned calibration
parameters along the low-bit recursive trajectory. The calibration objective jointly considers teacher
alignment, language modeling loss, clean-state task defects, and trajectory-dependent defects.
Algorithm 4 summarizes the final calibration procedure of LoopQuant. LCTC does not modify the
quantization structure or precision allocation. Instead, it optimizes lightweight loop-conditioned
parameters to correct task-space quantization defects and trajectory-dependent error amplification.
After calibration, only static calibration metadata are retained in addition to the quantized model;
temporary optimization states are discarded.

ADDITIONAL EXPERIMENTAL DETAILS
A.3.1
CLDC OPTIMIZATION DETAILS
For each quantized linear layer, CLDC learns a loop-shared diagonal scaling and block-diagonal or-
thogonal transformation to construct a unified quantization-friendly representation across recursive
states. The scaling parameters are parameterized as logarithmic channel vectors, where the actual
scaling factors are obtained through exponential mapping and clipped to [10−3, 103] for numerical
stability. The orthogonal transformations are parameterized by block-diagonal Cayley maps with a
block size of 32. The Cayley parameters are initialized to zero, corresponding to identity transfor-
mations, and incomplete final blocks are masked according to their actual dimensions.
Both scaling and orthogonal transformation parameters are jointly optimized using Adam with a
learning rate of 1 × 10−2 for 80 steps. No learning-rate scheduler is applied. Gradient norms are
clipped to 1.0, and the parameters achieving the lowest optimization objective are retained.
A.3.2
LCTC OPTIMIZATION DETAILS
LCTC optimizes only the loop-conditioned calibration parameters Γm,t while keeping quantized
weights, CLDC transformations, ILPA precision assignments, and backbone parameters frozen. The
teacher-alignment term is implemented as the token-averaged KL divergence between the teacher
and student output distributions without temperature scaling (i.e., τKD = 1).
The interpolation coefficient α used in the trajectory-aware defect estimation is sampled from
{0.5, 1.0, 1.5} with probabilities (0.25, 0.50, 0.25). For the first loop, the trajectory term is disabled
since no inherited quantization drift exists.
The clipping parameters are initialized through local reconstruction search. Specifically, for each
module and loop, we select the clipping ratio from {0.50, 0.51, . . . , 1.00} that minimizes the recon-
struction error on calibration activations. The gain parameters are initialized to one and the bias
parameters are initialized to zero.
During calibration, the loss weights remain fixed: λNLL = 0.1, λa = 0.1, λt = 0.03, and
λreg = 10−4. The anchor and trajectory losses are normalized by their corresponding pre-calibration
reference values before being combined with other optimization objectives.

A.3.5
COMPARISON WITH LOOPQ
LoopQ Fang et al. (2026) is the first work that studies low-bit quantization for recursive Trans-
former architectures, making it the closest related approach to LoopQuant. Both methods recognize
that recursive computation introduces quantization challenges beyond conventional feed-forward
Transformers. However, they focus on different aspects of the problem.
LoopQ mainly investigates cross-loop distribution shifts and recursive quantization errors, and im-
proves quantization robustness through loop-aware calibration. In contrast, LoopQuant develops
a more comprehensive framework that jointly considers three key challenges in LoopLM quanti-
zation: cross-loop activation dynamics, heterogeneous loop sensitivity, and the misalignment be-
tween local quantization objectives and recursive task behavior. Specifically, CLDC learns a shared
quantization-friendly activation geometry across recurrent states, ILPA performs loop-wise activa-
tion precision allocation by modeling task sensitivity and cross-loop interactions, and LCTC further
calibrates quantization behavior along the low-bit recursive trajectory.
To further compare with LoopQ, we report the results originally published in the LoopQ paper and
compare them with our corresponding results. Since LoopQ does not provide publicly available
implementation and complete experimental configurations, we cannot perform a fully controlled
reproduction. Therefore, we only compare the overlapping models and evaluation metrics reported
by both works.
Table 6 presents the comparison between LoopQ and LoopQuant under different low-bit quantiza-
tion settings. Under the more challenging W4A4 setting, LoopQuant consistently achieves better
robustness on both Ouro-1.4B and Ouro-2.6B. For example, on Ouro-2.6B, LoopQuant improves
the four zero-shot metrics from 68.60/62.27/56.80/50.34 to 75.07/66.75/65.88/56.09, while reduc-
ing WikiText2 perplexity from 15.89 to 8.27. These results indicate that LoopQuant better preserves
model capability under extremely low activation precision.
Beyond the quantitative comparison, the two methods adopt different optimization perspectives.
LoopQ mainly focuses on mitigating distribution shifts introduced by recursive execution, whereas
LoopQuant further models loop-wise precision sensitivity and task-level error propagation along
the complete recursive trajectory. By jointly optimizing activation geometry, precision allocation,
and trajectory-aware calibration, LoopQuant provides a more comprehensive solution for low-bit
LoopLM deployment.
