Fast Quantized Johnson–Lindenstrauss Transforms
Angelos Korakitis∗
Vasileios Nakos†
Abstract
Quantization has emerged as one of the primary techniques for reducing the memory foot-
print of large language models during inference. A prominent example is Quantized John-
son–Lindenstrauss (QJL), which compresses KV caches using 1-bit asymmetric quantization
while approximately preserving the inner products required by the attention mechanism. Re-
cent theoretical and practical work aims at replacing the dense Gaussian projections used by
the aforementioned transforms with more structured embeddings — such as compositions of
randomized Hadamard transforms — while retaining the same embedding dimension.
In this paper, we introduce two constructions based on a composition of randomized Hadamard
transforms, albeit with a Gaussian rather than Rademacher diagonal in the second stage. The
first adds Gaussian dithering to smooth the quantizer, while the second inserts a random per-
mutation to regularize the covariance structure of the resulting Gaussian measurements.
We prove embedding-dimension and running-time guarantees analogous to those of Fast
JL in the non-quantized setting. The first construction achieves m = O(ε−2 log2(d/δ)), while
the second achieves m = O(ε−2 log(d/δ)) under a restriction on m. Both constructions can be
applied in O(d log d) time.

Introduction
Vector quantization (VQ) in Euclidean space has emerged as a crucial technique in the fine-tuning
and deployment of AI models, as well as in vector databases, which heavily operate on similary
search. The core idea behind VQ is to keep low-bit representations of high dimensional vectors,
often obliviously to the considered data, such that quantities of interest, including inner products
or mean squared errors (MSEs), are retained with minimal error.
VQ becomes particular significant in transformer-based architectures, which face a significant
challenge due to the self-attention mechanism. The memory footprint of the Key-Value (KV) cache
can often be a bottleneck; though the expressiveness of high dimensional vectors drives much of
the success of LLMs, storage issues may prevent further scalability. This has lead researchers to
the following question: how can we compress keys, values and queries without sarcificing too much
accuracy? This would (and has) allow(ed) LLMs to process much longer context windows and
support larger batch sizes without meeting the barrier of current GPU memory.
Additionally, the VQ framework is of direct application to nearest neighbor search in vector
databases [Qdrant, 2026, Pg, 2026, Pinecone, 2026], where searches are often performed with respect
to inner product or cosine similarity measure [Jegou et al., 2011, Gao et al., 2025, Ge et al., 2013].
Retrieval-augmented generation and information retrieval have become ubiquitous, as they allow
AI models to access external data, up-to-date data. These techniques allow factual groundings, cost
savings (as expensive re-training can be bypassed, since local files can be accessed dynamically)
and semantic matching.

A significant building block behind VQ is the application of a uniformly random rotation to
the input vectors and then quantizing down to a small number of bits. This approach has led
to consider quantized analogues of the the Johnson-Lindenstrauss transforms, with asymmetric
inner product estimators for KV cache compression [Zandieh et al., 2025]. The aforementioned
embeddings, namely QJL and the 4-bit analogue PolarQuant [Zandieh et al., 2025, Han et al., 2026],
are behind the recent success of Google’s Turboquant [Zandieh and Mirrokni, 2026]. The general
pipeline of applying a random linear transform followed by coordinatewise scalar quantization has
a long history [Charikar, 2002, Dong et al., 2008, Vargaftik et al., 2021, 2022, Chee et al., 2023,
Tseng et al., 2024b,a, Ashkboos et al., 2024, Gao and Long, 2024, Gao et al., 2025, Zandieh et al.,
2026].
These embeddings achieve a favorable balance between dimensionality reduction and the er-
ror introduced, but are typically dense and therefore slow to apply. Thus, both researchers and
practicioners have suggested alternative constructions, such as composing series of Randomized
Hadamard transforms (RHTs), which allow embedding in O(d log d) time [Agarwal et al., 2024,
Zandieh et al., 2026, Vargaftik et al., 2022, 2021, Feng et al., 2026, Ben-Basat et al., 2026b, Tseng
et al., 2024a, Le et al., 2013, Dasgupta et al., 2011, Ailon and Chazelle, 2009, Sarlos, 2006, Krah-
mer and Ward, 2011, Ordentlich and Polyanskiy, 2025, Koneˇcn`y et al., 2016, Dorfman et al., 2023,
Caldas et al., 2018, Ben Basat et al., 2025, Basat et al., 2024, Andoni et al., 2015]. Recent work
[Ben-Basat et al., 2026b] has shown that composing two such RHTs yields a transform T such that
Tx is close in Kolmogorov distance to randomly rotating x.
In this work, we continue this line of research by designing Quantized Johnson-Lindenstrauss
transforms which enable O(d log d) time and have analogous guarantees to the renowned Fast JL
transforms in the unquantized case. Our constructions are careful twists of the aforementioned
framework of composing two RHTs and provide results which, to the best of our knowledge, our
new, and are incomparable to the work of [Ben-Basat et al., 2026b].
Our contributions.
We introduce two variants of a fast asymmetric quantized JL estimator. The
first, Dithered Fast Quantized Johnson–Lindenstrauss D-FQJL, uses dithered quantization, while
the second, Permutation-based Fast Quantized Johnson–Lindenstrauss P-FQJL, combines RHTs
with a random permutation. The sketch used in our permutation-based variant is the structured
transform previously considered in other settings [Dasgupta et al., 2011, Le et al., 2013]. Our
objective and analysis, however, are quite different, as we argue that the covariance of gaussian
ensembles arising throughout is close to the identity.
Both constructions retain the asymmetric structure of QJL: one vector is stored as an m-
bit binary sketch obtained by applying a 1-bit quantizer to each of the m projected coordinates,
whereas the other is represented by an m-dimensional floating-point sketch. If both projected
vectors were sign-quantized, their bit agreements would instead give an unbiased estimator of their
angle [Charikar, 2002]. Recovering the inner product would then require applying the cosine function
to the estimated angle and multiplying by the vector norms; this nonlinear plug-in step is generally
biased. Quantizing only one vector avoids this issue and yields the sign–linear identity needed for
exact unbiasedness. It also reduces the storage for a pair of vectors from 2m floating-point numbers
(FPNs) to m + 1 FPNs and m bits. The additional scalar stores the normalization or quantization
scale associated with the binary sketch.
Our structured representation further reduces the space required to store the sketching op-
erator. Whereas a dense m × d Gaussian matrix requires O(md) our construction requires only
O(m + d) space. Moreover, the algorithm is data-oblivious: the sketch is sampled independently of
the keys, values, and queries, and therefore requires no data-dependent training, optimization, or
2


preprocessing.
We prove inner-product estimation guarantees for both variants and show how they can be
applied directly to KV-cache compression. A cached key can be stored as an m-bit binary sketch
while the corresponding query is transformed to a compact representation.
Our results also connect TurboQuant and EDEN/Drive randomized quantization methods [Zandieh
et al., 2026, Vargaftik et al., 2022, 2021, Ben-Basat et al., 2026a]. Both lines of work use or advocate
RHTs for fast practical implementations. However, directly replacing the Gaussian projection in
the asymmetric sign-linear estimator by HD± does not, in general, preserve exact unbiasedness:
the Gaussian sign identity underlying QJL no longer applies, and the RHT substitution can be
biased on worst-case inputs. Our constructions address this gap by retaining fast-transform com-
plexity while recovering an exactly unbiased asymmetric estimator. Our embeddings are twists of
compositions of two RHTs, similarly to [Ben-Basat et al., 2026b], see Related work for more details.
1.1
Related Work
KV-cache compression.
Prior work reduces KV-cache costs through direct low-bit quantiza-
tion [Liu et al., 2024], rotation-based outlier suppression [Ashkboos et al., 2024, Liu et al., 2025],
and randomized quantization designed to preserve geometric quantities such as inner products or
distances [Zandieh et al., 2025, 2026, Gao and Long, 2024]. Our work follows the third direction,
replacing dense random projections with fast structured transforms. A complementary approach
compresses the sequence dimension by retaining only selected cached tokens [Kochetkova et al.,
2026].
Randomized Hadamard and fast orthogonal transforms.
RHTs provide fast, hardware-
efficient mixing in O(d log d) time [Agarwal et al., 2024] and are widely used to reduce coherence
before quantization [Ashkboos et al., 2024, Liu et al., 2025, Chee et al., 2023]. Their structured
output is more difficult to analyze than a dense random rotation, and classical theory therefore
focused mainly on JL and subspace-embedding guarantees [Ailon and Chazelle, 2009, Ailon and
Liberty, 2013, Krahmer and Ward, 2011, Sarlos, 2006]. More recent work studies uniform approxi-
mation and/or quantization guarantees for Gaussian-diagonal or randomized Hadamard construc-
tions [Cherapanamjeri and Nelson, 2022, Feng et al., 2026, Schuchman, 1964, Ben-Basat et al.,
2026b, Zilca and Mendelson, 2026]. In [Ben-Basat et al., 2026b] in particular the authors prove
applying that the composition of two RHTs yields a vector the marginal distribution of every fixed
coordinate of which is close to Kolmogov distance to the normalized rotated: the gap is O(d−1/2)
and is incomparable to our guarantee. For a thorough practical discussion of this framework see
also the blog of Christiani [Christiani, 2025].

2.2
Attention Mechanism
We now review the attention mechanism used by autoregressive transformers. Given a sequence of
tokens, the model produces three embeddings for each token position: a query, a key, and a value.
We denote these embeddings at position i by qi, ki, vi ∈Rd, respectively. These embeddings allow
us to derive the contextual representation of each token.
To produce the contextual representation of a specific token, the model compares its query with
all keys in the current context, producing a vector of similarity scores. These scores are normalized
by the softmax function into a vector of attention weights, or attention scores. The contextual
representation of the token is then the convex combination of the value vectors, weighted by these
attention scores.
Formally, at every step j, in order to generate the (j + 1)-st token, define the attention-score

As is apparent from Equation (2), evaluating attention at step j requires access to every key and
value embedding generated so far. These are stored in what is commonly known as the KV cache,
so that they need not be recomputed at each subsequent step. Its memory footprint grows linearly
with j and, kept in full precision, can become substantial for long contexts. Likewise, computing the
attention scores involves j inner products of dimension d, giving a per-token complexity of O(jd).
It is precisely this cost of storing Kj and computing Kjqj that motivates our work: we reduce the
memory footprint of the KV cache using quantization and dimensionality reduction.

Fast Quantized Johnson–Lindenstrauss Transforms
We now develop fast structured analogues of the asymmetric quantized Johnson–Lindenstrauss
estimator. The goal is to replace the dense Gaussian projection used in QJL by a transform that
can be applied in nearly linear time, while preserving the same asymmetric storage format: the
key is stored only through quantized sketch values and its Euclidean norm, whereas the query is
sketched at evaluation time.

underlying our constructions and explain why it has the correct Gaussian marginals. This yields

3.4
Space Complexity
The memory cost has two distinct components: the shared sketching operator and the vector-
dependent representations. QJL uses a dense Gaussian matrix in Rm×d and therefore requires md
FPNs to represent the operator.
In both D-FQJL and P-FQJL, the Hadamard matrices are implicit. The shared D-FQJL oper-
ator stores only the Gaussian diagonal g ∈Rd, the random signs defining D±, and the m sampled
indices defining SI. The dither is generated while quantizing a key and need not be stored. P-FQJL
stores the same quantities together with d integer indices specifying the permutation Π. Thus, both
operators require O(d+m) storage.
For either construction, representing a key–query pair requires an m-bit code and one FPN
for the key norm, together with an m-dimensional floating-point sketch of the query. Thus, the
vector-dependent representation uses m bits and m + 1 FPNs. As a consequence, a cache of n keys
requires nm bits and n FPNs, in addition to the single shared structured operator; the query sketch
is computed on demand and need not be cached.

Connection to TurboQuant
TurboQuant and the earlier DRIVE/EDEN line of work use random rotations to spread vector
energy before quantization [Zandieh et al., 2026, Vargaftik et al., 2022, Ben-Basat et al., 2026a].
Applying a dense rotation requires O(d2) time per vector, so practical implementations instead use
an RHT, which can be applied in O(d log d) time.
TurboQuant constructs a b-bit representation in two stages. The first stage uses (b −1)-bit
quantization to obtain an approximation ex of the input x. It then forms the residual r = x −ex and
encodes it using 1-bit QJL, producing an m-bit residual sketch. This residual correction is designed
to improve the mean squared error while approximately preserving inner products.
The theoretical QJL analysis uses a projection with independent Gaussian rows and relies on
Lemma 1. In its experiments, however, TurboQuant replaces this Gaussian projection with an RHT
for computational efficiency. Although this substitution performs well empirically, it does not auto-
matically inherit the unbiasedness guarantee: the rows of HD± are structured Rademacher vectors,
and orthogonality alone does not imply the Gaussian sign identity. This theoretical distinction is
also noted in [Ben-Basat et al., 2026a].
Our constructions address this theoretical gap using Gaussian–Hadamard transforms that retain
1-bit quantization while preserving the exact sign–linear expectations required for unbiasedness.
The resulting residual sketch still contains m bits in total, but the transform can be applied in
O(d log d) time. Our constructions therefore provide provably accurate fast replacements for Tur-
boQuant’s dense residual QJL stage.
6
Conclusion
In this paper we provided fast quantized Johnson-Lindenstrauss transforms with corresponding
asymmetric estimators. Our work provides running time and embedding dimensions analogous to
ones in fast Johnson-Lindenstrauss transforms, proving that the power of dimensionality reduction
persists under scalar quantization.
