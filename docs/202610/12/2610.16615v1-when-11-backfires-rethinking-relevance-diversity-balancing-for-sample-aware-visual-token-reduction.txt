Abstract
Visual token compression has emerged as a critical technique for
reducing the inference cost of Vision-Language Models. Existing
methods select tokens based on text relevance, visual diversity,
or a fixed combination of both—treating the relevance-diversity
balance as a universal constant independent of the input. We con-
duct a systematic analysis to challenge this assumption. At the
dataset level, grid-searching the optimal relevance-diversity weight
per benchmark reveals substantial cross-task variation. More crit-
ically, we further maximize per-sample answer likelihood to re-
cover sample-level oracle weights, finding that their distributions
not only differ markedly across benchmarks but also exhibit high
within-benchmark variance. Based on these observations, we pro-
pose SAVER, a framework for Sample-Aware relevance-diversity
balanced Visual tokEn Reduction. We first derive a Geometric
Intent Prior (GIP) from the spatial concentration of text-relevant
tokens to infer a sample-specific relevance-diversity weight, and
then apply this weight to jointly select tokens via a Weighted Text
Centroid (WTC) for relevance scoring and a Visual Marginal Cov-
erage Gain (VMCG) for diversity. SAVER requires no additional
training and achieves sample-aware balancing entirely at inference
time. Extensive experiments demonstrate that SAVER consistently
outperforms state-of-the-art baselines across diverse benchmarks,
retaining 96.5% of full-model performance on LLaVA-1.5-7B with

Introduction
Vision-Language Models (VLMs) [2, 5, 7, 14, 19, 22, 23, 25, 26, 30, 37],
which jointly process visual and textual inputs, have achieved sig-
nificant progress across a wide range of multimodal tasks, including
visual question answering, image captioning, and document un-
derstanding. To faithfully encode image semantics, these models
represent visual inputs as a large number of tokens. The resulting
long token sequences directly inflate the input length, incurring
𝑂(𝑁2) attention complexity in the Transformer [36] backbone and
creating a critical computational bottleneck that limits the practical
deployment of LVLMs.

To mitigate this bottleneck, visual token reduction [6, 10, 11,
16, 17, 24, 27, 33, 38, 39, 41–43] has emerged as a promising direc-
tion. Existing methods can be broadly grouped into three fami-
lies. Text-relevance methods [6, 39] prioritize visual tokens that
align strongly with the prompt, but often over-focus on a few high-
response regions and discard peripheral context. Visual-diversity
methods [3, 16] instead maximize coverage of the image content,
but remain agnostic to the query and may waste token budget
on irrelevant regions. More recent methods [27, 38, 42] attempt
to integrate both objectives within a unified pipeline. However,
they do not explicitly model the sample-dependent trade-off be-
tween relevance preservation and visual coverage. As observed
in prior work [21], such input-agnostic balancing does not consis-
tently outperform single-objective baselines, a phenomenon termed
“1 + 1 < 1.” These findings suggest that the central challenge lies
not merely in combining relevance and diversity, but in estimating
an appropriate per-sample allocation between the two objectives.
In this work, we investigate this phenomenon through a system-
atic two-level analysis (Section 3). Specifically, we formulate the
token selection score as a weighted combination of text relevance
and visual diversity, controlled by a scalar weight 𝜃, and greedily
select tokens in descending order of this score. At the dataset level,
we find that each benchmark exhibits a clear optimal relevance-
diversity weight that delivers noticeably better performance than
alternative allocations, while the locations of these optima vary
substantially across tasks. We further investigate the source of this
variation at the instance level. Since answer correctness is discrete
and does not provide a continuous optimization signal, we use
per-sample answer likelihood as a surrogate objective to recover
oracle weights for individual inputs. The recovered oracle weights
further show that the sample-level preference is consistent with the
relevance-diversity tendency observed at the dataset level. These
results suggest that effective token reduction fundamentally
requires sample-aware relevance-diversity balancing.
Closing this gap is non-trivial. A practical sample-aware to-
ken reduction method must satisfy two key requirements. 1). The
prior must be derived from existing knowledge and the current
input, without relying on any task-specific or dataset-level context
unavailable at inference time. This is precisely the limitation of
MoB [21]: although it provides a principled theoretical account of
the relevance-diversity trade-off, its allocation strategy must be
configured per dataset. In open-world deployment, however, the
dataset identity of an incoming input is simply unknown. 2). The
prior must be obtained within the early prefill stage from shallow
semantics alone, without auxiliary modules or additional inference
beyond the compression pipeline. Relying on an extra predictor, or
waiting for more complete semantics from deeper layers, would in-
troduce substantial pre-pruning cost and compromise the efficiency
gains of token reduction, thereby undermining its very purpose as
an inference acceleration technique.
Crucially, we observe that the spatial distribution of high-relevan-
ce visual tokens provides an effective cue for the desired relevance-
diversity balance. When high-relevance tokens are spatially con-
centrated, the textual semantics are strongly aligned with a specific
local region of the image, which is likely to contain the informa-
tion most relevant to answering the query. When high-relevance
tokens are broadly dispersed, the model cannot localize truly rele-
vant visual tokens with high confidence, making relevance scores
less reliable as a basis for selection. In this case, a larger diversity
weight is preferred to preserve broad visual coverage and maintain
prediction accuracy.
Driven by these observations, we propose a Sample-Aware Rele-
vance-Diversity Balanced Token Reduction framework. Its core
component is the Geometric Intent Prior (GIP), a training-free prior
that quantifies the text-visual coupling of each sample from the
spatial covariance of its text-relevant visual tokens. GIP produces
a continuous scalar that adaptively modulates the relative weight
of each objective, enabling sample-specific objective balancing at
negligible cost. Complementing GIP, we introduce two objective-
specific improvements: a norm-weighted text centroid for text rel-
evance estimation, free of cross-modal attention and compatible
with FlashAttention [9]; and a facility location formulation for vi-
sual coverage whose marginal gain jointly accounts for novelty
and representativeness. Extensive evaluations demonstrate that our
method retains 96.5% of full-model performance using only 11% of
the visual tokens, consistently outperforming existing approaches
under equivalent compression budgets.
Our main contributions are summarized as follows:
• Analysis for Relevance-Diversity Balancing. We con-
duct a systematic two-level empirical analysis showing that
the optimal relevance-diversity balance varies substantially
across both tasks and individual samples, establishing that
per-sample balancing of the relevance-diversity objectives
is necessary for effective visual token compression.
• Solution to Sample-Aware Objective Balancing. We pro-
pose GIP, a training-free, efficient solution to sample-aware
relevance-diversity balancing, which derives a sample-specific
weight from the spatial distribution of text-relevant tokens.
• SAVER Framework. We propose a Weighted Text Centroid
for text relevance scoring and a Visual Marginal Coverage
Gain for diversity-aware token selection. By dynamically
balancing these two objectives via GIP, our proposed frame-
work SAVER achieves superior performance over competi-
tive baselines across multiple benchmarks and generalizes
to diverse model architectures.

Related Work
2.1
Large Vision-Language Models
Large Vision-Language Models (LVLMs) [5, 22, 25, 26, 37] have
emerged from the foundational success of Large Language Models
(LLMs) [1, 4, 8, 13, 35]. The standard LVLM pipeline consists of
three core components. A visual encoder, typically a pre-trained
vision transformer such as CLIP [32], extracts a sequence of visual
tokens from the input image. A text tokenizer independently pro-
cesses the natural language instruction. Both modalities are then
projected into a shared embedding space and fed into a core LLM for
joint multimodal reasoning. This architecture has achieved strong
performance across a wide range of tasks, including visual question
answering, image captioning, and document understanding. De-
spite these advances, faithfully encoding image semantics requires
generating a large number of visual tokens. This directly inflates

2.2
Token Reduction Methods
Existing token reduction methods largely fall into two categories:
text-relevance and visual coverage. Text-relevance methods use
cross-attention maps to select tokens that align with the text prompt.
FastV filters tokens by raw attention scores [6], and PyramidDrop
extends this into a progressive, layer-wise pruning scheme [39].
These methods quickly locate core semantic regions. Yet they tend
to over-select tokens near these hubs and discard important pe-
ripheral context. Visual coverage methods instead maximize the
semantic diversity of the selected token set. SAINT prunes via bipar-
tite graph node degrees [16], and DivPrune formulates redundancy
reduction as a Max-Min Diversity Problem [3]. These methods re-
cover the global visual context more faithfully. However, they are
task-agnostic. They do not consider the text instruction during
selection, which can waste token budget on irrelevant regions.
Recent work has attempted to combine both objectives within a
unified pipeline [38, 42]. Most such approaches use fixed weight-
ing schemes that do not adapt to the input. In practice, the opti-
mal balance between relevance and diversity varies considerably
across different questions. Li et al. formalize this trade-off using
𝜖-covering theory [21]. They show that multimodal datasets split
into two regimes—weak and strong text-visual coupling—and that
each regime requires different allocation parameters for best perfor-
mance. A limitation of their method is that it requires knowing the
target dataset in advance to set these parameters. This limits appli-
cability in open-ended settings where the task intent is unknown
at inference time.

diversity trade-off in visual token compression. We first formulate
the dataset-level optimal relevance-diversity weight and identify
how it varies across benchmarks. To gain deeper insight into the
origins of this cross-dataset variation, we further investigate the dis-
tribution of instance-level optimal weights within each benchmark.
Since no continuous metric directly reflects token selection quality
at the sample level, we introduce per-sample answer likelihood as
a surrogate objective to recover these instance-level weights. Based
on both levels of analysis, we report empirical observations that
motivate the design of our method.

Methodology

Geometric Intent Prior
As established in Section 3, the optimal relevance-diversity balance
is inherently sample-dependent, yet existing methods treat it as a
fixed, input-agnostic hyperparameter. Accessing deep cross-modal
representations from the LLM backbone could in principle provide
rich cues for estimating the sample-specific balance, but doing
so requires a full forward pass—the very computation that token
reduction is designed to avoid. A practical solution must therefore
rely on a proxy derivable from shallow representations in the early
prefill stage, before the full depth of the LLM is engaged.
We propose to use the spatial distribution of high-relevance vi-
sual tokens as such a proxy. The key observation is that the spatial
spread of these tokens reflects the degree to which the input query
can be resolved by a localized region of the image. When high-
relevance tokens are spatially concentrated, the query is grounded
in a specific local region, and a higher relevance weight is appro-
priate to retrieve detail-oriented tokens from that region. When
they are broadly dispersed, the text tokens lack strong semantic
alignment with any specific visual region, making relevance scores
unreliable as a selection criterion; in this case, a higher diversity
weight is warranted to ensure broad spatial coverage and allow
the model to attend to global visual details. This spatial geome-
try is fully determined from shallow token representations within
the LLM, requiring no additional training or full forward pass. We
formalize this as the Geometric Intent Prior (GIP).

4.3
Text Relevance via Weighted Text Centroid
Previous token reduction methods [6, 38, 42] estimate text relevance
from cross-modal attention maps within the LLM, measuring the
relevance of each visual token by its accumulated attention weight
toward text tokens. However, two limitations hinder this approach
in practice. First, modern inference pipelines increasingly adopt
FlashAttention [9], which fuses the attention kernel and does not
materialize the full attention matrix, rendering attention-based
relevance estimation inapplicable. Second, LLM attention maps are
susceptible to positional biases [42], where early tokens attract
disproportionately high attention irrespective of their semantic
content, introducing systematic noise into the relevance scores.
We instead propose to estimate text relevance via the semantic
centroid of the text token embeddings, which is both attention-free
and compatible with any inference backend. A naive unweighted
mean, however, is susceptible to interference from function words
and punctuation tokens, which contribute equally despite carrying
little semantic content. Empirically, content words exhibit con-
sistently higher 𝐿2 norms in the embedding space than function
words and punctuation. We exploit this property to define a norm-
weighted centroid, termed Weighted Text Centroid (WTC), which
naturally suppresses semantically weak tokens.

Visual diversity in token selection has been approached in two main

methods favor tokens that best represent dense groups of similar
visual patches, which tends to over-allocate budget to large homo-
geneous regions (e.g., background) and under-represent compact
foreground structures rich in semantic detail.

We compare inference efficiency against SparseVLM [42] and DAR-

introduce no additional computational overhead.

6
Conclusion
We present a systematic empirical analysis of the relevance–diversity
trade-off in visual token reduction, demonstrating that the opti-
mal objective balance varies substantially across both tasks and
individual inputs and cannot be captured by any fixed strategy.
Motivated by this finding, we propose SAVER, a training-free frame-
work centered on the Geometric Intent Prior (GIP), which infers a
sample-specific relevance-diversity weight directly from the spatial
distribution of high-relevance tokens. GIP is paired with a Weighted
Text Centroid for attention-free relevance estimation and a Visual
Marginal Coverage Gain for diversity-aware selection. Extensive
experiments show that SAVER consistently outperforms competi-
tive baselines across all compression budgets, while matching their
inference efficiency, confirming that instance-level adaptive balanc-
ing is a principled approach to visual token reduction.
