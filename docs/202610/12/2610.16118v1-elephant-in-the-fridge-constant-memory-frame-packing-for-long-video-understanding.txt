Abstract
Hour-long videos, streaming feeds, and movie-length content are pushing vision-
language models (VLMs) past their breaking point—the standard recipe of encod-
ing each frame into visual tokens yields sequences that grow linearly with video
length, causing prohibitive memory consumption and inference latency. Existing
approaches, such as token reduction and keyframe selection, attempt to shorten the
token sequence but leave the O(n) scaling intact, failing to resolve the fundamental
bottleneck. We propose FramePack-U, a strikingly simple, training-free frame
packing framework that compresses arbitrarily long videos into a fixed-size visual
representation at constant memory cost. FramePack-U requires no fine-tuning or
architectural modification — it plugs directly into off-the-shelf VLMs at inference
time, yet consistently surpasses more elaborate baselines. Drawing inspiration from
FramePack in generation literature, we propose a new content-aware compression
strategy that operates under a fixed token budget for visual understanding. Specifi-
cally, FramePack-U proceeds in two steps: (1) scoring each frame by its semantic
similarity to the text prompt and ranking frames in a diversity-driven manner, and
(2) packing the selected frames at varying resolutions into a fixed-length context
window. This design concentrates the memory budget on frames that are both
instruction-relevant and visually diverse. While in theory FramePack-U can pack
arbitrarily many frames into the fixed budget, we identify an empirical instantia-
tion that generalizes robustly across different settings. Extensive experiments on
LongVideoBench, MLVU, and Video-MME show that FramePack-U consistently
outperforms full-resolution baselines as well as prior token reduction and keyframe
selection methods, across diverse model families, parameter scales, and frame
budgets. Notably, on Qwen3.5-35B-A3B with a 128-frame budget, FramePack-U
yields gains of +12.9%, +10.6%, and +9.7% over the official model on the three
benchmarks, respectively.

Introduction
Long video understanding underpins a wide range of real-world applications, including surveillance
analysis, autonomous driving, sports commentary, movie comprehension, and instructional or educa-
tional assistants. Many of these scenarios further demand streaming settings, where models must
continuously ingest incoming frames and respond on the fly rather than process a bounded clip
offline. Unlike images or short videos, hour-long or unbounded streams carry rich temporal dynamics,

evolving narratives, and long-horizon causal dependencies, all of which are essential for higher-level
reasoning. Recent vision-language models (VLMs) [2, 27, 10, 16, 5] offer a promising path forward
by encoding visual content into tokens that are jointly processed with text instructions alongside
an LLM backbone. Yet when scaled from images and short clips to long or streaming videos, this
paradigm encounters a fundamental obstacle: the number of visual tokens grows proportionally with
the number of frames, which severely dilutes context, inflates memory consumption, and drives
inference cost up linearly or higher with video duration.
To address this, a large body of work resorts to information compression, typically through token
reduction [20, 34, 8] or keyframe selection [22, 28, 39]. By scoring visual features against handcrafted
or learned criteria, these methods filter out non-essential content and retain only the clues deemed
necessary for understanding. The VLM then operates on a much smaller set of visual tokens, yielding
faster inference and reduced memory footprint without retraining the backbone. However, such
compression schemes share two fundamental limitations. (1) They only postpone the scalability
bottleneck rather than eliminate it: the retained token count still scales as O(n) or higher with
video duration, so memory and latency continue to grow without bound as videos get longer. (2)
Hard-dropping tokens or frames is a discrete and inherently lossy operation. Forcing a binary keep-
or-discard decision fragments temporal continuity and erases transient actions that are often pivotal
for long-horizon reasoning – precisely the regime where long-video understanding matters most.
In this work we propose FramePack-U, a strikingly simple, training-free frame compression and
packing method that extracts a fixed-size representation from videos of arbitrary length, achieving
constant O(1) memory complexity. FramePack-U requires no fine-tuning or architectural modi-
fication—it plugs directly into off-the-shelf VLMs at inference time. The key idea is to assign
heterogeneous spatial resolutions to frames under a fixed total token budget regardless of video
duration, so that the memory budget is spent on frames that matter most. Inspired by FramePack
[36], which packs frames at varying resolutions for efficient video generation, we adapt this idea
for long video understanding. However, the original formulation is designed for generation, with a
schedule favoring temporal continuity and proximity, hence unsuitable for understanding tasks that
must preserve task-relevant evidence throughout the video.
To mitigate this issue, FramePack-U is designed to be content-aware, and operates in two steps. (1)
Relevance- and diversity-aware sampling. We compute the semantic similarity between the text
prompt and each frame, producing a relevance score that identifies frames containing instruction-
relevant evidence. Starting from the highest-scoring frame as a seed, we iteratively rank the remaining
frames by selecting, at each step, the frame most visually distinct from those already chosen. This
produces a single ordering that balances instruction relevance with visual diversity (Sec. 3.1). (2)
Hierarchical resolution packing. We then assign frames along this ranking to resolution hierarchies,
with top-ranked frames preserved at finer spatial resolutions and lower-ranked frames progressively
downsampled (Sec. 3.2). In principle, all frames can be retained, since the tiered scheme packs
them into a fixed-size context regardless of video length—concentrating the memory budget on
diverse, instruction-relevant frames while preserving full temporal coverage. While many tiered
packing schedules are possible in principle, we provide an empirical instantiation, identified through
experiments, that generalizes robustly across architectures, scales, and benchmarks.

Related Work
Vision-Language Models. VLMs have emerged in recent years as the dominant approach for image
understanding and grounding [6, 16, 23, 10]. The prevailing recipe pairs a CLIP-style [19, 25] visual
encoder with an LLM backbone, bridging the two through a lightweight connector that projects image
embeddings into the LLM token space [6, 12]. Video VLMs [3, 5, 2, 4, 13] typically inherit this
design and apply the image encoder frame-by-frame to obtain per-frame embeddings. A handful of
efforts replace or augment the per-frame pipeline with pretrained video encoders, often operating on
frame pairs or short clips [40, 26, 24], but scaling video encoders to longer temporal windows still
trails per-frame image encoding with strong backbones such as SigLIP 2 [25].
Long Video VLM. To curb the token overhead of frame-wise encoding, a growing body of work
pursues more compact video representations. One line of research performs token reduction inside the
visual stream, merging or pruning redundant tokens based on attention scores, similarity, or learned
importance [21, 11, 27]. The second line focuses on keyframe selection, scoring frames by query
relevance or diversity and discarding the rest before they reach the LLM [22, 28, 39, 33]. Other works
explore architectural changes, including memory banks for streaming video [35], sequential temporal
aggregation [29], and slow–fast pathways that mix high-fidelity keyframes with coarsely encoded
context frames [32, 40]. Despite their differences, these approaches share a common property: the
resulting token sequence still scales proportionally with video duration, so memory and latency
continue to grow as videos get longer.
Packing for Generation. FramePack [36] proposes a context packing strategy for next-frame
prediction models in video generation. The key idea is to assign heterogeneous token budgets to
input frames according to their importance, with frames closer to the prediction target receiving
longer contexts via finer-grained patchifying kernels. As a result, the generation workload becomes
invariant to video length, enabling long video synthesis on consumer GPUs. The packing principle has
since been adopted beyond video generation: FastAvatar [31] applies it to 3D avatar reconstruction,
retaining a sparse set of high-fidelity keyframes whilst compressing the remaining frames into an
aggregated token representation, enabling feedforward reconstruction from hundreds of frames
within a fixed memory footprint. Yume [15] and Yume1.5 [14] adopt related history-compression
strategies for interactive world generation, where historical frames are compressed at different rates
to support long-form autoregressive generation. FramePack-U shares the same packing philosophy,
i.e., allocating more tokens to more important frames within a fixed budget. However, rather than
generation, it targets understanding, adapting the packing strategy to the inference-time encoding of
video inputs in VLMs.

Discussion of the necessity of N. In principle, N can be as large as T, so FramePack-U can pack a
video of arbitrary length into the fixed budget. In practice, we treat N as a tunable parameter that
controls how many candidate frames are considered for packing, giving the framework flexibility
and enabling systematic study of how input scale affects performance. Later in experiments we
show that performance is not monotonically increasing in N. Under a fixed token budget, larger
N improves temporal coverage but forces lower per-frame resolution, and most VLMs degrade
once frame resolution falls below the range seen during pre-training. The optimal N thus reflects
a trade-off between temporal density and per-frame fidelity, and depends on model capacity and
pre-training data; we report the setting that generalizes best across our evaluations in Sec. 4.

3.2
Hierarchical resolution packing
Given the N ranked candidate frames from Sec. 3.1, FramePack-U assigns each one a spatial
resolution under a fixed token budget. The guiding principle is that frames differ in their relevance to
the query and their representativeness of the video: rather than encoding all frames at uniform fidelity,
FramePack-U allocates higher spatial resolution to more informative frames and progressively
compresses the remainder, maximizing temporal coverage within the budget. We next describe the
packing scheme, the frame ranking it relies on, and the concrete resolution schedule we adopt.

Implementation details. Given an input video, we uniformly sample T frames as the initial frame
set. Our method then assigns each frame to different resolution levels under a fixed memory budget.
Unless otherwise specified, we use the diversity-aware packing strategy as our default method. The
strategy first measures query-frame relevance using CLIP similarity and then performs diversity-
aware selection based on frame-level visual feature similarity, preventing visually redundant frames
from occupying the high-resolution budget. For the CLIP model, we choose CLIP-ViT-B/32 for its
lightweight nature. λ is set to 0.7. For the initial sampling rate, we sample all frames in an offline way
for the benchmarking experiments. It could also be set in online mode for lower latency. We use the
global feature extracted by ViT for frame-to-frame similarity. Regarding the resized dimensions, we
maintain the original resolutions following the default settings for LongVideoBench and Video-MME.
For MLVU, we proportionally resize candidate frames such that the longer edge is 1280 pixels before
subsequent processing. We follow the practice of the benchmarks and directly use their testing text
prompts.

4.2
Ablations
Ablation study on schemes.
The hierarchical packing scheme determines the trade-off between
temporal coverage and per-frame spatial fidelity. We conduct a broad search over mixed-resolution
schemes and identify several configurations that work consistently well across benchmarks. Table 2
reports representative strong schemes under the 512-frame memory budget on Qwen3.6-27B.
Overall, FramePack-U is robust to the exact choice of scheme. All mixed-resolution schemes
in Table 2 clearly outperform the vanilla baseline under the same memory budget. Different
schemes slightly favor different benchmarks: for example, d1f128d2f1536 performs best on
LongVideoBench, d1f384d2f512 performs best on MLVU, and d1f480d2f128 performs best
on Video-MME. This suggests that there is no single universally optimal allocation, as different tasks
may require different balances between fine-grained spatial evidence and broader temporal coverage.
Based on this search, we select schemes that achieve strong overall performance while keeping a
balanced allocation between high-resolution anchor frames and low-resolution contextual frames.
Importantly, the gains are not tied to a single carefully tuned configuration: even when the number
of packed frames varies substantially, from 608 to 1664 under the 512-frame budget, FramePack-U
maintains consistent improvements over the vanilla baseline. This robustness supports our design
choice of hierarchical resolution packing and shows that the key benefit comes from preserving broad
temporal context while assigning higher fidelity to informative frames.

Ablation study on frame diversity.
We further study the effect of the diversity-aware frame
selection strategy on Qwen3.6-27B. As an alternative, we consider a Relevance-only strategy, which
ranks frames solely according to their semantic similarity with the text query and assigns higher
resolutions to the top-ranked frames. While this strategy can identify query-relevant frames, it does
not explicitly consider visual redundancy among selected frames. As a result, multiple high-resolution
slots may be allocated to visually similar frames, limiting the effective temporal coverage.
Table 4 compares the Relevance-only strategy with our diversity-aware strategy under different
memory budgets. These results demonstrate that query relevance alone is insufficient for frame
packing. By jointly considering relevance and visual diversity, FramePack-U allocates the limited
high-resolution budget to more complementary frames, leading to more reliable performance under
fixed memory constraints.
Ablation study on prompt relevance.
We further analyze the effect of prompt relevance in the
packing strategy on Qwen3.6-27B. To isolate this factor, we construct a prompt-agnostic baseline,
Diversity-only, which does not use the text prompt for frame selection. Instead, it uniformly samples
N frames from the video to preserve coarse temporal coverage. It then assigns frame priorities
using a binary-tree midpoint ordering: the temporal midpoint of the sampled sequence is ranked first,
followed by the midpoints of the left and right sub-intervals, and this process is applied recursively in
a breadth-first manner. Given this priority order, the same resolution allocation scheme is applied as
in our method, with earlier-ranked frames receiving higher spatial resolutions. Thus, Diversity-only
preserves global temporal coverage but does not explicitly identify query-relevant evidence.
In contrast, our full strategy incorporates prompt relevance during frame sampling. Table 4 shows
the comparison, where our full strategy achieves stronger performance on most benchmarks and
memory settings, especially on MLVU. For example, under 512-frame memory, our strategy improves
LongVideoBench accuracy from 74.0 to 74.9 with the same d1f128d2f1536 scheme, and from
73.1 to 74.6 with d1f384d2f512. These results show that temporal diversity alone is not sufficient:
incorporating prompt relevance helps allocate high-resolution visual capacity to frames that are more
likely to contain task-relevant evidence.

6
Limitations
Despite its simplicity and effectiveness, FramePack-U still has several limitations. First, the benefit
of frame packing depends on the capacity of the underlying VLM. Although FramePack-U can pack
more temporally diverse frames into the same memory budget, smaller models may not be able to
effectively integrate and reason over the increased number of visual observations. As discussed in
Sec. 4.2, this can lead to unstable gains under larger memory budgets. For example, on smaller
Qwen-series models, increasing the number of packed frames sometimes improves LongVideoBench
but slightly degrades MLVU or Video-MME. We attribute this failure case to the capability boundary
of the base model rather than the packing strategy itself: when the model lacks sufficient visual
reasoning capacity, providing more contextual frames can introduce additional distraction instead of
useful evidence.
Second, FramePack-U is most naturally applicable to VLMs that encode video inputs as a sequence
of independently processed image frames. This design allows us to assign different spatial resolutions
to different frames before visual encoding. However, some recent video VLMs adopt grid-based
or tightly coupled video encoders, where frames are first organized into a fixed spatiotemporal grid
or processed jointly by a video-specific visual module. For such models, e.g., Eagle2.5 [3]-style
architectures, changing the resolution of individual frames may break the expected input format
or require non-trivial modifications to the visual encoder and preprocessing pipeline. As a result,
FramePack-U is currently less plug-and-play for these grid-based video architectures.
Third, the current implementation relies on CLIP similarity as a lightweight proxy for prompt rel-
evance. While this choice keeps the method training-free and efficient, CLIP may fail to capture
13


fine-grained temporal events, subtle interactions, or reasoning-heavy cues that are not easily recog-
nized from individual frames. In such cases, the relevance score may under-prioritize frames that
are important for answering the question. Similarly, the diversity term is based on visual feature
similarity and may not fully reflect semantic complementarity. Future work could explore stronger
query-frame scoring functions or lightweight temporal-aware relevance models while preserving the
training-free nature of the framework.
Finally, FramePack-U introduces a scheme-selection hyperparameter that controls the trade-off
between temporal coverage and per-frame spatial fidelity. Although our ablations show that the
method is robust across a range of mixed-resolution schemes, the optimal allocation can still vary
across models, benchmarks, and video domains. A more adaptive strategy that automatically selects
the packing scheme based on video length, query type, and model capacity would further improve
usability and robustness.
7
Conclusion
We introduced FramePack-U, a simple, training-free approach to long video understanding that
achieves constant O(1) memory complexity by packing frames into a fixed token budget at heteroge-
neous spatial resolutions. FramePack-U ranks frames by jointly considering instruction relevance and
visual diversity, then allocates resolutions hierarchically so that the memory budget is concentrated
on the most informative frames while full temporal coverage is preserved. Across LongVideoBench,
MLVU, and Video-MME, FramePack-U consistently outperforms full-resolution baselines as well
as prior token reduction and keyframe selection methods, and frequently matches or exceeds substan-
tially larger proprietary VLMs. As a plug-and-play module for off-the-shelf VLMs, FramePack-U
offers a practical step toward scalable long and streaming video understanding.

Alternative strategies
Binary-tree midpoint ordering.
In the absence of query information, frame importance can
be estimated purely from the temporal structure of the video. Inspired by the frame ordering of
FramePack [36], the most naïve approach assigns ranks in chronological order, giving full-resolution
quota to the earliest frames. This is problematic because the first frames of a long video are not
necessarily more informative than later ones; in practice, this strategy reduces to uniform sampling of
the early segment, discarding later temporal context entirely.
A more principled alternative is binary-tree midpoint ordering. It assigns ranks recursively: the
temporally central frame of the full video is ranked first, followed by the midpoints of the left and
right sub-intervals, and so on, proceeding breadth-first across the resulting binary tree. More formally,
let the frames be indexed as {1, . . . , N}. The rank-k frame is the midpoint of the sub-interval defined
by the k-th node in a breadth-first traversal of the complete binary tree over the temporal axis, as
detailed in Alg. 2. Such a design ensures that, for any budget n1 < N, the n1 anchor frames are
distributed across the full temporal extent of the video rather than concentrated at one end, thus
preserving global temporal coverage whilst allocating higher fidelity to temporally representative
moments.

CLIP-guided sampling.
Another straightforward alternative is to rely only on text–frame relevance
for frame sampling and ranking. Given a text query q and video frames {xi}T
i=1, we use CLIP [19]
to compute the cosine similarity between the text embedding of q and the visual embedding of each
frame:
ri = cos (ϕimg(xi), ϕtext(q)) ,
where ϕimg(·) and ϕtext(·) denote the CLIP image and text encoders, respectively. Frames are then
sorted in descending order according to ri, and the top N frames are selected as candidates for
packing. The same ranking order is also used for resolution allocation: frames with higher CLIP
similarity are assigned smaller downsampling factors and thus receive higher spatial resolutions,
while lower-ranked frames are more aggressively compressed.
This strategy provides a simple query-aware baseline, as it explicitly prioritizes frames that are
semantically aligned with the instruction. However, since it only considers query relevance, it may
repeatedly select visually similar frames when several adjacent frames all match the text query. As a
result, the selected high-resolution frames can become redundant and fail to cover complementary
evidence across the video. In contrast, our default strategy augments CLIP relevance with a diversity-

More comparisons with similar methods
Token compression and key frame selection is a recognized paradigm in efficient video LLMs.
Methods like SlowFast-LLaVA [32], Q-Frame [37] and LongVU [21] explore how to identify tokens
or frames with key information in different ways. FramePack-U differs from these works in the
following aspects:
SlowFast-LLaVA
relies on a rigid, query-agnostic two-stream architecture. Its resolution assign-
ment is strictly dictated by a predetermined temporal frame rate. The Slow pathway operates at a
low frame rate with high spatial resolution, while the Fast pathway utilizes a high frame rate with
aggressive spatial pooling. It assigns resolution simply based on temporal position, completely
ignoring whether the visual content is relevant to the instruction. In stark contrast, FramePack-U
dynamically and deterministically assigns resolutions based on content relevance and visual diversity.

Q-Frame
assigns High, Medium, and Low resolutions using a probabilistic Gumbel-Max sampling
trick applied to CLIP scores, followed by static hyperparameter thresholding to rank frames into
resolution tiers. FramePack-U is mathematically governed by a strict constant memory constraint,
formulated as Eq. 3. Our resolution packing is not a heuristic ranking strategy. It is a deterministic,
hierarchical optimization that mathematically guarantees O(1) memory complexity regardless of the
video length.
LongVU
relies heavily on an extensive video-language fine-tuning stage, whereas FramePack-U
operates entirely training-free on off-the-shelf VLMs. Methodologically, LongVU employs a dis-
jointed pipeline that query-agnostically discards frames based on DINOv2 [18] visual similarity
before introducing any cross-modal text queries. This creates a severe bottleneck that blindly destroys
instruction-relevant evidence simply due to low-level visual redundancy. In contrast, FramePack-U’s
deterministic greedy algorithm evaluates semantic text-relevance and visual diversity simultaneously
in a single, unified step. This joint optimization ensures that our strict memory budget is precisely
allocated without prematurely dropping query-critical frames.
Empirical Comparison.
Empirically, we conduct controlled experiments to directly compare our
strategy with Q-Frame and LongVU. For fairness, we adopt the same VLM (CLIP-ViT-B/32) for
feature extraction and similarity calculation, and apply these methods on the same VLM (Qwen3.5-
4B) under the same resolution scheme contract (d1f96d2f128). For the training-based method
LongVU, we implement a LongVU-style frame ranking strategy for resolution allocation. These
experiments control the same resolution assignment process and isolate the ranking strategy.
Table 11 shows that our method achieves superior performance, while other methods demonstrate
a lack of robustness or even degradation. These results prove that our ranking strategy effectively
distinguishes the most critical frames from less informative ones.
