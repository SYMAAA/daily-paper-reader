## Abstract 

Post-training quantization (PTQ) is a key technique for efficiently deploying large language models, as it compresses weights into low-bit representations without retraining. Existing PTQ solvers, including GPTQ, LDLQ, and block coordinate descent, commit each weight block to a single codeword before its neighbors are considered, leaving near-optimal alternatives unexplored. In this work, we cast row-wise PTQ as posterior inference over discrete codebooks, in which existing solvers correspond to a zero-temperature, hard-decision limit retaining only the top codeword per block. We propose Message-Passing Quantization (MPQ), which preserves soft posterior information across blocks during inference and commits to hard codewords only at the output stage. MPQ first uses vector approximate message passing to compute approximate blockwise posteriors, then refines a hard-decision initialization by exact block coordinate descent. Across 2-bit lattice and 3- and 4-bit scalar quantization on dense language models from Llama, Qwen, and Mistral families, MPQ improves perplexity and zero-shot accuracy over strong hard-decision baselines, and composes favorably with preprocessing techniques such as activation-aware scaling and incoherence processing. Our implementation is available at https://github.com/poseidonchan/MPQ .

## 1 Introduction 

The growing capabilities of large language models have made them increasingly useful, motivating efficient deployment on personal devices [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. Post-training quantization (PTQ) compresses a pretrained language model by mapping full-precision weights to low-bit discrete codes, enabling deployment under tight memory and compute budgets without additional retraining. Recent progress has mainly improved the preprocessing and objective-design stage of PTQ: activation scaling methods reduce quantization difficulty by rescaling weights and activations [6, 7, 8, 9]; incoherence processing methods make weights easier to quantize through rotations or related transformations [10, 11]; structured codebooks improve the expressiveness of low-bit representations [12, 11]; and inter-layer error propagation objectives better account for how quantization errors affect later layers [13, 14]. In contrast, the discrete solver stage has changed more incrementally. Most existing solvers quantize blocks sequentially, make an immediate hard assignment to a single codeword, and then greedily

compensate for the resulting error. GPTQ, LDLQ, and later block coordinate descent variants differ in scheduling and local approximation, but they remain fundamentally hard-decision solvers [15, 10, 16, 17, 18]. This approach is efficient but commits to a single hard codeword per block before its neighbors are processed. This early commitment is especially harmful in the low-bit regime. With very few available codes, several codewords can produce nearly the same local error, and strong Hessian coupling makes the best choice for one block depend on its neighbors. A hard-decision solver therefore commits to one codeword trajectory through the discrete assignment space, with no mechanism to recover if those early choices turn out to be globally suboptimal. As preprocessing, codebook, and objective design improve the conditioning of the quantization problem, the discrete solver remains a relatively underexplored axis. Instead of assigning each block to a single codeword immediately, the solver should maintain a soft distribution over plausible codewords for each block and let these distributions interact with their neighbors before any block commits to a hard decision. We take a message-passing view of row-wise PTQ, casting it as posterior inference over a discrete codebook with a Gaussian likelihood. From this view, existing solvers such as GPTQ, LDLQ, and block coordinate descent are zero-temperature message-passing schemes that propagate point estimates between blocks and discard other plausible codewords. This perspective naturally suggests a finite-temperature alternative, where messages carry distributions over several likely codewords before any block commits. We instantiate this idea with Message-Passing Quantization (MPQ), a two-stage solver that first explores uncertainty and then commits to a discrete solution. In the first stage, MPQ uses vector approximate message passing (VAMP) at finite temperature to aggregate information about near-tied codeword assignments across blocks. In the second stage, it converts the resulting posterior into a hard initialization and refines it with block coordinate descent. Experiments on Llama, Qwen, and Mistral models show that MPQ matches or improves over greedy solver baselines, with the largest gains at 3-bit scalar and 2-bit lattice precision. Our contributions are as follows. • We give a message-passing view of row-wise PTQ as posterior inference over a discrete codebook under a Gaussian likelihood, recovering existing solvers as zero-temperature limits and motivating finite-temperature inference as an unexplored solver-side direction. • We propose Message-Passing Quantization (MPQ), a two-stage solver that combines finite-temperature vector approximate message passing with exact block coordinate descent. • MPQ improves over strong solver baselines in perplexity and zero-shot accuracy across Llama, Qwen, and Mistral at 2-, 3-, and 4-bit quantization, with the largest gains in the lowest-bit regimes • We complement the method with theoretical analysis of the VAMP solver’s asymptotic behavior and the role of preprocessing in finite-temperature inference. 

## 2 Related Work 

Preprocessing, codebook, and objective design. Many studies improve the quantization problem before the solver runs. Scaling and equalization methods such as AWQ, SmoothQuant, OmniQuant, and MagR reduce dynamic-range imbalance or protect salient channels before rounding [6, 7, 19, 20]. Rotation and incoherence methods such as QuIP, QuIP#, QuaRot, SpinQuant, and QTIP reshape the Hessian geometry using random or learned orthogonal transforms [10, 11, 8, 9, 12]. Fine-tuning methods such as PV-Tuning further improve the compressed representation after quantization [21]. YAQA and Qronos extend layer-wise PTQ to account for errors propagating across layers [13, 14]. 

Solver-side PTQ. GPTQ made layerwise second-order quantization practical at LLM scale by developing OBQ-style formulations into an efficient column-wise pass [15]. QuIP and QuIP# extended this to vector quantization with LDLQ and incoherence processing [10, 11]. QuantEase and CDQuant revisit local repair via explicit block coordinate descent [16, 17]. Recent theoretical work has shown that GPTQ is equivalent to Babai’s nearest-plane algorithm on the quantization lattice [22, 23]. Building on this lattice perspective, OJBKQ further augments greedy Babai rounding with Klein’s sampling to sample multiple stochastic trajectories in the search space [18]. While Klein sampling injects stochasticity across multiple Babai trajectories, each individual trajectory still 2commits to one codeword per block sequentially. Section 3 unifies these solvers as zero-temperature limits of a common posterior view. 

Message passing for discrete inference. Row wise PTQ is a linear inverse problem with discrete constraints. Methods in the approximate message passing family maintain soft beliefs over latent blocks rather than collapsing immediately to point estimates [24, 25, 26]. VAMP extends AMP with rigorous state evolution for large right orthogonally invariant operators [27]. Although the random Hadamard transform used in modern PTQ pipelines does not make a finite calibration Hessian exactly right orthogonally invariant, it randomizes the coordinate basis and improves incoherence, making the right orthogonally invariant model a useful surrogate for analysis [10, 11]. We therefore use VAMP as the finite temperature inference stage of MPQ and interpret the associated state evolution as qualitative guidance for the RHT conditioned PTQ problem.

## 6 Experiments 

6.1 Setup Protocol. We use 128 C4 [28] training-set sequences of length 2048 as calibration data to quantize dense language model weights, while keeping activations at 16 bits during evaluation. We measure WikiText-2 [29] and C4 perplexity and zero-shot normalized accuracy on ARC-Challenge, ARC-Easy [30], HellaSwag [31], PIQA [32], and Winogrande [33] using lm-eval harness [34]. We also repeat the 3-bit and 4-bit scalar experiments on four Llama models with three independently sampled C4 calibration sets. Methods share each calibration draw. Appendix A.7 reports mean perplexity and sample standard deviation. 

Codebook and solvers. For 3/4-bit scalar codebook quantization, we use a group size of 128 and apply the activation-aware scaling [6] followed by RHT [10] as preprocessing for all solvers. For the GPTQ baseline, we use the strongest variant from GPTQ [15] with activation-ordered column processing. For 2-bit quantization, we adopt vector quantization with the E8P12 codebook [11], which provides better rate-distortion than scalar codebooks at this aggressive bit width. Following the standard recipe in QuIP# [11], we employ RHT for incoherence preprocessing, and take LDLQ as the corresponding greedy baseline [11]. For each greedy solver, GPTQ in the scalar setting and LDLQ in the lattice setting, we also include a stronger baseline that applies 3 passes of block coordinate descent (CD), matching the CD refinement used by MPQ. Additionally, we include QuantEase [16] and OJBKQ [18] as strong CD and randomized greedy baselines, respectively. Our MPQ solver’s default setting is 16 VAMP iterations followed by 3 CD passes. 6.3 Zero-shot transfer across model families 

We next check whether MPQ’s perplexity gains carry over to downstream accuracy. Table 2 reports the five task average on Mistral 7B [37], Qwen3 8B [38], Llama 3 8B [36], Llama 2 13B, and Llama 2 70B [35], covering both the 3-bit scalar and 2-bit E8P12 regimes. MPQ improves over the matched CD polished greedy baseline in every model and bit width, with gains ranging from 0.4 to 1.7 percentage points. Against the stronger QuantEase and OJBKQ baselines, MPQ is best or tied best in nine of ten model/bit width cells. The only exception is Mistral 7B at 3-bit scalar, where QuantEase leads by 0.2 points and MPQ ties OJBKQ for second best. At 2-bit E8P12, MPQ is the best method for all five models, improving over LDLQ+CD by 0.4 to 1.4 points. ## 7 Conclusion 

We presented MPQ, a solver-side approach to post-training quantization that casts row-wise objective as posterior inference over discrete codebooks. This unified view explains classical greedy solvers as hard-decision limits, while exposing finite-temperature message-passing as a natural way to propagate codeword uncertainty across blocks before committing to a quantized solution. The benefit of postponing commitment is most visible in aggressive low-bit regimes, which are also the regimes that determine whether large language models can be deployed on commodity hardware. MPQ improves over strong solver baselines across these regimes, with the clearest gains at 2 bits. We believe message-passing inference is particularly well suited to this combinatorial problem, and will open a productive regime in the design space of low-bit quantization solvers. 10 References

Implementation notes. First, the per-row choice of γ01 via 1/MSE( w, w rtn ) removes one continu-ous hyperparameter. State evolution is locally insensitive to this choice, but per-row scaling avoids pathologically small initial precisions on easy rows. Second, the precision retries on α1 and the precision clipping on γ2, γ t+1 1 guard against the rare degenerate cavities that finite discrete-denoiser sensitivities can produce. These safeguards activate on a small fraction of iterations in our runs and do not change the qualitative behavior of the recursion. 

## C Limitations 

Asymptotic theory. The analysis in Section 5 takes the unanchored τ → ∞ limit and adopts an idealized right-orthogonally invariant surrogate. The MPQ algorithm itself runs at finite τ on Hessians that are RHT-conditioned but not exactly right-orthogonally invariant in the asymptotic sense. We therefore treat Theorem 5.1 and Proposition 5.2 as qualitative guidance rather than tight characterizations of the deployed algorithm. A finite-τ and finite-dimensional analysis remains open. 26 Codebook scope. Our experiments cover uniform scalar codebooks at 3 and 4 bits and E8P12 and D4 lattice codebooks at 2 bits. Adapting MPQ to learned vector codebooks (AQLM) or trellis-coded codebooks (QTIP) is straightforward in principle, since the message-passing view in Section 3 is codebook-agnostic, but it requires codebook-specific extensions to the discrete denoiser that we do not evaluate here. 

## D Broader impact 

This work proposes an improved solver-side algorithm for post-training weight quantization of large language models. Our contribution sits within the foundational research category, and we view its broader societal effects as inheriting from those of the underlying language models rather than introducing new categories of risk. Potential positive impacts include lower memory and compute requirements for deploying large language models. This can broaden access to language modeling technology in resource-constrained settings, including smaller research labs, edge devices, and regions with limited cloud infrastructure, and can reduce the energy footprint of inference at scale. Potential negative impacts derive from the same lowering of deployment cost. Quantization techniques that make large language models cheaper to serve can also accelerate the deployment of language models in applications with harmful intent, such as disinformation and automated abuse. We do not see a direct or unique path from MPQ specifically to such harms beyond what is shared by the broader class of weight quantization methods and by language model deployment in general. The present submission releases an offline quantization solver rather than pre-trained models, fine-tuned weights, or training data.
