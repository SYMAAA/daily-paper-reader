Abstract 

Large Audio Language Models (LALMs) rea-son over speech, environmental sounds, mu-sic, and other acoustic information. However, their audio encoders produce dense token se-quences that make long-form audio costly to process. Existing audio pruning methods re-duce this cost using heuristics such as attention, representation similarity, or temporal sampling, but do not explicitly learn which tokens are most useful for a given question. We propose AudioScorer, a lightweight model that scores audio tokens according to the user question. It is trained by distilling the ranking of audio to-kens under the frozen LALM’s answer-gradient saliency, together with downstream answer su-pervision. Compared with other pruning meth-ods, AudioScorer achieves the best or tied-best result at 10% token retention on 7 of 10 seman-tic and acoustic tasks, including speech recog-nition, emotion recognition, and audio-scene classification. 

1 Introduction 

Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) ex-tend LLM reasoning to modalities such as vision and audio (Alayrac et al., 2022; Li et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023; OpenAI, 2023). Particularly, Large Au-dio Language Models (LALMs) (Chu et al., 2023; Tang et al., 2024; Ghosh et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2025b) encode audio into dense embedding se-quences projected into the LLM input space. Since the number of audio tokens grows with duration, long recordings substantially increase decoder cost. For example, Qwen2-Audio (Chu et al., 2024) pro-cesses about 25 audio tokens per second, yielding roughly 750 tokens for a 30 -second clip. A given question, however, may depend on only a fraction of them, motivating task-aware token pruning. Existing question-aware pruning methods largely rely on predefined signals, while learned relevance predic-tors are typically question-independent. 2 Method 

2.1 Design Hypotheses 

Our goal is to retain the audio tokens most useful for answering the user’s question under a fixed to-ken budget. We build AudioScorer around two hy-potheses. (H1) Token relevance depends on the question: the same audio token may be useful for one question and irrelevant for another. (H2) The frozen LALM can serve as a relevance teacher: 

given the question and reference answer, gradient-based sensitivity of the answer likelihood provides a useful signal of audio-token relevance. 

2.2 AudioScorer Design 

Figure 2 illustrates AudioScorer and its integration with the frozen LALM. Its design follows directly from the two hypotheses above. 

Question-conditioned scoring (H1). We encode the question with bge-base-en-v1.5 (Xiao et al., 2024) and use a lightweight cross-attention layer in which each audio token attends to the question tokens. A scoring head maps the resulting question-conditioned representation to one score per audio token, allowing the same recording to receive dif-ferent rankings for different questions. 

LALM-guided relevance distillation (H2). For each training example, we feed the full audio and question to the frozen LALM, teacher-force the reference answer, and backpropagate its log-likelihood to the audio tokens at the decoder in-put. Each token’s gradient norm measures the lo-cal sensitivity of the answer to that token. We normalize these values into soft token-level tar-gets that supervise AudioScorer’s ranking. We complement this with downstream answer super-vision. We scale each audio token by its pre-dicted score: higher-scoring tokens are suppressed less than lower-scoring tokens. At inference, soft weighting is replaced by hard Top-K pruning after layer ℓp. Full details are provided in Appendix A. 

3 Experiments 

Models and evaluation. We evaluate Au-dioScorer on Qwen2.5-Omni-3B (Xu et al., 2025a) using AudioMarathon (He et al., 2025) and MMAU-Pro (Kumar et al., 2026). We compare against the following baselines: FastV (Chen et al., 2024), DART (Wen et al., 2025), uniform frame sampling (He et al., 2025), HeadRouter (He et al., 2026), and SpeechPrune (Lin et al., 2025) at matched 70% , 40% , and 10% retention. All meth-ods prune after decoder layer ℓp = 2 , so the first two layers process the full sequence. The same ranking is reused across retention ratios.

4 Ablation Study 

Table 3 shows that both of our supervision signals are important, especially under aggressive prun-ing. Relevance distillation has the largest effect: removing it substantially degrades Speech Con-tent and Audio Classification at 10% retention. Removing downstream CE causes a similar but smaller decline. This supports their complemen-tary roles: gradient-based targets provide explicit token-level relevance supervision, whereas CE di-rectly optimizes the retained evidence for answer quality. Simplifying the scoring head to a single linear layer reduces Speech Content performance at 10% retention. Replacing soft weighting with hard Top-K training using a straight-through esti-mator (STE) (Bengio et al., 2013) also hurts Speech Content and Audio Classification at 10% retention, while remaining competitive or slightly better at 

40% and 70% . This suggests that soft weighting is most helpful when pruning is aggressive. Ques-tion cross-attention has a smaller and more task-dependent effect, suggesting that much of the gain comes from learned relevance supervision, with explicit question conditioning providing additional adaptation. No component is uniformly beneficial across all categories. Full subtask results are pro-vided in Appendix D. 

5 Related Work 

FastV (Chen et al., 2024) uses attention-based ranking, DART (Wen et al., 2025) removes re-dundant tokens, and AudioMarathon (He et al., 2025) evaluates these methods for long audio alongside uniform sampling. SpeechPrune (Lin et al., 2025) combines speech–text similarity with approximated attention, while HeadRouter (He et al., 2026) uses calibrated head profiles. Other methods reduce tokens through merging rather than pruning (Xiang et al., 2026; Bolya et al., 2023). LearnPruner (Takezoe et al., 2026) learns question-independent vision pruning, introducing question conditioning only later within the decoder. AudioScorer instead learns question-conditioned audio-token relevance from reference-answer gra-dients and downstream supervision, contributing a new supervision and pruning design for audio. 

6 Conclusion 

AudioScorer learns question-conditioned audio-token relevance using supervision from a frozen LALM. At aggressive retention budgets, it im-proves speech recognition, audio classification, and speaker information modeling over pruning base-lines. The category-level trade-offs motivate fur-ther work on task-adaptive selection for efficient long-form audio understanding. 4Limitations 

Our experiments are limited to Qwen2.5-Omni-3B and the audio modality. Moreover, performance gains are not uniform across tasks under aggressive pruning. For example, audio authenticity detection degrades substantially at 10% retention. Improve-ments over competing methods should therefore be interpreted in the context of these task-dependent trade-offs. Question conditioning yields modest and task-dependent gains in our ablations. Neither our training datasets nor the evaluation benchmarks directly target question-specific token selection. AudioScorer is trained from individual audio– question–answer examples, without explicit super-vision that contrasts which evidence should be se-lected for different questions on the same recording. Likewise, the benchmarks evaluate downstream task performance rather than question-dependent changes in the selected evidence. We therefore cannot determine whether the modest gains reflect a limitation of the conditioning mechanism or of the used evaluation. More controlled question-switching data would allow this effect to be as-sessed directly. Our evaluation is subject to bench-mark limitations. To the best of our knowledge, AudioMarathon had not been published in a peer-reviewed venue at the time of submission.

A AudioScorer Architecture and Training Details 

We describe the configuration of the reported Qwen2.5-Omni-3B checkpoint. The LALM, in-cluding its audio encoder, remains frozen. We train 6only the scorer and its question-conditioning com-ponents; the gradient teacher described below has no parameters. 

A.1 Training Details 

The supervised mixture covers speech transcrip-tion, dialogue, sound description, music under-standing, speaker characteristics, emotion, and au-dio authenticity. LibriSpeech (Panayotov et al., 2015) supplies transcription and gender-label ex-amples. MultiDialog and IEMOCAP are drawn from the LongAudio-XL resources (Ghosh et al., 2026; Park et al., 2024; Busso et al., 2008); AudioCaps (Kim et al., 2019) provides sound captions. WavText5K, YouTube-8M, and Mu-sicBench use the AudioSkills-XL resources (Ghosh et al., 2026; Deshmukh et al., 2023; Abu-El-Haija et al., 2016; Melechovsky et al., 2024). Audio-authenticity examples come from the 

deepfake-audio-detection dataset (Stafford, 2024). We train for three epochs on a single NVIDIA A6000 GPU using AdamW (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2019) with a learning rate of 10 −4.Training AudioScorer takes approximately 9 GPU-hours. To balance the training sources, we ran-domly sample 1,000 examples from each dataset without replacement using a fixed seed of 42 . The selected subsets remain fixed across epochs. Final evaluation uses the available 6,347 AudioMarathon and 5,305 MMAU-Pro examples. 

A.2 Question-Conditioned Scoring 

Let X = {xi}Ni=1 denote the projected au-dio tokens. We encode the user question with 

BAAI/bge-base-en-v1.5 (Xiao et al., 2024), pro-ducing token-level representations T = {tj }Lj=1 ,where L is the number of question tokens. These representations are projected into the scorer space and used as keys and values for cross-attention with the audio tokens. Of the question encoder’s 109 .48 M parameters, only the final two transformer layers ( 14 .18 M) and pooler ( 0.59 M) are trainable; the remaining 

94 .72 M are frozen. A trainable projection and Lay-erNorm ( 1.58 M parameters) map T into the scorer space. The complete question-encoder module con-tains 111 .06 M parameters, of which 16 .35 M are trainable. A grouped-query cross-attention layer uses au-dio tokens as queries and question tokens as keys and values: 

C = CrossAttn( X, T), ci = Ci. (1) The cross-attention layer follows the Qwen2.5-Omni-3B (Xu et al., 2025a) configuration, using hidden size 2048 , 16 query heads, 2 key/value heads, and head dimension 128 .A scoring head f , an MLP with dimensions 

2048 → 4096 → 1024 → 1, maps the question-conditioned representation ci of each token to a logit, and a sigmoid turns the logit into the token score: 

si = σ(f (ci)) ∈ (0 , 1) . (2) Overall, AudioScorer has 50 .15 M trainable pa-rameters, of which 16 .35 M belong to the ques-tion encoder and its projection. Together with the 94 .72 M frozen parameters of the question en-coder, it contains 144 .87 M parameters. Relative to the approximately 3.72 B parameters of the frozen audio-language path of Qwen2.5-Omni-3B (lan-guage model plus audio encoder), AudioScorer corresponds to about 3.89% of the base model size, while its trainable parameters correspond to about 

1.35% .
