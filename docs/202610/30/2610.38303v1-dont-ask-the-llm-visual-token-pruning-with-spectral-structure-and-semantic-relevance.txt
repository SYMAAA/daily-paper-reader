## Abstract 

Large vision-language models (LVLMs) represent images and videos as long visual-token sequences, substantially increasing inference costs. These costs motivate visual-token pruning, whose effectiveness depends on both what signal is used for selection and when that signal be-comes available. Our analysis reveals complementary limitations: visual-side [CLS] attention produces scat-tered, background-biased selections under aggressive com-pression, while stronger query-conditioned signals become available after substantial computation within the large language model (LLM). This raises a natural question: can structurally coherent, query-aware token selection be performed before consulting the LLM? We therefore pro-pose ST-Pruner (Spectral-Textual Pruner ), a training-free framework for pre-LLM visual-token selection. It com-bines attention-independent spectral saliency with inverted patch–text similarity through parameter-free multiplicative fusion. Across diverse LVLMs, ST-Pruner retains over 95% 

of full-token performance using at most 11 .1% of visual to-kens and achieves a 3.2× end-to-end speedup on LLaVA-NeXT. Code is available at https://github.com/ sejong-rcv/ST-Pruner .

## 1. Introduction 

Building on recent advances in large language mod-els (LLMs) [2, 30], large vision-language models (LVLMs) [13, 14, 17–20, 29] extend language-model rea-soning to visual inputs represented as visual-token se-quences. As visual-token sequences grow with resolution, multi-view processing, and temporal inputs, quadratic self-attention costs [31] have motivated extensive work on visual token pruning [1, 3, 34, 36, 38].

This raises the central question behind our work: can both structural coherence and query awareness be ob-tained before consulting the LLM? We therefore propose 

ST-Pruner (Spectral-Textual Pruner ), a training-free pre-LLM framework that combines attention-independent spec-tral saliency from the low-rank structure of visual features with inverted patch–text similarity through parameter-free multiplicative fusion. To preserve visual structure without relying on a sin-gle global attention distribution, we characterize each patch by its participation in the dominant low-rank structure of the visual feature matrix using singular value decomposi-tion (SVD). The resulting spectral saliency produces more spatially coherent selections with reduced background scat-ter. Motivated by this observation, we use inverted patch–text similarity as a pre-LLM relevance signal. As shown in Figure 2(a,c), the nor-malized overlap between its top-K set and the in-LLM se-lections increases in the mid-to-late regime, reaching 4.1×

the random-overlap expectation at K = 32 . This provides a pre-LLM surrogate for the query-aware selections observed in mid-to-late LLM layers without decoder-side scoring. Together, the two cues provide complementary structural and semantic evidence before LLM inference. 

Contributions. Our contributions are threefold: • We conduct a systematic analysis of commonly used visual-token selection signals and identify complemen-tary limitations: [CLS] -based selection becomes noisy and background-biased under aggressive compression, while stronger query-conditioned selections appear only after substantial LLM computation. • Guided by these observations, we propose ST-Pruner ,a training-free pre-LLM framework that combines attention-independent spectral saliency from low-rank vi-sual structure with inverted patch–text similarity through parameter-free multiplicative fusion. • We demonstrate strong compression–performance trade-offs across diverse image and video LVLMs. ST-Pruner retains over 95% of full-token performance with at most 

11 .1% of the visual tokens, and achieves a 3.2× end-to-end speedup on LLaVA-NeXT while retaining 99 .5% of the full-token POPE F1. 

## 2. Related Work 

2.1. Training-Free Visual Token Reduction 

Training-free visual token reduction methods can be broadly categorized by where token importance is esti-mated: inside the LLM or before LLM inference. In-LLM methods estimate visual-token importance from decoder-side signals and prune tokens at intermediate layers. FastV [3] and SparseVLM [38], for example, use text-to-visual attention for token selection. Because impor-tance is estimated only after entering the decoder, their computational savings depend on the layer at which prun-ing occurs. Pre-LLM methods instead reduce visual to-kens before decoder execution. Some rely only on visual-side information, including attention, redundancy, diversity, spatial coverage, approximation error, or token sensitiv-ity [1, 11, 24, 26, 33, 34, 36, 39]. More recent approaches additionally incorporate textual guidance into pre-LLM se-lection [4, 21, 35, 37]. ST-Pruner belongs to this text-guided pre-LLM setting, but differs in how its structural and se-mantic selection signals are constructed, as discussed next. 

2.2. Structural and Semantic Signals for Token Selection 

Two forms of evidence are particularly relevant to pre-LLM token selection: preservation of visual structure and query relevance. For structural selection, prior methods rely on visual attention, feature redundancy, diversity, spatial cov-erage, approximation error, or token sensitivity [1, 11, 21, 24, 34, 36, 39]. These criteria primarily derive importance from attention distributions or relations among visual to-kens. In contrast, ST-Pruner uses an attention-independent spectral criterion, deriving token-wise saliency from each patch’s contribution to the dominant singular components of the visual feature matrix. Query relevance provides a complementary source of evidence. TRIM [28] exploits pretrained vision–language similarity, while recent pre-LLM methods incorporate tex-tual guidance through instruction relevance, textual similar-ity, or text-guided complementation [35, 37]. Prior CLIP in-terpretability studies further report that local patch–text re-sponses can exhibit counter-intuitive spatial behavior due to pooling-induced semantic shift [15]. Recent work has also explored reversed CLIP similarity for grounding-oriented visual token pruning [12]. ST-Pruner instead uses inverted patch–text similarity as a pre-LLM surrogate for query-conditioned token selection and combines it with SVD-derived spectral saliency. 

## 3. Method 

We present ST-Pruner , a training-free framework that per-forms visual token selection entirely before LLM inference. Figure 3 illustrates the overall pipeline, followed by the spectral, semantic, and fusion components described below.

3.3. Pre-LLM Semantic Relevance 

Spectral saliency is query-independent and therefore does not explicitly capture query relevance. For models equipped with a paired vision–language encoder, we use its pretrained shared embedding space to obtain a query-conditioned sig-nal before LLM inference. The visual projection maps each pre-projector patch feature zi to the shared vision–language embedding vi = ϕ(zi). The corre-sponding text encoder produces an EOS-token representa-tion, which is mapped by the text projection to q = ψ(T ).Both vi and q are ℓ2-normalized. We evaluate this spectral-only set-ting on Qwen2.5-VL in Section 4. 

3.4. Dual-Cue Fusion and Token Selection 

The spectral score si reflects a patch’s participation in domi-nant visual structure, whereas the semantic score ri encodes query-conditioned relevance under the inverted similarity surrogate. We combine the two normalized scores using aparameter-free multiplicative rule: 

gi = si · ri. (9) Collecting the token-wise fusion scores, let g =[g1, . . . , g N ]⊤. Multiplicative fusion favors agreement be-tween the two cues: a patch receives a high final score only when it is supported by both structural and semantic evi-dence. Unlike weighted additive fusion, this formulation introduces no additional balancing coefficient between the two normalized scores. The retained token set is then obtained by 

S = TopK( g, K ), |S| = K. (10) Here, TopK( g, K ) returns the indices of the K largest en-tries of g.The selected indices are sorted by their original token positions before constructing the retained visual-token se-quence, so pruning does not reorder the surviving tokens. ST-Pruner requires neither additional training nor learnable fusion parameters, and no LLM representation is required for token scoring. 

3.5. Computational Complexity 

For Z ∈ RN ×d and R ≪ min( N, d ), the randomized rank-R SVD has an approximate computational cost of 

O(N dR ) for a fixed number of subspace iterations, fol-lowed by O(N R ) computation for the spectral score. Thus, the spectral branch has leading complexity 

Cspec = O(N dR + N R ). (11) The semantic branch requires a single frozen text-encoder forward pass, followed by visual projection and patch–text similarity computation, while the final fusion consists only of an element-wise product between the two token-wise scores. Since both scoring branches are com-pleted before decoder execution, each visual group is re-duced from N to K tokens before the retained groups are concatenated into the LLM input. Because self-attention scales quadratically with sequence length, reducing the vi-sual prefix lowers the attention cost throughout all LLM lay-ers [31]. End-to-end savings including scoring overhead are evaluated empirically in Section 4. 

## 4. Experiments 

4.1. Experimental Setup 

Models and baselines. For image understanding, we evaluate ST-Pruner on the widely adopted LLaVA family, using LLaVA-1.5-7B [19] as a standard setting and LLaVA-NeXT-7B [20] for substantially longer multi-view visual in-puts. To assess cross-architecture transfer, we further evalu-ate the spectral branch on Qwen2.5-VL-7B [29]. For video understanding, we adopt Video-LLaVA-7B [17] to evalu-ate ST-Pruner on multi-frame visual inputs. We compare against FastV [3], SparseVLM [38], VisionZip [34], Di-vPrune [1], VisPruner [36], CDPruner [37], and ApET [24]. Published results are used when directly compatible with our setting. Otherwise, we reproduce the official implemen-tation. 

Datasets. For image understanding, we evaluate on VQAv2 [6], GQA [9], VizWiz [7], ScienceQA-IMG [23], TextVQA [27], POPE [16], MME [5], and MMBench (MMB) [22]. For video understanding, we evaluate on TGIF-QA [10], MSVD-QA [32], and MSRVTT-QA [32]. We report the standard benchmark metrics: accuracy for VQAv2, VizWiz, ScienceQA-IMG, TextVQA, and MM-Bench, exact match for GQA, F1 for POPE, and MME Per-ception (MME P). For video QA, we report GPT-3.5-turbo-judged accuracy following the standard Video-LLaVA pro-tocol. For aggregate comparison, each benchmark score is normalized by its corresponding full-token reference, and we report the mean retention over the displayed metrics. 

Implementation details. Unless otherwise specified, ST-Pruner uses ViT-space features, randomized low-rank SVD, direct multiplicative fusion, and R = N/ 8 within each visual group. All model parameters remain frozen. 4.2. Main Results on Image LVLMs 

LLaVA-1.5. We first evaluate ST-Pruner on LLaVA-1.5-7B, a widely used setting for visual token pruning. Fol-lowing prior work, we consider three token budgets of 192, 128, and 64 out of the original 576 visual tokens. As shown in Table 1, ST-Pruner achieves the highest average perfor-mance retention at all three compression levels, preserving 

99 .2% , 98 .6% , and 96 .3% of the full-token reference, re-spectively. Reducing the token budget threefold from 192 to 64 decreases average retention by only 2.9 percentage points, while removing 88 .9% of the original visual tokens. 

LLaVA-NeXT. We next examine whether ST-Pruner re-mains effective for substantially longer visual sequences. Unlike LLaVA-1.5, LLaVA-NeXT processes up to five 576-token views, yielding up to 2880 visual tokens. Table 2 shows that ST-Pruner retains 99 .6% , 97 .5% , and 94 .4% of full-token performance with only 640, 320, and 160 tokens, respectively. These results show that pre-LLM selection re-mains effective for long multi-view visual sequences.

4.5. Efficiency 

To evaluate practical inference efficiency, we compare ST-Pruner with representative token-pruning methods on LLaVA-NeXT-7B over the full POPE evaluation set of 8,910 samples. All experiments are conducted on a sin-gle NVIDIA V100 GPU under FP16, and the cross-method comparison uses a 320-token target budget. At this bud-get, ST-Pruner reduces analytical LLM prefill FLOPs from 

34 .9T to 4.5T and the visual-prefix KV cache from 1542 MB to 200 MB. As shown in Table 7, all reported latencies include method-specific pruning and scoring overhead. For ST-Pruner, this includes CLIP text encoding, patch–text rele-vance computation, randomized low-rank SVD, multiplica-tive fusion, and top-K selection. All components are exe-cuted within the timed generation call without precomputa-tion. For SparseVLM, which progressively removes tokens inside the LLM, FLOPs and KV cache are computed from the actual layer-wise token trajectory rather than from the final retained token count. ST-Pruner achieves a 3.6× prefill speedup and a 3.2×

end-to-end speedup while retaining a POPE F1 of 86 .1. Its spectral-only variant reaches 165 .4 ms prefill and 197 .2 ms end-to-end latency, closely matching VisPruner at 163 .0 ms and 197 .9 ms while improving F1 from 81 .3 to 83 .1. Vi-sionZip is faster at 142 .5 ms prefill and 178 .4 ms end-to-end, but its F1 of 82 .3 is lower than that of the spectral-only variant. The full ST-Pruner further improves F1 to 86 .1 while operating in a similar latency range to DivPrune and CD-Pruner. DivPrune reaches 83 .4 F1 at 221 .1 ms total la-tency, while CDPruner reaches 85 .8 F1 at 211 .9 ms. Rel-ative to spectral-only selection, the semantic branch adds only 22 .6 ms of prefill latency at K = 320 while improving POPE F1 from 83 .1 to 86 .1, corresponding to a 3.0-point gain for an additional 26 .8 ms of end-to-end latency. Com-plete 640/320/160-token efficiency results are provided in the supplementary material. 

## 5. Conclusion 

We presented ST-Pruner as a pre-LLM alternative to decoder-dependent visual token selection. Rather than treating pruning solely as an attention-ranking problem, our results show that dominant spectral structure provides an attention-independent cue for preserving coherent visual evidence, while inverted patch–text similarity supplies com-plementary query-conditioned relevance. Across image, video, and multi-view LVLMs, these cues enable effective visual-token reduction while preserving strong downstream performance, with ST-Pruner achieving a 3.2× end-to-end speedup on LLaVA-NeXT. More broadly, our findings sug-gest that the geometry of visual representations itself can provide useful signals for efficient multimodal inference be-yond conventional attention-based importance. 

Limitations and future work. The semantic branch of ST-Pruner assumes access to a text encoder paired with the vision encoder for pre-LLM patch–text scoring, which is not exposed by all LVLM architectures. In such cases, ST-Pruner currently operates using spectral saliency alone, as demonstrated on Qwen2.5-VL. Extending query-conditioned pre-LLM selection to architectures without such paired text encoders and examining whether similar spectral structure persists across different vision encoders and multimodal representations are promising directions. 

Additional analysis. Beyond the main paper, the sup-plementary material provides detailed evaluation protocols and complete fusion and spectral-rank sweeps (Secs. 6–7), feature-space and singular-value weighting ablations with qualitative spectral analyses (Sec. 8), and full efficiency re-sults across multiple token budgets (Sec. 9). ## 6. Evaluation Details 

6.1. Datasets 

We evaluate ST-Pruner on eight image benchmarks and three video benchmarks used in standard LLaVA and Video-LLaVA evaluation pipelines. Unless otherwise stated, we follow the task-specific inference and evaluation protocols used by the corresponding backbone. 

6.1.1. Image Benchmarks VQAv2 [6]. VQAv2 evaluates open-ended visual question answering while reducing language-only answer priors through adversarially balanced image–question pairs with contrasting answers. The benchmark is built on MSCOCO images and provides multiple human reference answers per question. We follow the standard LLaVA protocol and report VQA accuracy on the test-dev split containing 107,394 image–question pairs. 

GQA [9]. GQA focuses on compositional visual reasoning over objects, attributes, and relations derived from scene graphs. We evaluate on the test-dev split containing 12,578 image–question pairs and report exact-match accuracy. 

VizWiz [7]. VizWiz contains real-world images and questions collected from blind users. Its unconstrained images include challenging cases involving blur, poor lighting, occlusion, and questions that may be unanswerable from the image. We follow the standard LLaVA evaluation setting and report accuracy on the 8,000-example test-dev split. 

ScienceQA-IMG [23]. ScienceQA is a multimodal multiple-choice benchmark covering natural science, social science, and language-related topics across diverse topics, categories, and skills. Following prior LLaVA evaluations, we use the image-associated subset of the test set (SQA-IMG), containing 2,017 image–question pairs, and report multiple-choice accu-racy. 

TextVQA [27]. TextVQA measures question answering that requires recognizing and reasoning over text embedded in natural images, including signs, advertisements, and product packaging. Answers may be read directly from visible text or require combining OCR evidence with surrounding visual context. We evaluate on the 5,000-example validation split and report the standard TextVQA accuracy. 

POPE [16]. POPE evaluates object hallucination through binary questions about whether a queried object is present in an image. We report the average F1 score over the three standard sampling strategies. The full evaluation set contains 8,910 image–question pairs. 

MME [5]. MME evaluates multimodal perception and cognition through a collection of binary judgment tasks. Following the main paper, we report the MME Perception score (MME P), whose subtasks cover coarse- and fine-grained recognition, counting, position, color, OCR, and related perceptual capabilities. The perception evaluation used here contains 2,374 image–question pairs. MMBench [22]. MMBench is a bilingual multiple-choice benchmark designed for fine-grained evaluation of multimodal perception and reasoning. Its capability taxonomy spans high-level perception and reasoning categories and progressively finer visual-language abilities. The benchmark further uses CircularEval, which cyclically permutes answer choices to reduce option-position bias and tests whether the model answers a question consistently across permutations. We evaluate the English version (MMBench-EN), which contains 4,377 image–question pairs, using the standard LLaVA evaluation pipeline and report circular accuracy. 

6.1.2. Video Benchmarks Video evaluation protocol. For TGIF-QA, MSVD-QA, and MSRVTT-QA, we follow the standard Video-ChatGPT GPT-3.5-turbo evaluation protocol adopted by Video-LLaVA [17]. Generated answers are judged against the reference answers using the same correctness prompt and score-parsing procedure as the original evaluation pipeline. Our evaluation implemen-tation preserves this judging protocol while parallelizing API requests for efficient evaluation. We evaluate the full evaluation set used by our implementation for each benchmark and report GPT-3.5-turbo-judged accuracy. 

TGIF-QA [10]. TGIF-QA extends image-based visual question answering to animated GIFs and requires both spatial and temporal understanding. The benchmark contains approximately 72K GIFs and 165K crowdsourced question–answer pairs across several task types. We evaluate the FrameQA task. 

MSVD-QA [32]. MSVD-QA is constructed from the Microsoft Research Video Description Corpus and evaluates open-ended question answering over short video clips. The benchmark is derived from 1,970 videos and contains approximately 50.5K question–answer pairs. 

MSRVTT-QA [32]. MSRVTT-QA is based on the larger MSR-VTT video-text corpus, which contains 10K videos with more diverse scenes and content than MSVD. The derived QA benchmark contains approximately 243K question–answer pairs. We use the standard Video-LLaVA evaluation protocol. 

6.2. Models and Implementation Details 

LLaVA-1.5-7B [19]. LLaVA-1.5 follows the standard LLaVA architecture, coupling a pretrained CLIP ViT-L/14 vision encoder with a Vicuna-v1.5 language model through a two-layer MLP vision–language projector. Compared with the original LLaVA, LLaVA-1.5 increases the image resolution to 336 ×336 and incorporates additional academic VQA data during visual instruction tuning. At this resolution, the CLIP vision tower produces a 24 × 24 grid of 576 spatial patch tokens before the multimodal projector. 

LLaVA-NeXT-7B [20]. LLaVA-NeXT, also referred to as LLaVA-1.6, extends the LLaVA-1.5 design with dynamic high-resolution processing. Its AnyRes strategy selects an image grid according to the input aspect ratio, divides a high-resolution image into multiple 336 × 336 views, and encodes the resulting visual groups with the same vision tower before their representations are assembled for the LLM. This design preserves substantially more local image detail than the single-view LLaVA-1.5 input and is particularly relevant to OCR and fine-grained visual reasoning. Under the configuration used in our experiments, an image contains up to five independently encoded 576-token visual groups, corresponding to at most 2880 visual tokens. 

Video-LLaVA-7B [17]. Video-LLaVA extends the LLaVA-style framework to joint image and video understanding through the “alignment before projection” design. It uses LanguageBind visual encoders whose image and video representations are pretrained in a shared language-aligned feature space, followed by a shared projection interface to the language model. For video input, multiple sampled frames are encoded and their visual-token representations are concatenated before LLM inference. Following the standard Video-LLaVA evaluation setting used in our experiments, each video is represented by eight frames with 256 visual tokens per frame. 

Qwen2.5-VL-7B [29]. Qwen2.5-VL uses a native dynamic-resolution Vision Transformer together with the Qwen2.5 lan-guage model. Its vision encoder incorporates window attention for efficient high-resolution processing, while the multimodal architecture supports variable-resolution images and videos through a model-specific visual merging and projection inter-face. Unlike the LLaVA-family settings above, Qwen2.5-VL does not expose a CLIP-style text encoder paired with its vision encoder for pre-LLM patch–text scoring. We therefore use Qwen2.5-VL as a cross-architecture evaluation of the spectral branch. For controlled comparison, we use the fixed 1008 × 1008 setting from the main paper, which produces 1296 visual tokens. 

Token budgets and spectral settings. For LLaVA-1.5, ST-Pruner retains 192, 128, or 64 tokens from the original 576-token group and uses R = 72 . For LLaVA-NeXT, scoring and normalization are performed independently within each 576-token visual group with R = 72 , and we retain 128, 64, or 32 tokens per group. For an input with five groups, these settings correspond to at most 640, 320, or 160 retained visual tokens. For Video-LLaVA, we use R = 32 independently for each 256-token frame and retain 17 tokens per frame, giving 136 retained tokens over eight frames. For Qwen2.5-VL, we use R = 162 and retain 512, 256, or 128 tokens. For LLaVA-1.5, LLaVA-NeXT, and Video-LLaVA, randomized low-rank SVD uses sketch size qsvd = R with two power iterations and no additional oversampling. For Qwen2.5-VL, we use qsvd = R + 6 with two power iterations. Thus, the default spectral rank is R = N/ 8 within each local visual group for all evaluated backbones and is not tuned separately for individual benchmarks or target token budgets.
