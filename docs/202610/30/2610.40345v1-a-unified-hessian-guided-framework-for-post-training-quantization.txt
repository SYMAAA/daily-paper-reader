## ABSTRACT 

Low-bit quantization enables efficient deployment of large language models by reducing memory footprint and computational cost. Achieving high accuracy at low precision requires selecting quantization ranges, linear transformations, and weight rounding decisions. Existing approaches typically optimize these compo-nents independently using local reconstruction objectives, which fail to account for the varying impact of quantization errors on downstream predictions. Direct optimization of task-level metrics would provide a more faithful objective, but is challenging due to the discontinuous nature of quantization operations and the high computational cost of evaluating their downstream effects. In this work, we derive a Hessian-guided approximation of the output change in-duced by joint weight and activation quantization. Based on a tractable Fisher approximation, we formulate a unified post-training quantization framework that enables the joint optimization of quantization ranges, linear transformations, and weight rounding under a common output-sensitivity objective. The resulting method remains practical for modern large language models and consistently outperforms state-of-the-art transformation and rounding methods across both channel-wise and block-wise quantization settings. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Large language models achieve strong performance across a wide range of language tasks, but their memory and computational requirements make deployment expensive. Quantization reduces these costs by representing weights and activations at lower precision, improving memory efficiency and enabling faster integer computation. The central challenge is to obtain these benefits while preserv-ing the predictions of the full-precision model. We focus on integer post-training quantization (PTQ) of both weights and activations. A small calibration set informs the quantization parameters. At a fixed bit width, accuracy depends on the quantization ranges, rounding choices, and representation basis. Quantization ranges set the scales: narrower ranges give finer grids but can increase clipping error. Rounding then selects weights on those grids. Both choices operate on a given representation of the layer. An invertible, function-preserving transform (van Breugel et al., 2026), such as a rotation or Hadamard transform (Ashkboos et al., 2024b), changes that representation by redistributing weight and activation magnitudes while preserving the full-precision layer output. Range selection and transforms can address both weights and activations, whereas adaptive rounding primarily addresses weights. However, methods based on the Weight Hessian fail to capture the error introduced by quantizing the activations (Figure 1, second column). In this work, we use a quadratic expansion of the KL divergence between the original and quantized model predictions around the layer output with a tractable approximation (Figure 1, third and last column). Weight rounding. Once the representation and quantization grid are fixed, rounding assigns each weight to a representable value. Round-to-nearest treats weights independently, whereas adaptive rounding methods coordinate these decisions so that later choices can compensate for errors intro-duced earlier (Nagel et al., 2020; Frantar et al., 2023). Existing methods generally optimize either a layer-reconstruction objective or a weight-space approximation to the downstream model loss. 

Transformations. For any invertible transformation T ∈ Ri×i, transforming the activations and weights preserves the full-precision output, (XT )( T −1W ) = Z, but generally changes the quan-tized output, eZ = Q(XT )Q(T −1W ). We write ∆Z = Z − eZ for the resulting layer-output perturbation. If T reduces a weight group’s range under the same quantizer, Q(T −1W ) can use a finer grid and have lower weight error than Q(W ) in their respective representations (Sun et al., 2025). Diagonal transforms scale channels (Nagel et al., 2019; Xiao et al., 2023; Lin et al., 2023). Orthogonal transforms mix channels through randomized Hadamard transforms (Tseng et al., 2024; Ashkboos et al., 2024a), learned rotations (Liu et al., 2024), or blockwise rotations with permuta-tions (Lin et al., 2024). More general transforms combine rotations and scaling (Hu et al., 2025). FlatQuant jointly learns invertible Kronecker factors, channel scaling, and clipping through block reconstruction (Sun et al., 2025). CAT (Federici et al., 2026) and WUSH (Chen et al., 2026a) design transforms to minimize a local error proxy. 

## 3 METHOD 

Our derivation proceeds in four steps. We first express PTQ as the minimization of the prediction divergence induced by replacing one full-precision layer with its quantized counterpart. We then approximate this divergence by a quadratic form in the layer-output perturbation. Because the re-sulting output Hessian is intractably large, we introduce a tractable approximation. Finally, we use this common quadratic proxy to derive practical optimization procedures for transformations, weight scales and rounding, and activation clipping. 3.1 JOINT OBJECTIVE

Quantization and Calibration We consider two quantization granularities and evaluate three con-figurations: (i) W4A4KV4 , a channel-wise setting in which weights, activations, and the KV cache are quantized to 4-bit integers (Sun et al., 2025; Ashkboos et al., 2024b; Federici et al., 2026); weights use one scale per output channel, while activations use one scale per token; (ii) W4-g32 , a block-wise weight-only setting in which groups of 32 weight values share a floating-point scale, sim-ilar to the one used in Tseng et al. (2026); and (iii) W4A4-g32 , a block-wise weight-and-activation setting in which groups of 32 values share a floating-point scale (Chen et al., 2026b). For all meth-ods, quantization parameters and Hessian estimates are computed using 2048 randomly sampled sequences of 2048 tokens from the DCLM-Edu dataset (Allal et al., 2025). 

Evaluation Protocol Following standard evaluation practice for LLM quantization (Sun et al., 2025; Federici et al., 2026), we report perplexity on WikiText (Merity et al., 2017) using a context length of 2048 tokens and the mean normalized accuracy across six common-sense reasoning tasks (csr): PIQA (Bisk et al., 2020), WinoGrande (Sakaguchi et al., 2021), HellaSwag (Zellers et al., 2019), ARC-e and ARC-c (Clark et al., 2018), and LAMBADA (Paperno et al., 2016). For methods involving calibration-dependent optimization, we report the mean and standard deviation over three random calibration seeds. 4.1 CHANNEL -WISE W4A4KV4 Q UANTIZATION 

We first evaluate the W4A4KV4 setting, where only a single scale is available for each weight out-put channel and activation token. Results are reported in Table 2. HZ -PTQ consistently achieves the lowest perplexity across all evaluated architectures while matching or improving reasoning ac-curacy. Importantly, the gains are observed for every model family considered, ranging from the 1B-parameter Llama model to larger 8B-parameter architectures. This consistency indicates that the benefits of the proposed objective are not tied to a specific model architecture or size. Compared to FlatQuant, which learns quantization-aware transformations through layer-wise optimization, HZ -PTQ achieves lower perplexity on all four models. Likewise, HZ -PTQ consistently outperforms CAT, despite both methods relying on the same transformation with different parameters. 4.2 BLOCK -WISE QUANTIZATION 

We next consider block-wise quantization using groups of size 32. This setting is particularly chal-lenging for transformation-based methods since smaller quantization groups already reduce range variability, leaving less room for improvement through range equalization alone. Following Chen et al. (2026b), we apply independent block-wise transforms before quantization and fuse the cor-responding inverses into the weights. We evaluate both weight-only (W4-g32) and weight-and-activation (W4A4-g32) quantization scenarios. Results are reported in Table 3. Despite the reduced optimization headroom available in the block-wise setting, HZ -PTQ consis-tently achieves the lowest perplexity and highest accuracy across all evaluated architectures and quantization formats. The improvement persists in both W4 and W4A4 settings, indicating that the proposed objective benefits not only transformation design, but also downstream range selection and rounding decisions. ## 5 DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION 

We introduced a unified Hessian-guided framework for post-training quantization. Unlike exist-ing approaches that optimize transformations, range selection, activation clipping, and rounding independently and using separate reconstruction objectives, our framework derives all quantization decisions from a common approximation of downstream prediction loss. Central to this framework is the proposed Output Kronecker approximation, a tractable output-space Fisher factorization that better correlates with downstream KL divergence than conventional local reconstruction metrics. Across diverse architectures and quantization granularities, jointly optimizing these components consistently improved performance over existing state-of-the-art methods. These results suggest that much of the PTQ design space can be viewed as a single downstream optimization problem rather than a collection of independent heuristics. The method, nevertheless, remains an approximation to the ideal downstream objective. Its deriva-tion relies on a second-order expansion, a Kronecker-rank-one Fisher approximation, a decompo-sition of activation and weight errors, and rounding-noise and covariance assumptions. Moreover, 9estimating output gradients adds calibration time and memory relative to gradient-free reconstruc-tion methods. Future work could investigate richer output-curvature models, adaptive approxima-tions that trade accuracy for calibration cost, and extensions to non-uniform, mixed-precision, and hardware-aware quantization.
