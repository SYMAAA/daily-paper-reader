# ABSTRACT 

Vector quantization (VQ), in particular lattice-based methods, offer strong rate-distortion trade-offs for low-bit large language models (LLMs), yet their behavior under quantization-aware training (VQ-QAT) remains poorly understood. We scale lattice VQ-QAT to billion-parameter LLMs and multi-billion-token training regimes, and introduce SQ UAT , a shape-gain VQ-QAT approach trained with a separate straight-through estimator (STE) for the shape and gain. SQ UAT decomposes weights into direction and magnitude: normalized directions are quantized using an E8-derived spherical codebook, while gains are quantized independently with low-bit scalar quantizers. We show that, unlike a standard STE applied to the full quantizer, which ignores the shape-gain structure, these separate STEs induce tangent-space updates for directions, yielding an implicit form of spherical (Riemannian) optimization. To enable scalability, we develop a distributed exact nearest-neighbor assignment scheme, in which workers quantize disjoint weight blocks and communicate only discrete indices, reducing VQ-QAT training time by 12.8 × and allowing us to scale it to 10B tokens. Across instruction-tuned LLaMA-3.2-1B/3B, Qwen3-4B, and base Qwen3-1.7B, SQ UAT consistently outperforms standard VQ-QAT STE under identical quantizers and initialization, and achieves SOTA VQ-QAT accuracy.

To address this, we introduce SQ UAT , a Spherical Lattice Vector Qu antization-Aware Training method for billion-parameter LLMs, that trains shape-gain quantizers with a factored straight-through estimator (STE), illustrated in Figure 1. SQ UAT represents each weight block using separate trainable direction and gain parameters. In the forward pass, the direction parameter is normalized and quantized with an E8-derived spherical codebook, includ-ing normalized spherical E8P12 codebook (Tseng et al., 2024a) or our proposed E8S construction from equal-norm lattice shells, while the gain is quantized independently with a low-bit scalar quantizer. In the backward pass, the STE is applied separately through the direction and gain quantizers, rather than jointly through the reconstructed weight. This factored STE induces tangent-space updates for the direction parameter, yielding spherical optimization of quantized weight directions while decoupling magnitude optimization into the gain parameter. While spherical parameterizations themselves are well established in machine learning (e.g., DoRA (Liu et al., 2024a)) and quantization, prior work has not analyzed how combining such parameterizations with STE-based quantization alters the surrogate gradients and the resulting optimization dynamics. # 2 BACKGROUND 

2.1 SCALAR QUANTIZATION 

Scalar quantization maps each weight independently to a low-precision discrete set, typically using uniform integer levels (Jacob et al., 2018). During QAT, quantization is applied in the forward pass and gradients are propagated with a straight-through estimator (STE). As our scalar INT-QAT baseline, we use Stretched Elastic Quantization (SEQ) (Liu et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2025), which shifts the signed quantization grid by half a step and improves stability in low-bit regimes. 2.2 VECTOR AND LATTICE QUANTIZATION 

Vector quantization (VQ) groups parameters into blocks w ∈ Rd and maps each block to its nearest codeword from a codebook C = {c1, . . . , c K } ⊂ Rd:

By operating in higher dimensions, VQ can exploit the joint parameter distribution and achieve better rate–distortion trade-offs than scalar quantization. Explicit learned or unstructured codebooks become infeasible in higher dimensions since the number of required codewords (and the cost of nearest-neighbor search) grow exponentially in dimension 

d. Lattice quantization instead uses implicitly specified, highly structured codebooks with strong packing properties, retaining good rate–distortion behavior (Gibson & Sayood, 1988; Conway & Sloane, 2013) while enabling scalable assignment procedures. Yet, VQ-QAT demands repeated nearest-neighbor (NN) assignments during training, making efficient assignment a central challenge. 2.3 SHAPE -GAIN (POLAR ) DECOMPOSITION

In this study, we analyze how STE updates commonly used in VQ interact with shape-gain. 2.4 LATTICE CODEBOOKS FROM E8

The E8 lattice is a highly symmetric lattice in R8 that achieves the densest known sphere packing in eight dimensions (Conway & Sloane, 2013). Its points are organized into norm shells, where 

Sr = {x ∈ E8 : ∥x∥22 = r}. The first nonzero shells contain 240 , 2160 , 6720 , and 17520 points, corresponding to the coefficients of the E8 theta series. Using a single shell, or a union of shells, as a codebook yields a spherical code where nearest-neighbor assignment depends on angular alignment. Since E8 is infinite, finite low-bit codebooks require truncation. We use them either in Euclidean form, retaining lattice norms, or normalized form, projecting codewords to the unit sphere for shape-gain (SG) quantization. In our notation, non-SG variants use Euclidean form, while SG variants use the normalized spherical form. We study two E8-derived constructions. First, QuIP# (Tseng et al., 2024a) introduces E8P12, a structured 2-bit-per-weight vector quantizer with 216 possible codewords per 8D block. E8P12 is derived from an E8-related shifted ˆD8 construction and is designed for compact decoding: its codewords are represented using a small base table together with parity, sign, and shift operations. Second, we introduce E8S, a spherical E8 truncation that enumerates points from low-norm E8 shells, optionally subsamples the final shell to match fractional bit budgets, and normalizes the selected points to form a spherical code. See App. A for details. 

# 3 SQ UAT: S PHERICAL LATTICE VQ-QAT 

Normally, VQ-QAT maintains full-precision shadow parameters in Euclidean space while emulating low-precision inference by applying quantization only in the forward pass. For vector quantizers, the forward map is typically discrete and thus non-differentiable; gradient-based training therefore requires an explicit surrogate for backpropagation. The most common choice is the straight-through estimator (STE). In shape-gain vector quantization, however, STE can be inserted at multiple plausible locations in the computation graph, and these choices lead to different surrogate gradients and, consequently, different optimization dynamics. In this work, we study how STE choices affect optimization for shape-gain vector quantization. We analyze two natural cases: in Sec. 3.1 we consider ‘Standard VQ-QAT:’ the standard joint STE applied to the overall vector quantizer, and in Sec. 3.2 we consider our ‘Spherical VQ-QAT’ in which we apply the STE separately to the shape and gain quantizers, coupled with a spherical parameterization of the weights. We show that these choices induce distinct surrogate gradients and therefore different 

effective updates on the implied Euclidean weight vector. Moreover, we interpret the resulting update of Spherical VQ-QAT as a hyperspherical (Riemannian) update: a tangent-space step for the direction combined with a radial update for the gain, where normalization acts as a retraction. We adopt this update as our training rule, denoted SQ UAT, and validate its benefit empirically. Alternative parameterizations and STE assumptions. While Secs. 3.1–3.2 focus on the two primary regimes, the Euclidean shadow weights with a joint STE (Standard VQ-QAT) and a shape-gain parameterization with factored STE (Spherical VQ-QAT, or SQ UAT), other parameterization and STE choices are possible. In App. B.1, we isolate the effect of the parameterization by using spherical shadow variables (u, γ ) but retaining a joint STE; this yields a tangential–radial update similar in form to SQ UAT, but driven by the unquantized (s, γ ) rather than the quantized forward quantities

Finally, App. B.3 shows an implementation trick that recovers the exact Standard VQ-QAT update while storing parameters in a factored form. Exact nearest-neighbor (NN) search is the main compu-tational bottleneck in VQ-QAT. With N weight blocks and a d-dimensional codebook of size K ≈ 2d·bpw , brute-force search costs O(N Kd ) per quantization step. Unlike PTQ, where search is performed once or a few times, QAT repeats assignment on every forward pass to simulate inference-time quantization. In our implementation, each data-parallel worker computes exact NN (13) for a disjoint subset of vectors using a shared codebook C, and only the resulting integer indices are synchronized via all-gather. Furthermore, a new Triton kernel fuses normalization, codeword selection, and reconstruction. We also cache quantized weights across gradient-accumulation steps, so assignment is invoked only once per optimizer step. For E8S on LLaMA-3.2-1B, these optimizations reduce train-ing time from 62.5 s/it for vanilla VQ-QAT to 4.9 s/it, yielding a 12.8 × speedup while preserving the exact nearest-neighbor assignment, see Figure 3. Overall, our lattice VQ-QAT implementation incurs only moderate overhead relative to scalar QAT.

QuIP (Chee et al., 2023) uses Hadamard rotations to make weights more amenable to quantization, while YAQA (Tseng et al., 2026) improves Hessian estimation. 

Vector and lattice quantization. Vector quantization (VQ) improves low-bit rate–distortion by exploiting correlations across parameters (Tseng et al., 2024a;b; Liu et al., 2024b; Savkin et al., 2025; van der Ouderaa et al., 2026). Representative approaches include AQLM (Egiazarian et al., 2024), which represents weights as sums of multiple codewords, and GPTVQ (Van Baalen et al., 2024), which leverages high-dimensional effects for accurate compression. However, naive VQ scales poorly due to large codebooks and expensive nearest-neighbor search. Lattice VQ addresses this by using structured codebooks derived from dense lattices (Gibson & Sayood, 1988; Conway & Sloane, 2013). QuIP# (Tseng et al., 2024a) introduces an efficient finite subset of the E8 lattice, QTIP (Tseng et al., 2024b) scales VQ via trellis-based decoding, and recent work explores learning lattice structure directly (Zhang et al., 2025). A few recent VQ methods further decompose weights into shape and gain to allocate bits more efficiently. Pyramid Vector Quantization (PVQ) (van der Ouderaa et al., 2024) projects vectors onto the hypersphere using an implicit lattice, while PCDVQ (Yue et al., 2025) explicitly learns separate codebooks for shape and gain. These approaches primarily focus on post-training quantization. 

Quantization-aware training (QAT). Quantization-aware training uses simulated quantization and straight-through estimators during training (Jacob et al., 2018). Prior work improves stability via learned clipping (Choi et al., 2018), learned step sizes (Esser et al., 2020), stochastic rounding (Gupta et al., 2015), and low-rank adaptations (Bondarenko et al., 2024; Loeschcke et al., 2024). Stretched Elastic Quantization (Liu et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2025) further improves low-bit scalar QAT, including in long-horizon LLM training. Only limited work studies QAT with large vector or lattice codebooks. PV-Tuning (Malinovskii et al., 2024) introduces explicit reassignment steps, however they do not study longer token training. LC-QAT (Wang et al., 2026) enables up to 10B QAT through a linear-constrained vector-quantization parameterization. Separately, constraining optimization to spherical manifolds has been shown to improve stability in large-scale LLM training, e.g., via spectral-norm sphere constraints (Xie et al., 2026). DoRA (Liu et al., 2024a) similarly decomposes weights into shape and gain, revealing distinct optimization dynamics in full-precision and low-rank settings. In contrast, we study vector-quantized QAT, where applying separate STEs to shape and gain quantizers induces spherical optimization of quantized weight directions. 

# 7 DISCUSSION , FUTURE WORK , AND LIMITATIONS 

This work studies lattice VQ-QAT at billion-parameter scale and multi-billion-token horizons. With shape-gain parameterization and spherical E8 codebooks, SQ UAT substantially improves over VQ-PTQ baselines and a standard joint-STE VQ-QAT baseline under the same quantizer and initialization. SQ UAT outperforms INT-QAT at 2.25 bpw across almost all models, metrics, and QAT token budgets, while remaining competitive at 2.0 bpw. Mature INT-QAT benefits from a wide range of stabilization techniques, whereas VQ-QAT remains relatively underexplored. To our knowledge, this is the 9Preprint first demonstration of exact nearest-neighbor VQ-QAT at billion-parameter and multi-billion-token scale, enabled by an efficient distributed assignment implementation. Future work should explore VQ-specific training improvements, including adaptations of INT-QAT techniques such as clipping. Our evaluation is limited to E8P12 and E8S codebooks, up to 4B models, and budgets up to 10B tokens. 7B+ models, broader architectures, and higher-bit hierarchical schemes remain future work. Tseng et al. (2024a) demonstrate the hardware efficiency of the E8P12 codebook through a custom CUDA implementation. However, E8P12 is limited to a fixed 2.0 bpw codebook rate. In contrast, E8S enables flexible rate allocation, but currently lacks an efficient dequantizer. Recent work on Leech-lattice VQ (van der Ouderaa et al., 2026) suggests that fast dequantization may also be achievable for spherical lattice codebooks, making efficient E8S implementations a promising direction. 

# 8 CONCLUSION 

We presented SQ UAT, a shape-gain lattice VQ-QAT method for low-bit LLM compression. SQ UAT decomposes each weight block into a normalized direction (shape) and scalar gain, quantizes shape with an E8-derived spherical lattice codebook, quantizes gains separately, and applies separate STEs through the two quantizers. This induces spherical optimization of quantized weight directions, and our ablations show that it improves over a standard VQ STE under the same quantizer and PTQ initialization. To make long-horizon lattice VQ-QAT practical, we introduced distributed exact nearest-neighbor assignment for shared lattice codebooks, reducing training time from 62.5 s/it to 4.9 s/it. Across LLaMA-3.2-1B/3B and Qwen3-4B/1.7B models, QAT with SQ UAT substantially improves performance over VQ-PTQ baselines and a standard joint-STE VQ-QAT baseline, while remaining competitive with scalar INT-QAT at comparable bit budgets. Our ablations further show the importance of chi-based gain quantization, gain bit rate selection, and shadow-weight updates.

Tables 12 and 13 study the effect of blockwise Hadamard rotations after 1B-token QAT on LLaMA-3.2-1B. Across all configurations, Hadamard rotations consistently improve performance. For example, in the E8S-based setting at 2.25 bpw, SQ UAT improves perplexity from 14.53 to 13.85 and MMLU from 37.0 to 40.3 when rotations are enabled. These results suggest that Hadamard rotations and shape-gain offer complementary benefits: Hadamard improves quantization by equalizing statistics, while shape-gain consistently improves accuracy regardless of whether rotations are present. While Hadamard rotations are beneficial, unfused rotations introduce additional latency overhead and may be unsupported on some deployment targets.
