ABSTRACT 

Sequence compression is a fundamental operation and a critical information bot-tleneck in efficient language and multimodal models, and yet prevailing ap-proaches based on pooling or learned projections remain underperforming. We revisit sequence compression through the lens of test-time regression: input tokens define a regression problem whose fitted coefficients form the compressed repre-sentation. Importantly, the regression problem is learned end-to-end via back-propagation, while its coefficients are computed efficiently in closed form during the forward pass. This leads to TTR-Compressor, a general alternative to conven-tional pooling and projection-based compression. We evaluate TTR-Compressor as the compressor for block-wise sparse attention and the visual token merger for multimodal large language models. On block-wise sparse attention, TTR-Compressor improves RULER performance over attention-based compressors by a large margin. For visual token merging, it consistently outperforms the stan-dard MLP merger and matches its performance with 55.6% fewer visual tokens. These results establish test-time regression as an effective primitive for sequence compression in efficient language and multimodal models. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Sequence compression is a ubiquitous operation in modern efficient large model architectures (Qwen Team, 2026b; DeepSeek-AI, 2026), and yet remains surprisingly underexplored. For example, block-wise sparse attention compresses each token block into a summary vector used to score and 1arXiv:2610.31574v1 [cs.LG] 22 Oct 2026 SphereLab Technical Report select relevant blocks (Jiang et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2025; Yuan et al., 2025), while multimodal large language models (MLLMs) merge neighboring visual features into compact tokens before feeding them into the language backbone (Wang et al., 2025; Bai et al., 2025; Team et al., 2026a). In both settings, the compressor acts as a critical information bottleneck. Once information is discarded, it cannot be recovered downstream. One would expect compression to be a carefully designed ar-chitectural component. However, most models rely on simple mechanisms, such as parameter-free pooling, learned projections over concatenated tokens, or at best, attention-weighted pooling. We argue that these default compressors share a fundamental structural limitation, i.e. , they aggre-gate tokens using weights that do not account for inter-token relationships . Mean pooling uses uniform weights, linear projection uses fixed position-dependent weights, and attention-weighted pooling scores each token independently. As a result, redundant tokens can reinforce one another, while distinctive information is diluted. From a statistical perspective, these methods primarily exploit first-order token statistics while ignoring second-order inter-token correlations, the very structure that reveals which information is redundant and which is distinctive. We introduce a fundamentally different compression strategy. Rather than asking which weighted average best summarizes a set of tokens, we let the tokens define a regression problem and take its 

fitted coefficients as the summary. Specifically, our proposed TTR-Compressor, constructs a self-supervised ridge-regression problem directly from the input tokens, requiring no external labels, and uses its closed-form solution as the compressed representation. This formulation creates a nat-ural division: the outer training loop meta-learns which regression problem produces task-useful representations, while the inner closed-form solution determines how that problem should be fitted to each input. The key distinction from conventional pooling and projection arises from the Gram matrix in the regression solution. By explicitly capturing inter-token correlations, TTR-Compressor fits correlated tokens jointly, so redundant evidence has diminishing rather than purely additive in-fluence. This input-adaptive behavior cannot be reproduced by a fixed projection or additive token combination. Moreover, learning the regularizer removes the need for ridge hyperparameter tuning in large-scale pretraining. Because the compression window is typically smaller than the channel dimension ( i.e. , T < d ), we solve the dual problem with an efficient T × T Cholesky factorization. Thus, TTR-Compressor incurs minimal overhead and introduces only 2d learnable parameters per compressor, making it an effective drop-in replacement for sequence compression. We evaluate TTR-Compressor on two applications that impose fundamentally different requirements on compression. In block-wise sparse attention , the compressed summary must act as a faith-ful retrieval key . In this setting, TTR-Compressor consistently outperforms traditional baselines on RULER (Hsieh et al., 2024) retrieval tasks, surpassing sophisticated attention-based compres-sors (Hu et al., 2026) by a considerable margin. In visual token merging , by contrast, the compressed summary must preserve a faithful representation . Here, TTR-Compressor consistently outperforms the widely used MLP merger across diverse compression ratios, and achieves comparable perfor-mance with 55.6% fewer visual tokens. Our contributions are summarized below: • A novel compression framework. We introduce a test-time regression framework for sequence compression, reframing token summarization as an instance-adaptive optimization problem. • A practical operator. We derive an efficient dual closed-form solution and eliminate hyperpa-rameter tuning, making TTR-Compressor fully practical and scalable for large-scale pretraining. • Empirical effectiveness. As a drop-in component in large models, TTR-Compressor consistently outperforms conventional baselines in both block-wise sparse attention and visual token merging. 

2 RELATED WORK 

Sequence compression. Compressing a sequence of tokens into a single vector is a fundamen-tal operation across modern architectures, with applications ranging from multimodal token reduc-tion (Bolya et al., 2022; Li et al., 2023; Yao et al., 2024; Cha et al., 2024; Chu et al., 2024) to block-wise sparse attention (Jiang et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2025; Gao et al., 2024; Lai et al., 2025). Existing compression operators largely fall into three paradigms: (1) parameter-free pooling , such as mean pooling; (2) concatenation followed by learned projection ; and (3) attention-based com-pression , which summarizes tokens via attention-weighted averaging (Mohtashami & Jaggi, 2023; Yang et al., 2025b). A fundamental limitation of these families is that they compute token combi-nations without explicitly accounting for the inter-token correlations. In contrast, TTR-Compressor 2SphereLab Technical Report derives its representation as the solution to a linear system whose Gram matrix directly encodes these correlations, providing an expressive, drop-in replacement for the operators above. 

Test-time regression and test-time training. The idea of adapting part of a network on each input at inference time dates back to fast-weight programmers (Schmidhuber, 1992; Schlag et al., 2021) and was revived as test-time training (TTT), first for robustness under distribution shift (Sun et al., 2020) and then as sequence-modeling layers whose hidden states are small models updated by gra-dient descent on a self-supervised loss (Sun et al., 2024; Behrouz et al., 2026b;a; Han et al., 2026). In-Place TTT (Feng et al., 2026) further extends this paradigm by directly adapting the existing MLP projection weights of pretrained Transformers. A parallel line of work reinterprets linear at-tention and its descendants ( e.g. , DeltaNet (Yang et al., 2024), Gated DeltaNet (Yang et al., 2025c), Longhorn (Liu et al., 2025), Titans (Behrouz et al., 2026b)) as online regression: each layer main-tains a key-to-value regressor that is refined with one gradient or delta-rule step per token and read out by the query. MesaNet (von Oswald et al., 2026) pushes the inner optimization to convergence, solving the problem at every time step with conjugate gradients, while LaCT (Zhang et al., 2026) updates fast weights over large, unordered chunks to improve hardware utilization. TTR-Compressor differs from these methods in two fundamental ways. First, because compression operates independently on small, disjoint blocks without cross-block dependencies, the regression admits an efficient, exact closed-form solution via a single T × T dual solve. This avoids both iter-ative gradient-based inner optimization (as in TTT, ViT 3, In-Place TTT, and MesaNet) and single-step approximations (such as delta-rule updates). Second, rather than using test-time regression to produce input-dependent model weights (Bertinetto et al., 2018; Sun et al., 2020) or a fast-weight memory that is subsequently queried (Schlag et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2025c; Behrouz et al., 2026b), we use regression to directly yield a representation of a token block. Specifically, the fitted coeffi-cients themselves serve as the compressed representation ( e.g. , a visual token or a retrieval key).

5 APPLICATIONS AND EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS 

We evaluate TTR-Compressor in two settings: block-wise sparse attention for efficient language modeling, where compressed representations guide context selection, and visual token merging for efficient multimodal understanding, where they serve as the visual input to the language backbone. 5.1 BLOCK -WISE SPARSE ATTENTION 

Architecture and Baselines. Block-wise sparse attention partitions the context into blocks, scores their compressed representations against each query, and attends to tokens in selected blocks along-side a local window. The compressor therefore determines which distant information remains ac-cessible: an uninformative summary can prevent a relevant block from being selected, regardless of the quality of its individual token representations. We adopt HiLS-Attention (Hu et al., 2026) as a base framework and vary only the compressor. HiLS-Attention has three effective architectural components: hierarchical softmax attention, which com-bines within-block attention with learned block-level weights; low-rank query calibration, which adapts queries for scoring compressed representations; and HoPE, which applies RoPE to only a subset of feature dimensions to support length extrapolation. We compare four compressors: mean pooling, concat & projection, the attention-weighted pooling used in the original HiLS-Attention, and TTR-Compressor. Full attention and sliding-window attention serve as additional references. 

Training and Evaluation. We train 345M-parameter language models on the FineWeb dataset (Penedo et al., 2024) with an 8K context length. Training runs for 30,000 optimization steps with a global batch size of 128 sequences, corresponding to approximately 30B tokens, and uses AdamW with a learning rate of 3 × 10 −4. All sparse attention variants use 64-token blocks, select up to 32 distant blocks per query, and retain a 512-token local window. Evaluation covers two complementary aspects. First, since queries use the compressed represen-tations to retrieve relevant blocks, retrieval accuracy directly reflects compression quality. Results. As shown in Table 1, the choice of compressor largely determines retrieval quality. TTR-Compressor outperforms all baselines by a large margin, and its advantage persists when extrapolat-ing to 16K and 32K. We attribute this to its second-order modeling: pooling and projection add up tokens independently, so the redundant context that fills most of a block drowns out the few infor-mative tokens. The Gram matrix in TTR-Compressor fits correlated tokens jointly and reduces the influence of redundant evidence, so the resulting key keeps the distinctive content that queries need to find. The convolutional variant brings a further gain, reaching 12.24 points over the strongest baseline, as convolution makes the regression targets more informative and thus improves compres-sion quality. On the benchmarks in Table 2, the evaluation contexts are too short to activate sparse attention, so these results only reflect the common-sense reasoning ability acquired during pretrain-ing. Concat & Projection has substantially more parameters than the other compressors, which may benefit pretraining. TTR-Compressor performs well and on par with full attention. 5.2 VISUAL TOKEN MERGER 

Architecture and Baselines. High-resolution images produce many visual tokens, making their di-rect processing by a language backbone expensive. Modern multimodal models therefore commonly use a spatial merger to reduce the visual sequence length. A widely adopted design concatenates each 2 × 2 group of neighboring visual features and maps it to one token through a two-layer MLP (Wang et al., 2025; Bai et al., 2025; Qwen Team, 2026a; Team et al., 2026b). Longer multi-modal contexts and agentic workflows with repeated visual observations further motivate stronger compression, such as 3 × 3 merging (Team et al., 2026a; DeepSeek-AI, 2026). We compare TTR-Compressor with the standard MLP merger. TTR-Compressor fits one regression per spatial group to compress its four or nine visual features into a single vector, which a two-layer MLP then projects to the language model’s hidden dimension. Its convolutional variant applies a 

3 × 3 depthwise convolution to the feature map before constructing the regression targets.

Criticality of Key Normalization. As shown in Table 5, we find that omitting key normalization (kt = xt/∥xt∥2) precipitates a collapse across retrieval tasks. We attribute this to two reasons. First, from an optimization perspective, since scalar targets are linearly generated via vt = a⊤xt,using raw keys ( kt = xt) makes the static weight a a trivial exact fit ( k⊤ 

> t

a ≡ vt). Consequently, the regularized solution h⋆ is biased toward the fixed vector a, which weakens the instance adaptivity of test-time regression and pushes it toward an input-invariant projection. Second, from a geometric and numerical viewpoint, outlier tokens with large norms dominate the Gram matrix KK ⊤, causing correlation modeling to reflect token norms rather than semantic similarity. Normalization resolves both issues at once: it breaks the trivial alignment with static parameters and prevents large-norm tokens from dominating the Gram matrix. 

From Static Heuristics to Anisotropic Regularization. Table 6 compares fixed regularization scalars against learnable formulations. All fixed settings outperform the baseline. Moderate val-ues around λ = 10 −2 perform well, while performance decreases when λ is too small ( 0.4364 at 

λ = 10 −4) or too large ( 0.4238 at λ = 1 ). Notably, a Learnable Scalar already achieves a com-petitive average accuracy of 0.4971 , demonstrating that the outer-loop task objective can reliably discover effective regularization strengths end-to-end. Extending this to a coordinate-wise Learn-able Diagonal (D = diag(softplus( ρ)) ) achieves the overall best performance. This progression shows the benefit of learning a separate regularization strength for each channel: the model penalizes less useful feature directions more strongly while preserving task-critical ones. 

6 CONCLUDING REMARKS 

In this work, we revisit sequence compression, a pervasive yet often overlooked bottleneck in mod-ern architectures, through the lens of test-time regression. Instead of relying on fixed pooling or learned projections, we formulate token compression as an instance-adaptive ridge regression problem, allowing the compressed representation to be solved directly from each input sequence. By exploiting inter-token correlations through second-order interactions, TTR-Compressor offers a more expressive way to preserve sequence information while remaining lightweight and parameter-efficient. Its dual-form solution further makes this formulation scalable in practice. Empirically, TTR-Compressor performs strongly across both block-wise sparse attention and multimodal token reduction, improving long-context retrieval while matching standard visual mergers with signif-icantly fewer tokens. More broadly, our results suggest that sequence compression need not be viewed as static aggregation, but as an input-dependent inference problem that adapts to the structure of each sequence. We expect this perspective establishes test-time regression as a simple, expressive, and scalable primitive for efficient foundation models. 9SphereLab Technical Report

C IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS 

Numerical Precision. Convolution and target projection use the input precision (BF16), while re-gression keys and targets are cast to FP32 before the dual solve. Diagonal regularization is evaluated in FP32. We solve the positive-definite dual system using Cholesky factorization without explicitly forming an inverse, and cast the compressed representation back to the input dtype. 

Local Target Construction. Only the regression targets incorporate convolutional context; keys are computed from the original inputs. For language, we apply a non-causal depthwise 1D convolution with kernel size 5, independently within each compression block, using two-sided zero padding of width 2. The convolution has neither bias nor activation and is initialized to the identity. For vision, we use a 3×3 depthwise convolution. Each channel has its own spatial kernel, with no cross-channel mixing before the scalar target projection. The base compressor has 2d effective trainable parameters: d for the target projection and d for di-agonal regularization. A bias-free depthwise convolution with s kernel elements adds sd parameters and O(sT d ) computation. Thus, the language and vision variants require 7d and 11 d parameters, re-spectively. Their total computational complexity remains O(T 2d) for fixed kernel sizes and T ≪ d.

D EXPERIMENTAL DETAILS 

Block-wise Sparse Attention. HiLS-Attention (Hu et al., 2026) argues that small models provide a more diagnostic setting for RULER’s predominantly “find-and-copy” tasks, as larger models may compensate for retrieval inaccuracies through greater information bandwidth. We therefore follow their setup and use 345M-parameter models to evaluate compression quality. All models use 16 Transformer layers, a hidden dimension of 1,024, an FFN dimension of 4,096, and grouped-query attention with 16 query heads and 2 key–value heads. We use rank-64 query calibration. Training uses AdamW weight decay of 0.01 and gradient clipping at a norm of 1.0; normalization parameters, biases, and embeddings are excluded from weight decay. The learning rate warms up over the first 

0.7% of training and remains constant thereafter. 

Visual Token Merger. We use BF16 training with DeepSpeed ZeRO-1, gradient checkpointing, and a maximum sequence length of 16,384 tokens. The two stages use per-device batch sizes of 4 and 1, with gradient accumulation over 4 and 8 steps, respectively. Both stages apply a 3% learning-rate warmup followed by cosine decay, with minimum learning rates of 10 −5 and 0 and weight decay of 

0.01 and 0.02 , respectively. For TTR-Compressor, the regression systems are solved in FP32.

E.1 SCALING UP MODEL SIZE AND TRAINING BUDGET 

To further verify that the advantage of TTR-Compressor is not merely an artifact of the smaller-scale setting used in the main paper, we scale up both the model size and the training budget. Specifi-cally, we train a 1B-parameter model on 100B tokens and evaluate it on the same RULER sub-tasks across context lengths ranging from 4K to 32K. For comparison, we also scale up Attention-Weighted Pooling—the strongest baseline among all compression methods evaluated in the main paper’s 345M-parameter experiments—to the same 1B-parameter, 100B-token setting. As shown in Table 7, TTR-Compressor consistently outperforms the Attention-Weighted Pooling baseline across almost all context lengths and subtasks, achieving an average accuracy of 0.7996 compared to 0.735. Notably, the performance gap widens as the context length increases, indicating that TTR-Compressor scales more gracefully to longer contexts even when both model size and training budget are substantially increased relative to the main paper’s 345M-parameter setting.
