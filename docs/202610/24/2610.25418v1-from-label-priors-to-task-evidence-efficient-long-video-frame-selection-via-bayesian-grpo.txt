## Abstract 

Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) face significant scalability chal-lenges in long-video understanding due to quadratic attention complexity and context window constraints. Existing retrieval-based approaches typically rely on supervised learning from static keyframe annotations. However, these label priors often fail to align with the actual information required by downstream MLLMs for complex reasoning, creating a misalignment between the training target and inference needs. To bridge this gap, we propose a probabilistic frame-work that reformulates frame selection as latent-variable inference. We first ini-tialize a lightweight policy using supervised learning to capture general visual saliency. Then, we treat a frozen MLLM as a task environment and use its an-swer likelihood as dense task evidence to refine the policy via Bayesian Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). Within this formulation, the negative log-likelihood (NLL) over ground-truth answer tokens provides a principled dense reward, while a prior-matching KL term preserves semantic consistency with the elicited label prior. This process transitions the selection principle from static label priors to task evidence. Extensive experiments on Video-MME, MLVU, and LongVideoBench demonstrate that our selector achieves competitive accuracy with MLLM-based scorers while retaining the inference efficiency of lightweight meth-ods. The selector is trained once with a frozen MLLM environment and transfers directly to multiple downstream MLLMs without retraining. Code is available at 

github.com/Miraclecsc/Frame_Selection_GRPO .

## 1 Introduction 

Recent advancements in Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) have revolutionized video understanding [1–3, 8, 57, 10, 54, 17], enabling sophisticated reasoning over interwoven visual-textual data. However, despite their impressive zero-shot capabilities and instructional alignment, MLLMs face a fundamental struggle when processing long-form videos [9, 36, 13, 18]. The quadratic computational complexity inherent to self-attention mechanisms [43], coupled with strict context window limitations, typically prohibits the direct ingestion of lengthy, uncompressed frame sequences. Consequently, extracting relevant spatiotemporal information from temporally extended content remains a critical bottleneck, hindering the deployment of MLLMs in real-world applications such as movie analysis and long-horizon agent navigation. To mitigate this scalability issue, existing approaches [46, 32, 60, 56, 31] generally fall into two dom-inant paradigms: architectural modification and external frame retrieval. Architectural approaches, such as streaming video LLMs [7, 22, 33, 21] or dynamic memory compression, attempt to fun-damentally modify the MLLM’s internal token-processing mechanism to accommodate infinite or

highly compressed contexts. While theoretically promising, these methods often incur prohibitive training costs and suffer from catastrophic forgetting, degrading the model’s general-purpose ca-pabilities. Alternatively, retrieval-based methods [4, 37, 55, 62] bypass architectural changes by employing an external module to select a concise subset of keyframes. However, this paradigm suffers from a severe efficiency-accuracy trade-off. Lightweight selectors ( e.g. , CLIP-based similarity matchers) [41, 24, 39, 23, 52] rely on shallow semantic alignment and lack the deep causal reasoning required for complex, multi-hop temporal queries. Conversely, utilizing a powerful MLLM as a dense frame scorer [64, 51, 50, 47] bridges the reasoning gap but introduces unacceptable computational overhead and latency during inference. In this work, we propose a lightweight, plug-and-play selection module that achieves high reasoning accuracy without imposing heavy inference latency. We identify a fundamental theoretical misalign-ment in current retrieval-based methods: a static label prior is not strictly identical to task evidence .Standard supervised selectors trained on fixed human annotations learn the former (typically visually salient or summary frames), but downstream MLLMs require the latter (specific visual evidence necessary to complete a logical reasoning chain). To bridge this semantic gap, we depart from a ranking paradigm and reformulate frame selection as a probabilistic latent-variable modeling problem. Specifically, we introduce a rigorous two-stage probabilistic framework that transitions from human-derived label priors to MLLM-driven task evidence. First, we construct a lightweight selector that learns a Label Prior from keyframe annotations via pairwise ranking. This initializes the policy within a semantically plausible region, drastically reducing the combinatorial search space. Second, we perform Posterior Inference to dynamically align this prior with the target MLLM. By treating the frozen MLLM as the environment and maximizing the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO), we naturally utilize the MLLM’s Negative Log-Likelihood (NLL) as dense task evidence, while simultaneously leveraging the elicited prior for principled KL-regularization. We solve this optimization using a novel Bayesian Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) algorithm, which effectively refines the policy into a task-adaptive posterior that autonomously identifies reasoning-critical frames without reward hacking. Our main contributions are summarized as follows: • We propose a plug-and-play, lightweight frame selector that decouples selection from inference, enabling highly efficient long-video understanding without modifying the target MLLM’s architecture. • We introduce a probabilistic framework that reformulates frame selection as Bayesian inference, theoretically justifying the sequential transition from supervised Prior Elicitation to MLLM-driven Posterior Inference. • We derive a novel Bayesian GRPO algorithm. By leveraging the target MLLM’s answer likelihood as dense task evidence and the label prior as KL-regularization, our method robustly refines the selector while mitigating reward hacking. • We empirically demonstrate that our selector acts as a universal plugin across multiple downstream MLLMs, achieving a superior accuracy-efficiency trade-off compared to both training-free lightweight methods and computationally heavy MLLM-based scorers.

2.2 Bayesian Methods in Large Models 

Bayesian frameworks [25, 35, 49] have seen a resurgence for quantifying uncertainty and integrating priors in large models. Recent studies such as Bayesian Evaluation of LLM Behavior [30] and Bayesian Mixture of Experts [11] demonstrate that probabilistic modeling can effectively calibrate model outputs and mitigate hallucinations. Recent works [5, 48] formulates LLMs as prior action dis-tributions, showing that Bayesian inference can significantly accelerate exploration in reinforcement learning. This line of research provides strong theoretical grounding for treating supervised signals as priors and downstream task feedback as evidence, a core tenet of our proposed framework. 

2.3 Policy Optimization and Reinforcement Learning for MLLMs 

Reinforcement learning has become a standard paradigm for aligning MLLMs [20, 28, 29, 14, 59] with complex reasoning tasks. General-purpose reasoning has been enhanced by algorithms like Hint-GRPO [19], which applies Group Relative Policy Optimization to boost mathematical and multimodal reasoning capabilities. In the specific domain of frame selection, recent works have explored various decision-making formulations. Focus [63] models keyframe selection as a combinatorial multi-armed bandit problem to balance exploration and exploitation. Similarly, FrameMind [16] employs trajectory-level RL to dynamically request visual information during reasoning. Unlike these methods which often rely on sparse rewards or heuristic bandits, our approach leverages a dense NLL-based reward signal within a rigorous Bayesian inference framework to achieve task-adaptive frame selection. 

## 3 Methodology 

We formulate long-video frame selection as a probabilistic latent-variable modeling problem, as shown in Figure 1. We use V ∈ RN ×D to denote a sequence of N candidate frames uniformly sampled from the raw video, Q to denote a textual query, and y to denote the ground-truth answer. Here, N is reserved for the number of candidate frames, while L denotes the number of Transformer blocks in the selector. 3Our objective is to learn a parameterized policy πθ (z | V , Q) that selects a discrete subset z ⊂{1, . . . , N } with cardinality |z| = K, maximizing the marginal likelihood of the correct answer under a frozen Multimodal Large Language Model.

## 4 Experiment 

4.1 Experimental Setup Models and Baselines. We compare our approach against two distinct categories of frame se-lection strategies, lightweight scoring and MLLM-based methods. The first category consists of lightweight scoring methods. This includes methods utilizing efficient vision-language models, such as SigLIP2 [42], to measure semantic alignment. Specific approaches include AKS [41], which segments videos based on adaptive similarity; BOLT [24], which computes importance scores fol-lowed by inverse transform sampling; and Q-Frame [58], which employs dynamic resolution based on cross-modal retrieval. F2C [39] utilizes CLIP-based anchor keys to adjust clip lengths during selection. Optimization-based methods like MDP3 [40] and Nar-KFC [12] formulate selection as subset or subgraph problems using mathematical estimation. Additionally, Focus [63] employs multi-armed bandits to balance exploration and exploitation, while Frame-Voyager [52] and FrameO-racle [23] use pairwise ranking or joint importance prediction to identify keyframe sets. The second category, MLLM-based methods, leverages heavy multimodal models for direct scoring. Examples include ViaRL [47] and Kframes [51], which apply reinforcement learning for temporal grounding; A.I.R. [64], which uses a two-stage iterative selection process; and GenS [50], which treats frame scoring as a generative sampling task. Results are reported over multiple runs. Efficiency and Training Cost Analysis are provided in Appendix C. 

Benchmarks and Implementation Details. We evaluate our method on three widely adopted long-form video benchmarks, covering diverse domains and reasoning granularities. Details about benchmarks are provided in Appendix C. We utilize SigLIP2-SO400M-Patch16-512 [42] as the shared visual-textual backbone for feature pre-extraction. More details are provided in Appendix C.

## C Implementation Details 

In this section, we provide comprehensive implementation details to ensure the reproducibility of our method. 

C.1 Benchmarks. Video-MME [15] serves as a large-scale multimodal evaluation suite, assessing capabilities in video understanding, reasoning, and knowledge-grounded tasks. It incorporates both short and long-form videos to test context management strategies comprehensively. LongVideoBench [45] focuses specifically on long-form scenarios, with videos spanning tens of minutes to hours. It challenges models with tasks such as VQA, summarization, and temporal grounding, rigorously testing temporal reasoning efficiency. MLVU [61] evaluates hierarchical comprehension. It tests models on multiple levels of reasoning, including frame-level recognition, clip-level temporal understanding, and video-level holistic comprehension, ensuring that the selection method preserves both local details and global context. 

C.2 Implementation Details. 

For all frame-selection methods evaluated under our controlled setting, candidate frames are uniformly sampled from the raw video at 1 FPS before selection. Given a video of duration T seconds, this produces N = T candidate frames, and each method selects K frames from the same candidate pool. Unless otherwise specified, lightweight baselines including AKS, BOLT, Q-Frame, F2C, and 15 our method use the same SigLIP2-SO400M features, ensuring that performance differences are not caused by different feature extractors.The lightweight selector consists of L = 4 blocks of our proposed Video-Text Fusion Transformers. The hidden dimension is set to D = 1152 to align with the visual and textual features. We train the selector on the GenS-Video-150K dataset with 150k video-question pairs. The training is conducted in two stages. In Stage I, we optimize the pairwise ranking loss with a learning rate of 1e−4. In Stage II, we fine-tune the selector using Bayesian GRPO with a learning rate of 5e−6. The Stage II environment is Qwen2.5-VL-7B, which is kept frozen throughout training. After training, the selector is directly deployed to other downstream MLLMs without additional posterior training. All training and evaluation experiments are conducted on 8 NVIDIA H100 (80GB) GPUs.

C.4 Efficiency and Training Cost Analysis 

A central goal of our method is to preserve the inference efficiency of lightweight selectors while avoiding the heavy test-time overhead of MLLM-based scorers. We therefore report both compu-tational cost and measured latency under a common setting. Following the evaluation protocol, candidate frames are sampled at 1 FPS. For a 300-second video with K = 32 , we estimate the selector-side computational cost with fp16 model weights and report the peak memory usage. As shown in Table 8, our selector introduces only a small overhead over SigLIP2-based lightweight methods, increasing the estimated cost from 240–241 GFLOPs to 264 GFLOPs and memory from 0.80GB to 0.93GB. In contrast, MLLM-based scorers require repeated large-model inference over candidate frames or windows, leading to orders-of-magnitude higher cost. We further measure wall-clock latency on Video-MME using a single NVIDIA H100 GPU, with 

K = 32 and candidate frames sampled at 1 FPS. The average per-sample latency is reported in Table 9. Our method remains close to lightweight training-free methods and is substantially faster than generative MLLM-based scoring. Although our method is not training-free, the training cost is incurred only once. Stage I learns the label prior from keyframe annotations, and Stage II uses a frozen Qwen2.5-VL-7B environment for posterior refinement. After training, the selector is reused as a standalone module and directly transferred to different downstream MLLMs without re-running Stage II. Therefore, the training cost is amortized across benchmarks, frame budgets, and downstream MLLMs, while inference requires only one selector forward pass for each video-query pair.

## D Dataset Details 

We utilize the GenS-Video-150K [50] dataset for training. The dataset annotation pipeline leverages GPT-4o to construct grounded video QAs and score frame relevance from 0 (irrelevant) to 5 (most relevant). To ensure comprehensive coverage of video understanding capabilities, the dataset includes 12 specific question types categorized into three primary domains: (1) Reasoning Tasks , which test the model’s ability to infer logical relationships involving objects, actions, spatial configurations, and temporal changes; (2) Perception Tasks , focusing on the basic visual understanding of scenes, attributes, and spatial layouts; and (3) Specialized Tasks , which assess fine-grained capabilities including video detail referring, counting, OCR, and temporal sequence perception.
