Methods, Trade-offs, and Evaluation

Abstract
Video Large Language Models (VLMs) have
demonstrated strong capabilities in video un-
derstanding and reasoning, but processing long
visual sequences incurs substantial computa-
tional, latency, and memory overhead. This
survey examines training-free inference accel-
eration, focusing on reducing inference costs
while preserving task-relevant spatiotempo-
ral evidence. We organize existing methods
by their intervention stages in the inference
pipeline, covering input, visual encoding, pro-
jector, prefill, and decoding. Post-training quan-
tization (PTQ) is discussed separately as a
cross-stage extension with varying calibration
requirements. We analyze how intervention
position affects evidence preservation and com-
putational savings, and why local efficiency im-
provements may not translate into end-to-end
acceleration. We complement this analysis with
quantitative evidence from prior studies and our
own experiments under a shared evaluation set-
ting to assess accuracy–latency trade-offs.

Introduction
VLMs extend language-model reasoning to video
by integrating encoded visual representations with
textual queries, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Processing
more frames or higher-resolution inputs increases
visual encoding and multimodal context process-
ing costs (Maaz et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025a;
Wang et al., 2025c; Bai et al., 2025b; Yuan et al.,
2025b; Li et al., 2025b; Zhang et al., 2026c). How-
ever, video redundancy is non-uniform: brief ac-
tions may provide critical evidence, while visually
similar frames may contain different task-relevant
information (Ye et al., 2025a; Wang et al., 2025d;
Tang et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025d). Efficient
VLM inference must therefore reduce redundant
computation while preserving the spatiotemporal
evidence required for reliable video understanding
(Wang et al., 2026a; Zhang et al., 2026c).

Training-free acceleration seeks to reduce VLM
inference costs without training dedicated accel-
eration modules. These methods operate across
the inference pipeline: input-stage methods reduce
frames or spatial resolution (Zhu et al., 2026; Shi
et al., 2026); encoding-stage methods merge or
reuse visual features (Wang et al., 2026d; Wu et al.,
2026b); projector- and prefill-stage methods re-
duce visual-token or KV-cache overhead (Ma et al.,
2026c; Li et al., 2026a); and decoding-stage meth-
ods reduce KV-cache access or generation over-
head (Zeng et al., 2026; Kong et al., 2026). In-
tervention position affects both computational sav-
ings and available information: earlier stages can
avoid more downstream computation, whereas later

stages exploit richer representations. However, lo-
cal savings do not necessarily translate into end-to-
end acceleration.
Existing surveys have examined multimodal ef-
ficiency from complementary perspectives. Some
provide broad reviews of efficient MLLM/LVLM
architectures and inference strategies (Jin et al.,
2025; Pathak and Han, 2026; Zhang et al., 2026c),
while others focus on multimodal token compres-
sion or organize compression along the inference
lifecycle (Shao et al., 2025; Yao et al., 2026; Wu
et al., 2026a).
Recent work has also surveyed
training-free long-video understanding (Liu et al.,
2025a). However, the combined effects of interven-
tion position on spatiotemporal evidence preserva-
tion and realized inference efficiency remain less
explored in training-free VLM acceleration.
To address these gaps, we examine where
training-free acceleration intervenes, how its bene-
fits and limitations vary across stages, and whether
local savings translate into end-to-end gains. Our
contributions are threefold: (1) we present a stage-
wise taxonomy of training-free VLM acceleration,
covering input, visual encoding, projector, pre-
fill, and decoding, with PTQ discussed separately
as a cross-stage extension; (2) we analyze how
intervention position affects the preservation of
task-relevant spatiotemporal evidence and poten-
tial computational savings; and (3) we synthesize
evidence from prior studies and conduct experi-
ments under a shared backbone and input protocol,
introducing Quality–Latency Balance (QLB) as a
baseline-normalized indicator of accuracy–latency
performance.
2
Preliminaries
2.1
VLMs for Video Understanding
Video understanding requires reasoning over tem-
porally distributed evidence, including object
states, actions, event order, and long-range depen-
dencies. Unlike static images, relevant evidence
may span multiple frames and depend on their tem-
poral relationships (Meng et al., 2026; An et al.,
2026). VLMs encode such spatiotemporal informa-
tion for language-model reasoning (Nguyen et al.,
2024).
As illustrated in Fig. 1, a typical VLM consists
of a vision encoder, a projector, and a large lan-
guage model (LLM). Given a video V and query T ,
input processing produces V′ = S(V), followed by
Xv = Eϕ(V′) and Hv = Pθ(Xv) ∈RNv×d. Let
Ht denote the embedded query tokens. In this sim-
plified pipeline, the LLM receives C = [Hv; Ht].
During prefill, the LLM processes this context, con-
structs the KV cache, and produces the first out-
put token. Autoregressive decoding then accesses
and updates the cache to generate subsequent to-
kens. Importantly, token compression after visual
encoding can reduce LLM computation but cannot
recover the encoding cost already incurred.
2.2
Why Training-Free
Rapidly evolving VLM architectures make it costly
to retrain and maintain backbone-specific accel-
eration modules (Wang et al., 2024b; Bai et al.,
2025b,a). Training-free methods avoid such re-
training and can be more readily applied to ex-
isting models (Yang et al., 2025a; Zhang et al.,
2025b). In this survey, training-free acceleration1
refers to methods that require no additional train-
ing or fine-tuning to obtain their acceleration ca-
pability. Frozen auxiliary models are permitted,
but their runtime overhead must be accounted for.
Methods requiring acceleration-specific training
are excluded from our core scope.
3
Taxonomy
As shown in Fig. 2, we organize training-free VLM
acceleration methods by their intervention stages in
the inference pipeline: Input (Sec. 3.1), Encoding
(Sec. 3.2), Projector (Sec. 3.3), Prefill (Sec. 3.4),
and Decoding (Sec. 3.5). This stage-wise organi-
zation highlights differences in computational sav-
ings and information availability. We additionally
review PTQ (Sec. 3.6) as a cross-stage extension,
distinguishing its calibration-dependent techniques
from the core training-free methods.
3.1
Input Stage
Input-stage methods reduce or adapt visual content
before encoding, potentially saving both visual en-
coding and downstream computation. They mainly
operate through keyframe selection (Sec. 3.1.1) and
spatial adaptation (Sec. 3.1.2).
3.1.1
Keyframe Selection
Reducing the frame budget lowers visual process-
ing costs but also limits downstream reasoning to
the retained temporal evidence. Existing methods
differ in whether selection is guided by the query.

3.1.2
Spatial Adaptation
Beyond temporal selection, resolution adjustment
and region-focused processing offer another way to
reduce visual computation. Free VLM employs
prompt-guided cropping (Han et al., 2024), Q-
Frame’s MRA component allocates resolution by
query relevance (Zhang et al., 2025c), and F2C bal-
ances resolution and clip length (Sun et al., 2025b).
LENS further combines spatial zoom-in with tem-
3


poral zoom-out (Zhang et al., 2026a). Reducing
spatial computation, however, may obscure fine-
grained evidence or remove context needed for ac-
curate reasoning.
3.2
Encoding Stage
Encoding-stage methods reduce visual computa-
tion by merging or reusing intermediate features.
The first stage of EarlyTom merges temporally re-
dundant features in shallow encoder layers (Wang
et al., 2026a), ST-Merge performs spatiotempo-
ral token merging (Chen et al., 2026a), and STC-
Cacher reuses stable features while selectively
updating dynamic content (Wang et al., 2026d).
Early merging reduces subsequent encoding costs
but may obscure subtle motion, whereas caching
avoids repeated computation at the risk of stale
features and additional matching overhead.
3.3
Projector Stage
Projector-stage methods compress visual tokens af-
ter encoding but before LLM processing, reducing
prefill costs without saving the encoding compu-
tation already incurred. They mainly employ to-
ken selection (Sec. 3.3.1) and token aggregation
(Sec. 3.3.2).
3.3.1
Token Selection
Selecting fewer visual tokens reduces LLM context
length but limits the evidence available for reason-
ing. Existing approaches differ in whether they
use attention or feature-derived signals to estimate
token importance.
Attention-Aware. The second stage of EarlyTom
uses encoder attention for spatial selection (Wang
et al., 2026a), while KTV’s second stage com-
bines [CLS] attention with intra-frame similarity
(Song et al., 2026). VisionZip retains dominant
and contextual tokens (Yang et al., 2025b), whereas
LLaVA-PruMerge combines attention-based selec-
tion with feature merging (Shang et al., 2025). The
CTR component of StreamingTOM selects tokens
using temporal changes and visual saliency (Chen
et al., 2026b). Such attention cues, however, may
not align with query-specific evidence.
Attention-Agnostic. ReTaKe-DPSelect exploits
temporal differences (Wang et al., 2024c), while
STC-Pruner evaluates spatiotemporal token nov-
elty (Wang et al., 2026d). VidCom2 uses frame and
token-level uniqueness (Liu et al., 2025c), whereas
V-CAST allocates temporal budgets based on cur-
vature and preserves representative spatial tokens
(Lin et al., 2026). These feature-based criteria can
improve coverage but may retain content unrelated
to the query.
3.3.2
Token Aggregation
To retain more information from redundant tokens,
aggregation merges similar representations rather
than discarding them. AIM performs similarity-
based merging (Zhong et al., 2025), PruneVid com-
bines temporally static and spatially similar tokens
(Huang et al., 2025b), and HoliTom employs global
temporal segmentation followed by spatiotempo-
ral merging (Shao et al., 2026). While merging
can preserve information lost by hard selection, it
may blur fine-grained distinctions and introduce
additional processing overhead.
3.4
Prefill Stage
Prefill-stage methods exploit intermediate LLM
representations to reduce subsequent computation
or KV cache storage. We categorize them into
token selection (Sec. 3.4.1), token aggregation
(Sec. 3.4.2), KV cache selection (Sec. 3.4.3), and
KV cache aggregation (Sec. 3.4.4).
3.4.1
Token Selection
Pruning visual tokens within the LLM reduces com-
putation in subsequent layers, although earlier lay-
ers must still process the original sequence. Meth-
ods mainly use attention-based or similarity-based
signals.
Attention-Based.
FastV and the second stage
of PruneVid remove low-attention tokens (Chen
et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2025b), while AIM’s
second stage propagates token importance through
attention (Zhong et al., 2025). AdaTP corrects
global and local attention biases (Sun et al., 2025a),
whereas PriorTR separates instruction relevance
from model-induced attention priors (Chen et al.,
2026d). Attention offers task-sensitive guidance,
but biased scores may discard critical visual evi-
dence.
Similarity-Based. DART removes tokens redun-
dant with selected pivots (Wen et al., 2025), while
SAINT applies similarity-aware pruning (Jeddi
et al., 2025). Such criteria reduce reliance on atten-
tion scores, although similar features may encode
distinct events.
3.4.2
Token Aggregation
Rather than discarding visual tokens, aggregation
merges redundant representations to retain part of
their information. STTM performs multi-granular
4


spatiotemporal merging (Hyun et al., 2025), while
HoliTom’s inner stage combines attention and sim-
ilarity (Shao et al., 2026). Unified introduces text-
aware merging within the LLM (Du et al., 2026),
whereas FrameFusion and PACT combine merging
with pruning (Fu et al., 2025b; Dhouib et al., 2025).
Merging can preserve contextual information, but
may blur subtle event distinctions and incur addi-
tional processing overhead.
3.4.3
KV Cache Selection
Beyond token reduction, KV cache selection limits
the states retained for subsequent attention. Re-
TaKe’s PivotKV retains representative frame states
during chunked prefill (Wang et al., 2024c), while
InfiniPot-V and StreamMem maintain bounded his-
torical memories through selective retention (Kim
et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2025c). This reduces cache
pressure but risks losing historical evidence needed
by later queries.
3.4.4
KV Cache Aggregation
To mitigate information loss from KV eviction,
LOOK-M merges selected cache states (Wan et al.,
2024), while MEDA combines KV merging with
layer-adaptive cache allocation (Wan et al., 2025).
StreamMem further maintains frame-level sum-
maries alongside salient states (Yang et al., 2025c).
These compact representations reduce persistent
memory costs, but cannot recover the prefill com-
putation already incurred and may introduce addi-
tional merging overhead.
3.5
Decoding Stage
Autoregressive decoding repeatedly accesses accu-
mulated KV states, making time per output token
(TPOT) sensitive to long visual contexts. Training-
free methods mainly employ speculative decoding
(Sec. 3.5.1), KV cache selection (Sec. 3.5.2), and
KV cache retrieval (Sec. 3.5.3).
3.5.1
Speculative Decoding
Speculative decoding (SD)2 accelerates genera-
tion through draft-and-verify execution (Leviathan
et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023). SpecVLM reduces
drafting costs through verifier-guided visual prun-
ing (Ji et al., 2025), while Sparse-to-Dense (StD)
combines sparse drafting with dense verification
(Zhang et al., 2025e). ParallelVLM overlaps draft-
ing and verification (Kong et al., 2026), whereas

tance (Ji et al., 2026). Speedup depends on drafting

3.5.2
KV Cache Selection
Reducing the visual KV states accessed during gen-
eration can lower repeated attention costs (Tu et al.,
2025). METok removes redundant visual KV states
during decoding (Wang et al., 2025a), while the
second stage of DyCoke dynamically updates the
active cache according to generation-time attention
(Tao et al., 2025a). Such selection reduces cache
access but may discard evidence needed by subse-
quent tokens.
3.5.3
KV Cache Retrieval
Rather than permanently discarding historical
states, retrieval preserves broader video context
for selective access. SparseVILA uses query-aware
visual KV access (Khaki et al., 2025), while ReKV
retrieves relevant states from stored video history
(Di et al., 2025). LiveVLM’s PaR enables page-
level retrieval (Ning et al., 2025), whereas Stream-
ingTOM’s OQM retrieves and dequantizes selected
KV groups (Chen et al., 2026b). Such access pre-
serves long-range evidence but adds retrieval over-
head, with latency benefits depending on when
states are loaded.
3.6
Post-Training Quantization
Post-training quantization (PTQ) reduces the pre-
cision of weights, activations, or KV states to
lower memory demands and potentially acceler-
ate inference. As a cross-stage extension, PTQ
may involve calibration or optimization beyond the
core training-free scope. We categorize methods
into weight-only quantization (Sec. 3.6.1), weight-
activation quantization (Sec. 3.6.2), and KV cache
quantization (Sec. 3.6.3).
3.6.1
Weight-Only Quantization

memory traffic while retaining higher-precision ac-
tivations. VLMQ incorporates token importance
into Hessian-based quantization (Xue et al., 2025),
while VLM-PTQ introduces modality-aware chan-
nel importance into weight compensation (Deng
and Huang, 2026). MBQ balances vision and lan-
guage sensitivities during calibration (Li et al.,
2025a), whereas QIG estimates fine-grained token
sensitivity using integrated gradients (Xiang et al.,

3.6.2
Weight-Activation Quantization
Further
quantizing
activations
enables
low-
precision computation but increases sensitivity to
quantization errors. Q-VLM considers cross-layer
dependencies during quantization search (Wang
et al., 2024a), while MBQ balances modality-
specific sensitivities (Li et al., 2025a) and QIG
models token-level interactions (Xiang et al., 2026).
MASQuant introduces modality-aware smoothing
and cross-modal compensation (Hu et al., 2026b).
The computational benefits depend on efficient low-
precision execution and sufficient accuracy reten-
tion.
3.6.3
KV Cache Quantization
Reducing KV precision lowers cache memory and
bandwidth demands, particularly for long visual
contexts. CalibQuant uses calibration and post-
scaling for 1-bit visual KV quantization (Han et al.,
2025), while VidKV applies distinct low-bit strate-
gies to key and value caches in VLMs (Tao et al.,
2025b). Dynamic KV Cache Quantization adapts
quantization parameters to runtime KV distribu-
tions (Fan and Chen, 2026). Smaller caches, how-
ever, do not guarantee lower latency when quanti-
zation and dequantization overhead is considered.

hardware, and measurement protocols limit direct
cross-study comparisons. We therefore combine
our controlled experiments with results reported
in prior studies, prioritizing comparisons under
matched settings.3

controlled
experiments
use

Challenges and Future Work
Our taxonomy and evaluation reveal that training-
free acceleration depends not only on reducing vi-
sual tokens, but also on preserving useful evidence
and translating local savings into actual inference
gains. We identify four directions for future re-
search.
Evidence-Aware Compression.
Video redun-
dancy is unevenly distributed, and brief events may
be lost under aggressive compression. DToMA
and RLP explore evidence recovery and semantic
preservation (Yuan et al., 2025a; Ma et al., 2026a),
while DyToK and GTR adapt token budgets to
frame importance and query relevance (Li et al.,
2025b; Zhou et al., 2026). D-CoDe further com-
bines compression with question decomposition
(Huang et al., 2025c). Future methods should co-
ordinate temporal coverage, task relevance, and
selective evidence recovery without introducing
excessive selection overhead.
Early Encoder Optimization. Compared with
post-encoder compression, training-free optimiza-
tion within video encoders remains relatively un-
derexplored. EarlyTom and STC demonstrate the
potential of early merging and feature reuse (Wang
et al., 2026a,d), while MeToM exploits video meta-
data to reduce redundant encoding (Wu et al.,
2026b). Encoder-aware selection in broader VLMs
provides further insights (Wang et al., 2026e; Liu
et al., 2026). Future work should explore motion-
aware feature reuse and coordinated compression
across encoding and prefill, without discarding evi-
dence required by downstream reasoning.
Streaming Memory and Decoding. Streaming
VLMs must preserve historical evidence under
bounded memory while processing frames causally.
StreamingTOM and MuKV explore compressed vi-
sual memory (Chen et al., 2026b; Xiao et al., 2026),
whereas StreamMeCo and StreamKV investigate
persistent memory compression and selective re-
trieval (Wang et al., 2026b; Chen et al., 2026c).
Future systems should jointly manage retention, re-
trieval, and cache updates while maintaining timely
responses to incoming queries (Vasu et al., 2026).
End-to-End System Co-Design. As shown in Ta-
ble 4, substantial stage-level savings may yield
limited end-to-end improvements. Token selection,
memory access, and kernel inefficiency can offset
computational gains (Li et al., 2026c; Ma et al.,
2026c). Future research should coordinate token
compression, KV management, and low-precision
execution, optimizing measured latency and mem-
ory alongside task quality rather than compression
ratios alone.
Taken together, these directions call for a shift
from isolated compression strategies to coordi-
nated, evidence-aware inference.
The ultimate
goal is not merely to reduce visual tokens, but to
preserve reliable video reasoning while delivering
measurable end-to-end efficiency gains.
6
Conclusion
This survey highlights a central insight: training-
free VLM acceleration is not merely about re-
ducing visual tokens, but about balancing com-
putational savings with the preservation of task-
relevant spatiotemporal evidence. Across the in-
ference pipeline, intervention position determines
both which computations can be avoided and what
information remains available for downstream rea-
soning. Earlier compression offers broader com-
putational savings but risks irreversible evidence
loss, whereas later intervention exploits richer rep-
resentations while inheriting upstream costs. Our
analysis further reveals that reduced token counts
and local latency improvements do not necessarily
translate into end-to-end efficiency. These find-
ings point toward a shift from isolated compression
strategies to evidence-aware, cross-stage optimiza-
tion, where computation and memory are allocated
according to task demands and actual system bottle-
necks. Ultimately, progress should be measured by
reliable video understanding and realized inference
efficiency.

Limitations
This survey focuses on publicly available training-
free VLM acceleration methods and may overlook
recent or proprietary advances. Our controlled ex-
periments use a single backbone, a fixed frame
budget, and one GPU platform, limiting their gen-
eralizability. Other comparisons rely on published
results with potentially different experimental pro-
tocols. Moreover, PTQ evidence is largely image-
based, and available system measurements lack
complete latency breakdowns. Therefore, our find-
ings should be interpreted within their respective
evaluation settings rather than as universal perfor-
mance rankings.
Ethical Considerations
This survey synthesizes existing literature and in-
volves no human participants. Training-free ac-
celeration may reduce computational costs and
improve accessibility to video-LLMs. However,
lossy compression may discard critical evidence
and increase unreliable predictions. We encourage
the community to adopt robust evaluation proto-
cols that assess these risks alongside accuracy and
latency. Our experiments use publicly available
models and benchmarks, subject to their respective
licenses and usage terms. We also document evalu-
ation settings and comparison protocols to support
reproducibility.

A
Evaluation Metrics and Evidence
Protocol
A.1
General Evaluation Metrics
We evaluate training-free VLM acceleration along
three dimensions: task quality, compression, and
realized inference cost. Task quality is assessed
using benchmark accuracy or task-specific scores,
while compression is characterized by visual-token
or KV-cache retention and numerical precision. In-
ference efficiency is measured using Time to First
Token (TTFT), Time per Output Token (TPOT),
End-to-End latency (E2E), and Peak Memory. We
further employ the baseline-normalized Quality–
Latency Balance (QLB) to assess the trade-off be-
tween task quality and latency under matched set-
tings. Table 5 summarizes the metrics and their
definitions.
Here, tarr, tfirst, and tend denote request arrival,
first-token generation, and response completion
times, respectively, and No is the number of gener-
ated tokens (No > 1 for TPOT). For QLB, A and
A0 denote the task-specific quality scores of the
accelerated method and its matched baseline, while
Lx and L0,x denote their corresponding latencies.
The matched baseline has a QLB of 100. Higher
values indicate a more favorable relative quality–
latency balance, but do not imply comparability
across different evaluation settings.
A.2
Video Understanding Benchmarks
Table 6 summarizes representative benchmarks for
video understanding, covering temporal reasoning,
egocentric understanding, long-context comprehen-
sion, motion perception, and detailed video descrip-
tion.
Our controlled evaluation uses Video-MME,
EgoSchema, LongVideoBench, and MVBench (Fu
et al., 2025a; Mangalam et al., 2023; Wu et al.,
2024; Li et al., 2024b). The literature-based de-
coding comparison additionally includes Video De-
tailed Captioning (VideoDC/VDC), which evalu-
ates generated descriptions using VDCscore (Chai
et al., 2025). Other benchmarks provide comple-
mentary coverage of temporal perception, long-
form reasoning, and domain-specific understanding
(Yu et al., 2019; Xiao et al., 2021; Patraucean et al.,
2023; Liu et al., 2024; Fang et al., 2024; Song et al.,
2024; Zhou et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025b; Hong
et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2026a). These benchmarks
differ in task format, video duration, and evaluation
protocol; results should therefore be compared only
under matched settings.
A.3
Host VLMs and Language Backbones
The choice of host VLM plays an important role
in evaluating training-free inference acceleration.
Differences in visual tokenization, context capacity,
and model architecture affect both baseline perfor-
mance and computational cost. Consequently, sim-
ilar retention budgets may correspond to different
inference workloads across host models.
Existing
studies
employ
diverse
VLM
backbones,
including Video-LLaVA, LLaVA-
OneVision, and the Qwen-VL family (Lin et al.,
2024a; Wang et al., 2024b; Bai et al., 2025b,a).
Table 7 summarizes representative methods,
their host models, compression budgets, and
reported performance across video understanding
benchmarks. The table preserves a broad view of
the literature, including stage-specific components,
multi-stage methods, and architecture-dependent
approaches (Liu et al., 2025b; Wang et al., 2026a;
Liu et al., 2025c; Lin et al., 2026; Wang et al.,
2026c). Since these results originate from different
models, budgets, and evaluation protocols, they
serve as reported evidence rather than a direct
cross-method ranking.

C
Workload Shifts: Streaming and
Multi-Turn Inference
Most training-free VLM acceleration studies are
evaluated under offline, single-turn VideoQA set-
tings, where the complete video is available be-
fore answering a single query. However, streaming
and multi-turn workloads introduce additional con-
straints on inference efficiency. Streaming requires
causal processing of incoming frames and bounded
historical memory, whereas multi-turn interaction
creates opportunities for computation reuse while
increasing the cost of maintaining dialogue context.
These settings change how visual evidence is ac-
cessed, retained, and reused, making conventional
accuracy and single-query latency insufficient to
characterize deployment efficiency. We examine
these two workload shifts and their implications for
training-free acceleration.
C.1
Offline-to-Streaming Workload Shift
Offline VideoQA assumes access to the complete
video before answering a query, whereas stream-
ing VLMs must process visual evidence causally
as frames arrive. This shift introduces constraints
beyond task quality and query latency: historical
memory must remain bounded, and frame process-
ing must keep pace with the incoming stream (Tang
et al., 2026). A necessary condition for avoiding
sustained processing backlogs is

ing acceleration should be assessed through causal
task performance, memory growth, processing
throughput, and response latency, rather than of-
fline accuracy alone.
C.2

inference becomes a stateful interaction rather than

computed visual features may be reused, but di-
alogue history introduces additional context and
memory costs. Consequently, training-free multi-
turn acceleration must balance computation reuse
with the preservation of relevant visual and textual
evidence.

D
Speculative Decoding for VLM
Acceleration
Speculative decoding (SD) accelerates autoregres-
sive generation by allowing a draft model to pro-
pose multiple tokens for parallel verification by a
target model. For VLMs, however, drafting can be
expensive because both models must process long
visual contexts. Effective acceleration therefore
depends on draft cost, draft–target alignment, ver-
ification overhead, and accepted progress (Zhang
et al., 2025e; Ji et al., 2025).
D.1
Draft–Verify Efficiency
Training-free VLM SD follows several comple-
mentary directions. Sparse-to-Dense (StD) com-
bines sparse Top-K drafting with dense verifica-
tion, reporting up to 1.94× wall-clock speedup
without changing the target distribution (Zhang
et al., 2025e). SpecVLM instead reduces draft-
side visual tokens through verifier-guided pruning
while retaining full-context target verification. It
removes up to 90% of draft visual tokens and re-
ports up to 2.68× and 2.11× decoding speedup
on LLaVA-OneVision-72B and Qwen2.5-VL-32B,
respectively (Ji et al., 2025).
ParallelVLM further overlaps drafting and veri-
fication, using Unbiased Verifier-Guided Pruning
to improve draft–target alignment. It reports av-
erage speedups of 3.36× and 2.42× on the same
target models (Kong et al., 2026). LVSpec takes
a different approach by relaxing verification for
less visually relevant tokens while strictly checking
visual-semantic anchors. Its position-shift-tolerant
mechanism increases accepted progress without
guaranteeing exact output-distribution preservation
(Ji et al., 2026).
These methods expose three acceleration levers:
reducing draft computation, overlapping execution,
and increasing accepted progress. Their combined
effect must be assessed through measured decoding
efficiency rather than acceptance alone.
