Abstract 

Visual token pruning substantially reduces the inference cost of multimodal large language models (MLLMs), yet existing methods are mostly training-free and plug-and-play. They treat the resulting performance drop as an inevitable cost of compression and pursue increasingly complex pruning rules. We rethink this view through two questions: how much of the loss can post-training rescue, and, building on this recovery, how can joint compression push pruning to its limit? We curate a 282K-sample post-training mixture covering general VQA, reasoning, OCR, and grounding, and post-train 36 configurations that drop or merge tokens under a unified protocol. Post-training rescues most of the loss, even for OCR and grounding, and its adaptation gain in closing the gap between the pruned and the dense model grows as the budget tightens. For joint compression, we find that the vision encoder, not the LLM, dominates prefill time in native-resolution MLLMs, so where tokens are removed matters more than how many: keeping 40% of tokens at the encoder runs faster than keeping 10% at the projector. Our best configuration prunes the encoder lightly and the projector aggressively, leaves the LLM intact, and keeps general understanding and reasoning at the dense level. It accelerates the LLM stage by 3 .49 × but the end-to-end time-to-first-token by only 1 .54 ×, showing that LLM-stage speedups overstate end-to-end gains. We hope these findings offer insights for future research on joint visual token compression. Our data, protocol, and code will be released. 

1 Introduction 

Visual tokens, which often far outnumber text tokens, account for much of the inference cost of multimodal large language models (MLLMs), making visual token compression a primary route to acceleration [17, 27, 40, 45, 51]. Tokens can be reduced at three stages of the pipeline [46]: inside the vision encoder [3, 16], at the projector [1, 50, 59], or within the LLM [4, 8, 49], by either dropping or merging them, mostly as training-free modules [10, 37, 57]. Rethinking this practice raises two questions. First, how much of the accuracy lost to pruning can be recovered? As shown in Fig. 1, training-free pruning largely preserves coarse-grained understanding but sharply degrades OCR and grounding, consistent with prior observations [7, 9, 14]. Yet lightweight post-training under the same pruning configuration pulls these nearly collapsed capabilities back to a large extent. Compression-aware post-training (CAPT) may thus mitigate the mismatch between the pruned input and a model trained on dense tokens [7, 38, 50]. Second, how should the three stages be compressed together? A single stage offers limited room for compression, and combining stages can remove more tokens, as recent pipelines that chain several stages have begun to show [12, 28, 61]. Joint compression, however, couples the stages, and losses across stages accumulate. Early pruning shortens later sequences, but differing stage costs mean fewer

CAPT improves every configuration and recov-ers most of the performance lost to pruning. At an average keep ratio of 20%, it brings general understanding and reasoning close to the orig-inal model in nearly every configuration, and restores much of the OCR and grounding abil-ity that training-free pruning nearly destroys. To trace where this recovery comes from, we take LLM-level pruning as a case study and con-trast four training and evaluation states. They disentangle the pruning gap into a repairable adaptation mismatch and a residual of discarded evidence, and the post-training gain into a data gain and an adaptation gain. As the budget tightens, the residual grows, while the recovery relies increasingly on adapting to pruning. Visu-alizations further show that this adaptation acts on both sides of the pruning point. Upstream, the model learns features and attention that let the fixed rules select more relevant tokens. Downstream, the LLM learns to make better use of the few tokens that remain. CAPT also opens room for joint compression. With CAPT, joint configurations that keep only about 5% to 7% of the visual tokens still preserve the original model’s general understanding and reasoning, whereas recent training-free methods fall short of this level even at a 25% budget. Exploring such configurations further leads to a more instructive finding: fewer tokens do not mean a faster model, and the configuration that keeps the fewest tokens is not the fastest. The question thus shifts from how many tokens to remove to where to remove them, and the answer depends on where the time goes. In native-resolution MLLMs such as Qwen2.5-VL, the encoder processes several times as many patches as the visual tokens the LLM receives after the projector’s compression, and such encoders typically run less efficiently than their FLOP count suggests. 

Most of the prefill time therefore goes to the vision encoder rather than the 7B LLM : 56 .5% in our setting, in line with the 40% to over 80% reported for similar models [5, 26, 42, 60]. As a result, 

speedups measured inside the LLM overstate end-to-end gains . Prior work often reports speedups of 3 × or more at little accuracy cost, measured on the LLM stage or on LLaVA-style models whose light encoders add little latency [4, 44, 50, 58]. Without training, however, upstream pruning sacrifices fine-grained capabilities. CAPT recovers them, making these extreme speedups feasible. 2 Related Work 

Visual token compression reduces the redundant visual sequence in MLLMs and has mainly developed along two directions: dropping and merging. Token dropping removes low-utility visual tokens by estimating token importance from attention patterns [4, 49, 52] or feature similarity [1, 55]. FastV [4], for example, prunes half of the visual tokens after the second LLM layer using the attention from the last text token to visual tokens. Token merging takes a softer route by fusing redundant token representations. AIM [59] directly merges similar token pairs, FiCoCo [12] recycles information by merging less important tokens into important retained tokens, 

3Rescuing Your Pruning 

and PACT [8] clusters similar tokens into compact representatives. Unlike most training-free or plug-and-play studies, we revisit dropping and merging from a post-training perspective, analyzing how these two mechanisms behave after adaptation. Efficient MLLM inference has motivated visual token compression beyond a single module, with recent methods combining reduction across multiple pipeline stages. FiCoCo [12] adopts an encoder–LLM pipeline with attention-guided pruning and information recycling, while Focus-LLaVA [61] combines projector-level multi-scale sampling with LLM-level text-to-vision pruning. More recent methods extend compression to the encoder–projector–LLM pipeline: METEOR [28] integrates encoder pruning, cross-encoder redundancy removal, and instance-adaptive LLM pruning, and SAINT [16] applies similarity-aware pruning across stages. However, these methods mainly propose fixed multi-stage pipelines, leaving the interaction among stages and the allocation of compression budgets under the same final token budget insufficiently understood. We therefore systematically investigate joint encoder–projector–LLM compression and analyze how stage-wise budgets affect the accuracy–efficiency trade-off after adaptation. 

3 Post-Training and Evaluation 

Post-Training Data. Although some training-free pruning methods also report results with post-training, their data recipes always vary. We therefore first construct a unified, lightweight post-training dataset. Building upon the 133K LLaVA-Icons corpus [47], a compact subset of LLaVA-665K [25], we reorganize the originally multi-turn conversations into single-turn samples anchored by individual images. We then add small random portions of ChartQA [33], DVQA [18], AI2D [20], DocVQA [34], LLaVAR [56], and TQA [21], together with visual grounding data from LLaVA-665K, so that the mixture of about 282K samples covers general VQA, reasoning, OCR, and grounding. For visual grounding data used with Qwen-series models, we convert bounding boxes to normalized coordinates w.r.t. the original image resolution. Appendix A.1 details the dataset composition, Appendix A.2 reports train–test overlap statistics, and Appendix A.3 presents data ablations. 

Training Protocol. Training-free pruning rules are designed for a frozen model, so we adapt them to CAPT under one uniform protocol. Pruning is a discrete operation defined by fixed arithmetic rather than learnable parameters, so we keep every rule exactly as specified and cut the gradient at the selection: what adapts is the model upstream and downstream of the pruning point, never the criterion itself. Pruning also reshapes the batch, since the number of surviving tokens varies across samples. We re-pack each sequence after every reduction, pad to the longest one in the batch, and extend the attention mask and labels so that padded positions enter neither attention nor the loss. For positions, retained tokens keep their pre-pruning indices and rotary embeddings are recomputed from those indices rather than the compacted ones, preserving the spatial layout of the dense model. We experiment on Qwen2.5-VL-7B [36] and LLaVA-OneVision-1.5-8B (LLaVA-OV-1.5-8B) [2]. LLM-level methods unfreeze only the language model. All other methods are trained with every module unfrozen. One setting deserves note: Qwen2.5-VL encodes images at dynamic resolution, so max_pixels strongly affects fine-grained detail and training cost, and we fix it to 451 ,584 as a balance between the two. The remaining configuration is shared across runs: one epoch over the corpus, a learning rate of 1 × 10 −5, bfloat16 precision, a maximum sequence length of 8192, and DeepSpeed ZeRO-2. Each run takes roughly four hours on 8 NVIDIA H20 80 GB GPUs. 

Evaluation Protocol and Metrics. We evaluate four capability groups with lmms-eval [54] under identical settings for all models. General ability is measured on GQA [15], VQA-v2 [11], and OK-VQA [32], and reasoning on ScienceQA [30], AI2D [20], and POPE [23], the last of which

We report ANLS for DocVQA and Acc@0.5 for grounding, where RefCOCO and RefCOCO+ are averaged over testA and testB and RefCOCOg uses its test split. All other benchmarks report accuracy. 4.2 Where Does the Recovery Come From? 

The previous results show that post-training can recover a large fraction of the performance lost to visual-token pruning. Yet aggressively pruned models often remain below the dense reference even after training. This suggests a natural decomposition of the pruning-induced performance gap into two parts: a recoverable component that can be restored through post-training, and a residual component that mainly reflects the information loss inevitably introduced by pruning. We write ∆gap = Grecoverable + Rres . (1) The recoverable component requires further attribution. Our post-training uses a small and diverse dataset to adapt the upstream and downstream layers of the pruning operation to the compressed token distribution, thereby mitigating pruning-induced mismatches such as the attention gap. However, introducing post-training data inevitably brings an additional source of performance gain that is independent of pruning adaptation. CAPT rescues representation mismatch, but not discarded evidence. Dropping removes tokens outright, along with the information they carry. Merging instead aggregates this information into the retained tokens, at the cost of perturbing the original representations. Post-training adapts to this perturbation and lets the extra information kept by merging take effect. At 10% keep, PACT and PDrop gain comparably from adaptation, yet PACT leaves a residual that is one fifth to one third smaller and outperforms PDrop by 12 .5 and 9 .6 points in ATR on the two backbones. Post-training thus determines how well the model exploits the compressed representation, while the information preserved by pruning sets the ceiling of recovery. To qualitatively examine how post-training changes the pruned representation, we visualize the retained tokens and text-to-vision attention for all six methods at 20% keep on a RefCOCO grounding example in Fig. 4. Upstream of the pruning point, post-training reshapes the feature and attention distributions that the pruning rules operate on, so that the rules select tokens more effectively and more retained visual tokens fall inside the ground-truth region. Downstream, the LLM assigns stronger relative attention to the retained tokens within the target region, indicating that it learns to exploit the remaining visual evidence more effectively. Together, these two effects constitute the adaptation gain quantified above.

Once tokens are aggressively pruned before the LLM, further LLM-level pruning leaves latency nearly unchanged or even slightly higher, because the LLM already operates close to a length-independent latency floor, as analyzed in Appendix C.2. Joint pruning across levels is therefore worthwhile for real acceleration, and CAPT is what rescues the quality of such aggressive joint configurations. 

6 Conclusion 

This paper revisits visual token compression in multimodal large language models from the perspective of post-training. We point out that the performance degradation after pruning stems not only from unrecoverable information loss, but also from the adaptation gap between compressed visual tokens and the original dense-token distribution. To this end, we establish a unified compression-aware post-training framework and systematically analyze the recovery behavior of different compression mechanisms at the encoder, projector, and LLM levels. Our framework achieves significant and comprehensive performance recovery across diverse pruning settings, suggesting that a substantial portion of the degradation caused by training-free pruning is adaptable. Furthermore, we explore aggressive joint pruning across levels, which, with compression-aware post-training, attains the highest speedup among nearly lossless configurations.

D Details of Pruning Methods 

We study one dropping and one merging method at each pruning level: SAINT and ToMe at the vision encoder, DivPrune and AIM at the projector, and PDrop and PACT inside the LLM. 

SAINT [16]. SAINT can be applied in a training-free manner to prune visual tokens by estimating token redundancy from key-embedding similarity graphs and using layer-adaptive pruning rates across the multimodal pipeline. 

ToMe [3]. ToMe is a training-free token merging method that accelerates Vision Transformers by iteratively matching similar tokens and merging them in pairs inside transformer blocks, thereby reducing token redundancy without additional training. 

DivPrune [1]. DivPrune reduces visual tokens by formulating pruning as a Max-Min Diversity Problem, retaining a diverse subset of tokens while removing visually redundant or less informative ones. 

AIM [59]. AIM improves multimodal inference efficiency by first merging similar visual tokens before the LLM and then progressively pruning less important visual tokens inside LLM layers. 

PyramidDrop (PDrop) [49]. PyramidDrop progressively reduces visual tokens across trans-former layers with a pyramid-like schedule, removing more tokens in earlier stages while preserving more tokens in later layers. 

PACT [8]. PACT reduces visual tokens by pruning less informative tokens and clustering nearby redundant tokens into representative embeddings. 

E Details of Datasets and Benchmarks 

COCO [24]. COCO is a large-scale dataset of natural images with rich object, segmentation, and caption annotations, widely used for image captioning and general visual understanding. 

VG [22]. Visual Genome (VG) provides dense annotations of objects, attributes, and relation-ships grounded in image regions, supporting fine-grained scene understanding and region-level visual grounding. 

OCRVQA [35]. OCR-VQA consists of book cover images paired with questions about titles, authors, and other textual content, requiring joint OCR and visual question answering. 

ChartQA [33]. ChartQA evaluates question answering over chart images, requiring both visual perception of chart elements and logical or arithmetic reasoning over the underlying data. 

> 20 Rescuing Your Pruning

DVQA [18]. DVQA evaluates understanding of bar charts through questions about structure, data retrieval, and reasoning, with answers often containing chart-specific vocabulary. 

LLaVAR [56]. LLaVAR augments visual instruction tuning with text-rich images, enhancing the ability of multimodal models to read and reason over textual content embedded in images. 

TQA [21]. TQA (Textbook Question Answering) evaluates multimodal reasoning over middle-school science textbooks, combining text passages, diagrams, and questions that require contextual understanding. 

ShareGPT [6]. ShareGPT is a collection of user-shared conversations with ChatGPT, com-monly used as a language-only instruction-following corpus to preserve linguistic capability during multimodal fine-tuning. 

GQA [15]. GQA evaluates compositional visual reasoning based on scene-graph annotations from Visual Genome. 

VQA-v2 [11]. VQA-v2 is a balanced open-ended visual question answering benchmark designed to reduce language priors and encourage visual grounding. 

OK-VQA [32]. OK-VQA evaluates knowledge-based visual question answering, where answer-ing often requires external or commonsense knowledge beyond the image content. 

ScienceQA [30]. ScienceQA evaluates multimodal scientific reasoning over questions from natural, language, and social sciences, where the image-based subset requires models to reason over visual inputs. 

AI2D [20]. AI2D evaluates diagram understanding, requiring models to interpret labels, symbols, visual structures, and spatial relations. 

POPE [23]. POPE is a diagnostic benchmark for object hallucination, using binary questions to test whether models falsely report nonexistent objects. 

TextVQA [39]. TextVQA evaluates scene-text understanding by requiring models to recognize and reason over text in natural images. 

DocVQA [34]. DocVQA evaluates document image question answering, emphasizing OCR, layout understanding, and information localization. 

OCRBench [29]. OCRBench evaluates OCR-related capabilities of MLLMs across text recog-nition, document understanding, information extraction, and related tasks. 

RefCOCO [19, 53]. RefCOCO evaluates referring expression comprehension by requiring models to localize objects described by natural-language expressions.

RefCOCO+ [19, 53]. RefCOCO+ emphasizes appearance-based referring expressions with fewer absolute location cues, making it more dependent on fine-grained object attributes. 

RefCOCOg [31, 53]. RefCOCOg contains longer and more descriptive referring expressions, testing fine-grained and context-rich visual grounding.

F.1 Encoder-Level 

This section provides the full results of encoder-level pruning across different models, pruning ratios, and compression mechanisms. Overall, SAINT and ToMe achieve comprehensive im-provements across metrics after post-training, with especially clear recovery on fine-grained abilities such as OCR and grounding. This section reports the complete experimental results of projector-level pruning. DivPrune and AIM show stable and comprehensive improvements after post-training. Under 40% and 20% keep ratios, their overall performance approaches or even surpasses the Dense-Free baseline. These results indicate that projector-level pruning is a stable single-level pruning choice and provides a strong basis for multi-level combinations.

This section presents the full results of LLM-level pruning. The results show that both PDrop and PACT obtain overall improvements on general, reasoning, OCR, and grounding metrics after post-training, with PACT usually showing more stable performance. This demonstrates that LLM-side pruning can also be effectively adapted through post-training, although fine-grained abilities remain more vulnerable under extreme compression.
