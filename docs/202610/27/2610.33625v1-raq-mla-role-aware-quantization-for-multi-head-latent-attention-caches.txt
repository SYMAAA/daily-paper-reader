## ABSTRACT 

Multi-Head Latent Attention (MLA) has been adopted by recent frontier large language models. Instead of per-head keys and values, MLA caches a compressed KV latent that is up-projected into keys and values at decode time, together with a narrow positional key that carries position information into the attention score. Existing KV-cache quantization methods are designed around the keys and values of standard attention and do not account for what MLA actually caches, which costs accuracy at low bit widths. We analyze the MLA cache both in activation distribution and in how quantization error propagates to the attention output, and show that the two cached tensors play different roles: the latent mainly supplies the values, whereas the positional key dominates the attention score. We therefore propose RAQ-MLA (Role-Aware Quantization for Multi-Head Latent Attention Caches), which fits an invertible transform to each cached tensor against the error that tensor delivers to the attention output: attention-weighted value error for the latent and score perturbation for the positional key. Without training, RAQ-MLA quantizes the full MLA cache to about 2.5 bits per element, a 6.5× increase in theoretical KV-cache capacity, while remaining nearly lossless on reasoning and coding tasks across four MLA models. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Long-horizon agents have made ultra-long-context inference a mainstream serving workload (Yao et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2024; DeepSeek-AI, 2026). Serving it requires more than efficient long-sequence computation: the key-value (KV) cache must be held for the entire lifetime of a request and grows linearly with both context length and concurrency. At runtime, the cache competes with batch size for HBM capacity (Kwon et al., 2023); when persisted for prefix reuse, it consumes host memory, SSD capacity, and interconnect bandwidth (Qin et al., 2026; Zheng et al., 2024). Both pressures cap serving throughput and raise deployment cost, and both grow as agents run longer and at higher concurrency. To reduce this footprint, Multi-Head Latent Attention (MLA) (DeepSeek-AI, 2024) compresses each token’s key and value into a single low-dimensional latent cKV  

> t

, which is cached and up-projected into keys and values at decode time, alongside a narrow decoupled positional key kR 

> t

. MLA has been adopted by recent frontier models, increasingly together with sparse attention (DeepSeek-AI, 2025; GLM, 2026). Sparse attention, however, only selects which cached tokens each query attends to; the full cache is still retained. MLA thus shrinks the cache per token, but neither it nor sparse attention changes its linear growth with context length: at the one-million-token context length of recent models, a single sequence still occupies tens of GiB of cache. Compressing what MLA actually stores therefore remains necessary. KV-cache quantization is the other line of work that reduces cache size, lowering the average bits per stored element without any training (Hooper et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2026; Su et al., 2025). However, most prior methods (Liu et al., 2024; Hooper et al., 2024) design their 1arXiv:2610.33625v1 [cs.AI] 24 Oct 2026 Preprint granularity, precision, and transforms around the keys and values of standard attention. MLA caches neither: it stores the compressed latent cKV  

> t

and the decoupled positional key kR 

> t

, and recovers the content key and value from the latent only at use time. Existing MLA cache quantizers (Zhang et al., 2026; Muller et al., 2026) keep the latent at four or more bits and leave kR in full precision. In this paper, we aim to quantize the full MLA cache, including the positional key, to extremely low bit widths (about 2.5 bits per cached element on average). We analyze the two cached tensors both in distribution and in how their quantization error propagates through the MLA computation. Fixed-channel outliers appear in the decoupled positional key but not in the compressed latent (Figure 2), and the positional key carries 92 .5% of the attention-score attribution, most of it in a few outlier channels. As a result, the two tensors reach the output through different routes. At 4 bits, the latent’s 12 .3% error in the cache falls to 1.7% at the attention output through the content key but remains 8.3% through the value, whereas the positional key’s error rises from 9.1% to 14 .7% . The latent therefore mainly plays the value role in MLA, while the tensor that sets the score is the one existing MLA methods exempt from quantization. 1× 2× 3× 4× 5× 6×

## 3 MOTIVATION 

We analyze the MLA cache from a quantization perspective to decide, for each cached tensor, how it should be quantized and what its quantized bits must preserve. The analysis proceeds in three steps: the distribution of each tensor, how its quantization error propagates to the attention output, and its contribution to the attention score, which explains that propagation. Unless stated otherwise, all analyses use GLM-4.7-Flash on calibration prompts. 

How are the two cached tensors distributed? As shown in Figure 2, channel structure separates the two cached tensors. We compare cKV and kR in GLM-4.7-Flash and GLM-5.2 with the Key and Value caches of standard (non-MLA) attention in Llama-3.1-8B (Grattafiori et al., 2024) and Qwen3-8B (Yang et al., 2025), and each aligns with one of the two references. kR carries the channel-aligned outliers that conventional Key caches exhibit. cKV instead resembles a Value cache, spreading its magnitude broadly, with no fixed channel standing out. The first design decision in RAQ-MLA follows: the positional key is quantized per channel, which keeps its outlier channels out of a shared scale, and the latent per token, which confines the error to the token being quantized (Liu et al., 2024). This granularity reduces the quantization error of each cached tensor, but it does not target the errors that matter at the attention output. 

How does quantization error reach the output? We quantize the latent per token and the posi-tional key per channel, both at 4 bits, and measure the relative error stage by stage (Figure 3b). The latent’s two readouts start out alike: right after up-projection, kC and vC carry comparable relative error. Only the content key enters the attention weights, and there its error has shrunk to 1.9% , an order of magnitude below the positional key’s 17 .5% . At the attention output, the latent’s 12 .3% 

error in the cache has fallen to 1.7% through the content key but remains 8.3% through the value, whereas the positional key’s error has risen from 9.1% to 14 .7% .

Why do the two routes differ? The channel structure of Figure 2 accounts for this split. The outlier channels of kR dominate the attention score: pooled over all 47 layers, the positional term carries 92 .5% of the logit attribution (Appendix D) against 7.5% for the content term, and the outlier channels of kR, a tenth of its 64 , carry 63 .8% on their own (Figure 3a). Because the content term contributes so little to the logit, perturbing it barely moves the attention weights; the positional term sets them, so its error is amplified into the attention weights and carried on to the output. Within the latent, then, the value readout carries most of its error through to the output, while the attention score is dominated by kR.

## A RELATED WORK 

KV-cache quantization. Post-training quantization of weights and activations is well established (Frantar et al., 2023; Lin et al., 2024; Xiao et al., 2023). As context grows, the KV cache rather than the weights dominates decoding memory, and the same tools have been carried over to the cache. Existing KV-cache quantizers choose their granularity from the statistics of the stored tensor: keys have persistent channel-wise outliers and values do not, so KIVI (Liu et al., 2024) quantizes keys per channel and values per token, keeping a short window of recent tokens in full precision. KVQuant (Hooper et al., 2024) quantizes keys per channel before RoPE, fits non-uniform datatypes, and stores a small fraction of outlier values in a sparse full-precision matrix. At two bits, SKVQ (Duanmu et al., 2024) reorders channels and clips dynamically, with a full-precision sliding window of recent tokens. Finer groups are not free, since each group stores its own scale and zero point, so we report effective bits per cached element throughout. All of these methods choose a tensor’s quantization policy from that tensor’s own statistics, not from how attention reads it. 

Transforms for quantization. Rotating the cache before rounding is the most common remedy for outliers at low bit widths. QuaRot (Ashkboos et al., 2024) applies Hadamard rotations that leave the network output unchanged and quantizes weights, activations, and the KV cache to four bits; SpinQuant (Liu et al., 2025b) learns these rotations instead. RotateKV (Su et al., 2025) rotates keys before RoPE with a grouped-head Hadamard transform and outlier-aware channel reordering. OS-CAR (Zhou et al., 2026) derives fixed rotations and clipping thresholds offline from attention-aware covariance statistics, combining their eigenbases with a Hadamard transform. OScaR (Su et al., 2026), a different method despite the similar name, targets token-norm imbalance with canalized rotation and omni-token scaling. KVarN (Muller et al., 2026) applies a Hadamard rotation followed by variance normalization along both axes of the key and value matrices, without calibration. Be-yond orthogonal transforms, AffineQuant (Ma et al., 2024) and FlatQuant (Sun et al., 2025) learn general invertible affine transforms for low-bit LLM quantization by gradient descent on layer or block outputs. 

MLA cache quantization. Few methods target the MLA cache directly. SnapMLA (Zhang et al., 2026) quantizes the latent to FP8 per token and keeps the positional key in BF16, after observing that FP8 raises the error of the RoPE part by an order of magnitude. The FP8 cache format of FlashMLA (Li & Liu, 2025) likewise stores the latent in FP8 and the positional key in BF16. The official vLLM 13 Preprint implementation of KVarN (Muller et al., 2026) supports MLA models by quantizing only the latent, to INT4. All of them leave kR in full precision. 

Distinction from existing methods. RAQ-MLA is a low-bit post-training quantization method designed for the MLA cache. The general KV-cache quantizers and transforms above are designed around the keys and values of standard attention, and the MLA cache quantizers keep the latent in FP8 or INT4 and leave kR in BF16; even with a 2-bit latent, a BF16 positional key keeps the cache at 3.78 effective bits per element or more. RAQ-MLA quantizes the full MLA cache, including the positional key, to about 2.5 bits per cached element. It treats the two cached tensors according to their roles in attention: the latent is quantized per token with a value-aware transform, and the positional key per channel with a score-aware transform. Hadamard is fixed, and KVarN and OSCAR derive their transforms from data statistics before any rounding takes place; RAQ-MLA instead fits general invertible transforms to the error that rounding leaves at the attention output. AffineQuant and FlatQuant also learn invertible transforms, but for weights and activations and by backpropagating through the network, whereas RAQ-MLA is fitted by alternating minimization without any gradient through the model.

Latent. The latent is quantized per token in its learned basis. Before grouping, each token’s dc =512 channels are clipped: with τt the 94 th percentile of the token’s channel magnitudes, every channel is clamped to [−τt, τ t], which affects the 30 largest of the 512 channels. At two bits a group has only four levels, so a few large channels would otherwise stretch its range and coarsen the step for all others; clipping trades their error for a finer step on the rest. The clipped row is then split into four groups of 128 contiguous channels, each quantized with its own scale and minimum. 

Positional key. The positional key is quantized after RoPE and AR, per channel within each cache page: the P values of one channel in one page share a scale and a minimum. This grouping follows the fixed-channel outliers of kR (Section 3): a large channel receives its own range rather than stretching the range of the other channels in the same token. No clipping is applied. 

Cache pages and the BF16 tail. Following KVarN (Muller et al., 2026), a page is quantized when it is sealed. New tokens are written to the open page in BF16, and the whole page is quantized once its last slot is written; per-channel quantization of kR requires this anyway, because its scales are computed over the tokens of a page. The page size follows the attention backend: P = 128 tokens for GLM-4.7-Flash, Moonlight, and DS-V2-Lite, and P = 64 for GLM-5.2, whose sparse-attention backend uses 64 -token pages. The most recent tokens therefore stay in BF16, but on a sawtooth schedule rather than as a sliding window: the BF16 region grows to a full page and then collapses. All quantized methods, including the baselines, use the same page schedule.
