Abstract 

Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) incur substantial inference costs when processing long visual-textual sequences. While existing operation compression methods exploit modality-level redundancy, they largely treat computation within attention heads and shared feed-forward network (FFN) channels as unified units, leaving finer-grained redundancy underexplored. We find that redundancy varies both across modality-interaction paths within the same attention head and across visual and textual executions of the same FFN channel. Based on these findings, we propose Modality-aware Width-wise Operation Pruning (MWOP) , which independently prunes visual-to-visual (V2V), text-to-visual (T2V), and text-to-text (T2T) attention paths within each layer, and separately selects FFN channels for visual and textual inputs. A first-order Taylor criterion guides the pruning process, with FFN importance re-evaluated after attention pruning and LoRA-based recovery training. To translate the resulting fine-grained sparsity into practical acceleration, we further develop path-sparse Triton attention kernels and compact visual-side FFN execution. MWOP preserves the token sequence while reducing attention and FFN computation, making it complementary to token compression and enabling simultaneous reduction of sequence length and per-token computation. On LLaVA-OneVision-7B, MWOP alone achieves a 1 .6× prefill speedup with 99.7% average performance retention across 12 benchmarks. Combined with two representative token compression methods, it further increases their prefill speedups from 2 .0×

and 1 .9× to 2 .9× and 2 .7×, respectively. Results on Qwen2.5-VL-7B further demonstrate its applicability across architectures. The code is available at https://github.com/EIT-NLP/MWOP. 

1 Introduction 

Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) process hundreds or thousands of visual tokens together with textual instructions through a shared Transformer decoder [2, 22, 25]. The resulting long sequences incur substantial inference overhead, which can be reduced in two complementary ways: reducing the number of tokens being processed or reducing the computation performed on each token . The former, known as token compression , prunes or merges redundant visual tokens [41, 53], and has been extensively studied with diverse token importance criteria and pruning strategies [9, 43, 46, 47]. The latter corresponds to operation pruning , which reduces the computation applied to the retained tokens by skipping or sparsifying attention and FFN operations [23, 27, 49, 54]. Compared with token compression, operation pruning has received considerably less attention in MLLMs. Existing operation pruning methods are still relatively coarse-grained . These methods demonstrate substantial redundancy in MLLM computation, but largely treat the computation within each attention layer or FFN as a unified unit. Such a treatment overlooks the fact that different computations within and across layers can play different roles and exhibit different levels of redundancy. In fact, the computation in an MLLM layer is inherently heterogeneous. Under causal attention with visual tokens preceding textual tokens, each attention head contains three modality-interaction paths: visual-to-visual ( V2V ), text-to-vision ( T2V ), and text-to-text ( T2T ). We find that these paths exhibit distinct redundancy and pruning sensitivity: V2V spans a broader range of layers and heads and is more important for grounding, whereas T2V is concentrated in fewer heads and tolerates more pruning, while T2T requires more conservative compression (Section 4). A head can also be redundant along one path while remaining important along another. Similarly, despite shared channel parameters, FFNs show modality- and depth-dependent redundancy, with visual-side executions substantially more redundant than text-side ones and stronger redundancy in early and late layers than in middle layers (Section 5). These findings motivate fine-grained, nonuniform width-wise operation pruning , with independent V2V , T2V , and T2T pruning across attention heads and separate FFN channel selection for visual and textual inputs. Building on these insights, we propose Modality-aware Width-wise Operation Pruning (MWOP) . Comparisons of pruning granularity, importance criteria, and task sensitivity inform its staged design, which uses a common first-order Taylor criterion for both attention paths and modality-specific FFN channel executions [36]. MWOP first selects head–path pairs sequentially for V2V , T2V , and T2T , evaluating pruning budgets across task families to expose degradation hidden by aggregate scores. Crucially, attention pruning alters FFN channel importance. MWOP therefore recomputes modality-specific FFN Taylor scores under the selected attention masks before allocating channel budgets nonuniformly across layers. Candidate configurations undergo LoRA-based recovery to assess their post-training performance. Fixed offline masks, path-sparse attention, and compact visual-side FFNs translate these selections into practical acceleration without online selection overhead. To translate these pruning decisions into practical speedup, we develop a path-sparse Triton attention kernel that skips masked query–key and attention–value products while maintaining a shared online softmax over retained key intervals [13]. For FFNs, we pack selected projection slices

The optimized Qwen implementation further uses operator fusion to reduce data movement during modality splitting, activation, and output merging. These adaptations turn pruning decisions into measured latency reductions without online selection overhead. On LLaVA-OneVision-7B, MWOP prunes 40% of V2V , 60% of T2V , and 10% of T2T head–path blocks, together with 50% of visual-side FFN channel executions, while preserving all text-side FFN executions. 2.9 × over the dense model. Further evaluations support its applicability to Qwen2.5-VL-7B and transfer to multi-image and video inputs. Our contributions are threefold: (1) We show that redundancy differs across paths within the same attention head and across modalities within the same FFN channel, motivating finer-grained pruning decisions. (2) We develop MWOP, a fine-grained operation pruning framework that integrates head-wise path-level attention pruning and modality-conditioned FFN channel pruning through Taylor-guided staged selection and recovery training. (3) We demonstrate substantial standalone acceleration with near-dense performance, additional gains beyond token compression, applicability across two MLLM architectures and multiple input formats. 

2 Related Work 

Visual token compression methods can be categorized by the stage at which token reduction occurs: (1) Encoder-level methods prune or merge patch tokens during visual encoding [3, 44]. (2) Projector-level methods reduce visual token redundancy between the visual encoder and the LLM. PruMerge [37] and VisionZip [47] shorten visual token sequences by merging highly similar tokens. (3) LLM-level methods compress visual tokens within the LLM. FastV [4] and PyramidDrop [46] prune visual tokens using importance scores derived from text-to-visual attention. 

Operation compression skips or sparsifies attention and FFN operations while preserving the token sequence. Methods are broadly categorized into depth and width compression based on the targeted computational units. Depth compression skips visual token processing in selected LLM layers. ShortV [49] freezes visual token attention and FFN updates in selected layers, while ViCA [28] removes these updates throughout the LLM and retains T2V interactions only at selected layers. HiDrop [43] combines late injection and early exit with progressive token pruning. Width compression targets computation within layers. RedundancyLens [23] uses local V2V attention and dynamic FFN channel selection for visual tokens. In contrast, MWOP performs finer-grained width compression through head-wise path-level attention pruning and modality-conditioned selection of FFN channel executions, while remaining compatible with token compression.

Overview of MWOP. As illustrated in Figure 2, MWOP performs head-wise path-level attention pruning in the order V2V → T2V → T2T , guided by first-order Taylor importance. With the attention masks fixed, it recomputes FFN channel importance using the same Taylor criterion and selects channels separately for visual and textual tokens. LoRA-based post-training recovery then mitigates the performance loss from pruning. MWOP preserves the token sequence and is therefore orthogonal to token pruning. Appendices A.1 and B.1 detail Taylor importance estimation and present comparisons with other importance criteria. Algorithm 1 in the appendix summarizes staged pruning and configuration selection. Following this staged procedure, we first analyze attention-path redundancy in Section 4 and then modality-conditioned FFN redundancy under the selected attention mask in Section 5. 

4 Path-Wise Redundancy in Self-Attention 

Redundancy Is Path-Conditional and Task-Dependent. We conduct training-free anal-yses on LLaVA-OneVision-7B using representative General and Grounding benchmarks (Sec-tion 6.1). Within each task family, we rank heads separately for each path by aggregated first-order Taylor importance. Each bottom-50% set contains 392 of 784 heads, yet only 126 heads on General and 141 on Grounding are shared across all three sets (Figure 3(a)). Low importance along one path therefore does not imply low importance along others, motivating separate pruning decisions within each head. We then evaluate single-path masking on 1,000 examples from these benchmarks, keeping the other paths dense (Figure 3(b), left). General tolerates substantial V2V and T2V masking, whereas Grounding degrades sharply beyond 40% 

V2V masking but retains over 90% of baseline performance at 70% T2V masking. T2T masking causes performance collapse near 80% on General and 70% on Grounding. These differences motivate path-specific budgets evaluated across task families, with more conservative V2V 

pruning to preserve Grounding performance. Analyses for all four task families are provided in Appendix A.3.

8 Conclusion 

We identify fine-grained redundancy across modality-interaction paths within attention heads and modality-specific executions of shared FFN channels. Building on these findings, MWOP combines head-wise path-level attention pruning and modality-conditioned FFN channel pruning through Taylor-guided selection and post-training recovery. Experiments on two MLLM architectures show that MWOP reduces computation while preserving near-dense performance, including on fine-grained OCR and grounding tasks. Its composition with token compression further accelerates prefill while maintaining high performance retention, demonstrating the complementary benefits of reducing token count and computation per retained token. Future work will explore learnable gating to jointly optimize pruning decisions and compression budgets.

We present detailed pruning results and a complementary analysis of input-length effects. We first report per-benchmark attention-path and modality-conditioned FFN masking results on LLaVA-OneVision-7B (Section C.1), followed by corresponding results and comparisons with compression methods on Qwen2.5-VL-7B (Section C.2). Together, these results characterize performance retention and computational efficiency across tasks, backbones, and input profiles. For single-image evaluation, category retention averages the three benchmark score ratios to their post-trained dense references (in percent), and ATR, GRR, and OGR average category retentions over all four categories, General and Reasoning, and OCR and Grounding, respectively. 

C.1 Detailed Results on LLaVA-OneVision-7B Attention-Path Masking. Table 9 details the benchmark-level changes underlying Table 1. Increasing V2V masking from 30% to 60% lowers DocVQA from 87.1 to 83.6, while GQA and VQAv2 remain nearly unchanged. Similarly, with V2V fixed at 40%, increasing T2V masking from 50% to 80% reduces DocVQA from 85.7 to 81.9 and TextVQA from 74.7 to 72.0. D.1 Details of LMMs LLaVA-OneVision-7B connects a SigLIP vision encoder to Qwen2-7B-Instruct through a two-layer MLP projector that maps visual features into the language embedding space. Its AnyRes strategy represents high-resolution images using a global view and local crops.

Its vision encoder uses window attention, while an MLP merger combines each spatial group of 2 ×2 patch features into a single visual token aligned with the language embedding space. The Triton kernel traverses only the retained key intervals and maintains a single online softmax state across them. This implements pre-softmax path masking while skipping pruned QK and attention-value products, without materializing the attention matrix. Causal and interval-end masks handle boundary tiles. Operation pruning preserves GQA mapping, projection widths, and KV-cache dimensions. 

Modality-Conditioned FFNs. The retained rows of the gate and up projections, along with the corresponding columns of the down projection, are packed into compact visual weights. Visual tokens use these weights, while text tokens use the full FFN. Empty visual channel sets yield a zero FFN update. The packed weights require additional storage alongside the full text weights. To reduce intermediate data movement, the optimized Qwen implementation fuses normalization and modality splitting, gated activation, and output merging with residual addition. 

Latency Evaluation. We measure batch-one BF16 prefill latency on an NVIDIA A100-SXM4-40GB using model weights and synthetic embedding-table inputs. Timing inputs include system tokens. For single-image measurements, these add 18 tokens for LLaVA-OneVision-7B and 11 for Qwen2.5-VL-7B to the profiles in Section D.4. Dense and pruned models use matching input lengths within each timing setting. The dense baseline uses FlashAttention-2. Qwen2.5-VL uses its native multimodal rotary operator with identical linear temporal and spatial coordinates. We complete compilation, weight packing, kernel tuning, and graph capture before timing, with kernel configurations fixed for each measured shape. After warmup, CUDA events measure CUDA Graph replay latency. The reported latency is the median of five window means, each computed from ten replay batches. Attention and FFN latencies are obtained by summing separately measured layer-module latencies, while total prefill latency is measured independently. Speedup is the ratio of dense to pruned total prefill latency for each input setting on the same hardware.

E Details of Comparison Methods 

FastV [4]. FastV prunes visual tokens after shallow language-model layers using text-to-vision attention scores. It discards low-scoring tokens from subsequent layers, reducing sequence length and the associated attention and FFN computation. 

PyramidDrop [46]. PyramidDrop progressively prunes visual tokens at multiple depths of the language model. Instruction-guided attention determines which tokens to retain, allocating larger token budgets to shallow layers and smaller budgets to deeper layers. 

VisionZip [47]. VisionZip compresses visual representations before language-model processing by retaining dominant tokens and constructing contextual tokens. It combines attention-based selection with similarity-based aggregation to preserve salient visual content and complementary contextual information across the image. 

DivPrune [1]. DivPrune formulates visual token selection as a Max-Min Diversity Problem. It greedily maximizes the minimum pairwise distance among retained tokens, reducing redundant representations while maintaining broad coverage of diverse visual content. 

> 34 MWOP

DART [42]. DART performs duplication-aware visual token pruning using visual and textual pivot tokens. It retains tokens with low similarity to these pivots, emphasizing complementary visual information rather than relying solely on attention-based importance scores. 

VisPruner [51]. VisPruner combines visual importance and diversity for token pruning without text-guided attention. It first selects important tokens using visual attention, then supplements them with diverse tokens through similarity-based duplicate removal. 

FEATHER [8]. FEATHER combines token selection based on RoPE-free attention scores with uniform spatial sampling. Its two-stage pruning strategy preserves spatial coverage during early selection and further reduces visual tokens in later layers. 

MMTok [7]. MMTok formulates visual token selection as multimodal coverage maximization. It jointly considers text-visual relevance and visual-visual coverage, greedily selecting tokens that represent the image while preserving information relevant to the input query. 

PruMerge+ [37]. PruMerge+ combines attention-guided token selection, similarity-based merging, and spatial sampling. Selected tokens aggregate similar visual features, while additional spatially sampled tokens preserve broader image coverage beyond regions highlighted by attention scores in the initial token selection step. 

ZOO-Prune [20]. ZOO-Prune estimates visual token sensitivity at the projector stage using zeroth-order perturbations. It combines sensitivity with diversity to retain informative tokens before language-model processing, without requiring gradient backpropagation. 

ShortV [49]. ShortV identifies low-contribution layers through output divergence and freezes visual hidden states in those layers. It skips visual attention updates and FFN computation while preserving visual keys and values for text queries. 

RedundancyLens [23]. RedundancyLens combines Hollow Attention with Probe-Activated Dynamic FFN to reduce visual computation. It restricts visual self-attention to local neighbor-hoods and uses sampled visual tokens to select active FFN channels in designated layers. 

HalfV [38]. HalfV distinguishes intrinsic visual redundancy from architecture-dependent saturation. It combines token pruning with backbone-aware reductions in later visual processing, adapting compression to how visual representations evolve across the language model. 

DOP V [27]. DOP V combines VisPruner-based visual token reduction with depth-wise operation pruning across attention and FFNs. Under a computational budget, it uses output divergence to select different visual processing depths for attention and FFNs. 

YOPO [54]. YOPO reduces visual computation through head pruning, local visual attention, late-layer operation removal, and FFN channel pruning. These reductions establish a computation pattern shared across inputs rather than selected dynamically for each sample. 

> 35 MWOP

Skip-Vision [50]. Skip-Vision allows selected visual tokens to bypass FFN transformations while remaining available to attention. It combines token aggregation with learned summarization and removes skipped-token cache entries after prefilling to reduce decoding overhead. 

F Details of Benchmarks 

F.1 Single-image benchmarks GQA [14]. GQA evaluates compositional visual reasoning using scene-graph annotations from Visual Genome. It requires models to combine information about object attributes and relationships to answer questions grounded in the visual scene. 

VQA-v2 [12]. VQA-v2 is a balanced, open-ended visual question answering benchmark designed to reduce language priors. It encourages models to examine visual evidence and produce image-grounded answers rather than rely primarily on question patterns. 

OK-VQA [32]. OK-VQA evaluates knowledge-based visual question answering, where im-age content is often insufficient. Models must connect visual observations with external or commonsense knowledge to answer questions that extend beyond directly observable information. 

ScienceQA [30]. ScienceQA evaluates multimodal scientific reasoning across natural, language, and social sciences. Its image-based subset requires models to combine visual evidence with question context and relevant scientific knowledge to identify the correct answer. 

AI2D [18]. AI2D evaluates diagram understanding through questions involving labels, symbols, visual structures, and spatial relations. Models must connect these elements and interpret their relationships to reason about the information conveyed by a diagram. 

MMStar [5]. MMStar evaluates multimodal understanding with curated questions that require visual evidence. Its samples span multiple visual and reasoning capabilities and are selected to reduce language-only shortcuts and potential data leakage. We include it in the Reasoning category when reporting category-level performance retention in our experiments. 

TextVQA [39]. TextVQA evaluates scene-text understanding by requiring models to recognize and reason over text in natural images. Answering its questions involves integrating textual content with the surrounding visual context rather than performing text recognition alone. 

DocVQA [34]. DocVQA evaluates question answering over document images, emphasizing OCR, layout understanding, and information localization. Models must locate relevant document regions and interpret their textual content in the context of the question. 

OCRBench [29]. OCRBench evaluates OCR-related capabilities of MLLMs across text recogni-tion, document understanding, information extraction, and related tasks. It assesses the accuracy of text perception and the ability to use textual information for downstream understanding. 

RefCOCO [17, 48]. RefCOCO evaluates referring expression comprehension by requiring models to localize objects described in natural language. Models must match object attributes and relationships to the expression to distinguish the target. 

> 36 MWOP

RefCOCO+ [17, 48]. RefCOCO+ disallows location words during annotation, emphasizing appearance-based referring expressions. Models must identify the target from visual attributes such as color, shape, and clothing. 

RefCOCOg [31, 48]. RefCOCOg contains longer and more descriptive referring expressions, testing fine-grained and context-rich visual grounding. Models must integrate multiple cues, including object attributes and relationships, to identify the intended target. 

F.2 Multi-image benchmarks Q-Bench2 [55]. Q-Bench2 evaluates low-level visual perception through questions about image pairs. Models must jointly inspect and compare visual attributes, such as clarity, lighting, and distortions, to identify subtle differences and similarities between images. 

Mantis-Eval [15]. Mantis-Eval evaluates multi-image reasoning and co-reference through manually curated multiple-choice and short-answer questions. Models must compare visual content and combine evidence across images to answer questions requiring joint interpretation. 

BLINK [11]. BLINK evaluates core visual perception through tasks such as relative depth estimation, visual correspondence, and multi-view reasoning. It requires models to interpret fine-grained visual and spatial cues from single or multiple images. 

F.3 Video benchmarks MVBench [24]. MVBench evaluates temporal video understanding through 20 tasks spanning perception and reasoning. Models must combine evidence across frames to recognize actions, track state changes, and understand event sequences rather than rely on a single frame. 

Video-MME [10]. Video-MME evaluates video understanding across diverse domains and durations, from short clips to hour-long videos. Tasks assess visual perception, temporal reasoning, contextual understanding, and information integration across time. 

NExT-QA [45]. NExT-QA evaluates video question answering with an emphasis on causal and temporal action reasoning. Models must understand event order, object interactions, and the reasons behind actions, moving beyond recognition and description of visible content.
