Not All Frames Are Equal:
Difference-Aware Quantization for Ultra-Low-Bit ASR
Woori Jeon1, Jungmin So1,∗∗
1 Sogang University, Seoul, Korea
woori50300@gmail.com, jso1@sogang.ac.kr
Abstract
Post-training quantization (PTQ) methods such as GPTQ
and AWQ compress large language models effectively, but ap-
plying them to automatic speech recognition (ASR) at ultra-low
bit-widths (2-3 bits) causes severe transcription degradation.
Unlike discrete text tokens, speech activations are highly corre-
lated in steady-state and zero-padded regions yet change rapidly
at phonetic boundaries; when all frames contribute equally to
the Hessian, static frames dominate calibration and obscure in-
formative transitions.
We propose DiffAQ, which computes
frame-to-frame activation differences to measure the rate of
acoustic change and assigns Hessian importance proportion-
ally, concentrating quantization precision on phonetically criti-
cal frames. As a training-free modification to GPTQ, DiffAQ
consistently reduces WER across various Whisper sizes and
standard benchmarks, with the largest gains at 2-bit precision
where baseline methods frequently produce degenerate outputs.
Index Terms: speech recognition, post-training quantization,
Whisper, Hessian importance weighting, low-bit quantization
1. Introduction
The deployment of state-of-the-art Automatic Speech Recogni-
tion (ASR) foundation models, such as OpenAI’s Whisper [1],
on edge devices is heavily bottlenecked by memory footprint
and bandwidth constraints. While these models achieve near-
human transcription accuracy, their parameter counts preclude
real-time execution on resource-constrained hardware.
Recent advancements in Post-Training Quantization (PTQ),
notably GPTQ [2] and AWQ [3], have successfully mitigated
this issue for Large Language Models (LLMs). These meth-
ods compress weights to extremely low precision in a matter
of minutes without requiring the massive computational over-
head of Quantization-Aware Training (QAT). GPTQ achieves
this by using a second-order Hessian inverse matrix to distribute
quantization errors optimally, while AWQ scales salient weights
based on activation magnitudes.
However, researchers attempting to directly apply standard
GPTQ and AWQ to ASR models have observed severe tran-
scription degradation often resulting in complete hallucination
loops at 2-bit and 3-bit weight precisions [4]. We attribute this
failure to a fundamental modality gap between text and speech
[5]. Text consists of discrete tokens with uniform information
density. In contrast, ASR models process continuous audio rep-
resentations that are heavily padded, temporally correlated, and
acoustically redundant, as the waveform and spectrogram in
Figure 1 show. Standard PTQ evaluates parameter importance
by treating every temporal frame’s magnitude equally.

To bridge this modality gap, we introduce Difference-
Aware Quantization (DiffAQ). Since speech intelligibility re-
lies predominantly on dynamic spectral transitions [6, 7], we
modify the PTQ calibration process to track frame-to-frame ac-
tivation differences. DiffAQ assigns Hessian importance pro-
portional to the rate of acoustic change, concentrating quantiza-
tion precision on frames where phonetically distinctive informa-
tion is concentrated. Figure 1 visualizes this: wt drops during
silence and rises sharply at phonetic boundaries.
Our core contributions are as follows:
• We identify that standard PTQ methods are ill-suited for ASR
at ultra-low bit-widths (2-3 bits): their frame-agnostic Hes-
sian estimation is dominated by temporally redundant and
zero-padded frames, leaving the model poorly calibrated for
acoustic transitions that drive transcription accuracy.
• We propose DiffAQ, which computes frame-to-frame activa-
tion differences to measure the rate of acoustic change and
incorporates this as an importance weight during Hessian ac-
cumulation, ensuring that quantization error is minimized at
acoustically dynamic frames where phonetically distinctive
information is concentrated.
• We validate DiffAQ across three Whisper sizes (base, small,
and medium), demonstrating consistent WER improvements
at 2-bit and 3-bit quantization over standard GPTQ and AWQ
baselines.
As a training-free modification to GPTQ, Dif-
fAQ yields the largest gains at 2-bit precision where baseline
methods frequently produce degenerate outputs.

2. Background and Related Work
2.1. Activation-Aware Post-Training Quantization
The most straightforward PTQ approach is Round-to-Nearest
(RTN), which quantizes each weight by rounding it to the near-
est representable value at the target bit-width. While computa-
tionally efficient, RTN ignores the relative importance of indi-
vidual weights, leading to severe accuracy degradation at ultra-
low bit-widths [8]. Modern PTQ relies on calibration data to
identify which weights are most important. GPTQ, rooted in
Optimal Brain Quantization (OBQ) [2], minimizes the squared
output error using the empirical Hessian matrix of the activa-
tions:

2.2. ASR Model Quantization
Model compression for ASR has relied on knowledge distilla-
tion [9] or QAT [10, 11] to reach low bit-widths. While ef-
fective, these approaches require significant computational re-
sources and access to transcribed training data.
PTQ avoids retraining entirely, but early attempts such as
Q-ASR [12] reached only 8-bit precision, and ultra-low-bit
quantization remains challenging. Fish et al. [13] proposed
myQASR, a mixed-precision PTQ method based on layer sensi-
tivity. However, these methods, like standard GPTQ and AWQ,
still calibrate using raw activation magnitudes without account-
ing for the temporal structure of speech.
2.3. Challenges of PTQ for Speech
Unlike LLMs, ASR models operate on log-Mel spectrograms
where a 25 ms window advances in 10 ms steps, creating 60%
overlap between adjacent frames. Due to this overlap, steady-
state segments such as sustained vowels, silence, and padding
produce nearly static consecutive frames [5, 14], and architec-
tures such as Squeezeformer [15] and FastConformer [16] ex-
plicitly downsample the temporal axis to address this redun-
dancy.
When all frames contribute equally to the Hessian (Eq. 1),
these static frames disproportionately dominate the importance
estimate, leaving the quantized model poorly calibrated for the
acoustic transitions that drive transcription accuracy. This prob-
lem is amplified in Whisper, which forces all inputs into a fixed
30-second context window (3,000 frames). If a calibration ut-
terance contains only 3 seconds of active speech, the remaining
2,700 frames consist of zero-padding [17]. GPTQ’s Cholesky
solver then optimizes the quantized weights to reconstruct 27
seconds of silence, largely exhausting the network’s capacity to
preserve the brief transients that carry phonetically distinctive
information.
3. Difference-Aware Quantization (DiffAQ)
Meaningful information in speech is concentrated in regions of
rapid acoustic change [6], and spectral dynamics carry more
discriminative power than static coefficients [18]. We propose
DiffAQ, which computes the frame-to-frame activation differ-
ence and incorporates it as an importance weight during Hes-
sian accumulation. Although DiffAQ operates on intermediate
hidden activations rather than raw audio, our experiments show
that activation differences effectively distinguish dynamic from
static regions.
3.1. Frame-to-Frame Temporal Differencing

encoder.

are computed as temporal derivatives of the feature trajectory

difference:

This frame-to-frame differencing acts as a simple impor-
tance measure for the speech modality:
• Padding Eradication: The difference between consecutive
zero-padded frames is exactly zero, so padding receives min-
imal Hessian weight, a natural special case of the framework
rather than a separate mechanism.
• Redundant Frame Suppression: Due to the 15 ms spectro-
gram overlap, temporally static segments (sustained vowels,
background hum) produce highly correlated consecutive acti-
vations, yielding a near-zero difference and correspondingly
low weight. This aligns with RASTA filtering [19], which
suppresses slowly varying spectral components to improve
robustness.
Because these frames are already well repre-
sented by their neighbors, reducing their Hessian contribu-
tion does not discard information.
• Spectral Transition Emphasis:
At phonetic boundaries
(e.g., from a fricative to a vowel), the acoustic state changes
rapidly. These transient frames yield a large dt and receive
maximal Hessian weight, ensuring quantization error is min-
imized precisely where phonetically distinctive information
is concentrated.

[20], so we retain standard GPTQ (Eq. 1) for the decoder.
4. Experimental Results
4.1. Datasets and Metrics
We evaluate DiffAQ using three sizes of the Whisper architec-
ture: base.en (∼74M parameters), small.en (∼244M pa-
rameters), and medium.en (∼769M parameters). We report
Word Error Rate (WER) on two benchmarks: LibriSpeech [21]
(test-clean and test-other, where test-other con-
tains more acoustically challenging conditions) and the English
subset of FLEURS [22] as an out-of-domain benchmark. For
calibration, we randomly sample 128 utterances from the Lib-
riSpeech train-other-500 split. All results are averaged
over 10 random calibration seeds and reported with 95% confi-
dence intervals.
4.2. Baselines and Configuration
We compare DiffAQ against three primary baselines:
1. Round-To-Nearest (RTN) [8]: Naive uniform quantization
without activation-aware compensation.
2. AWQ [3]: Scales salient channels using activation magni-
tudes before RTN.
3. GPTQ [2]: Minimizes layer-wise reconstruction error using
the standard Hessian (Eq. 1).
All models are quantized asymmetrically using per-group
quantization (group size 64). We target ultra-low bit-widths: 3-
bit and 2-bit weight precision (W3A16 and W2A16). DiffAQ
introduces only element-wise vector subtraction and normaliza-
tion over the T × D activation matrix, which is asymptotically
negligible compared to GPTQ’s O(D3) Cholesky decomposi-
tion; in practice, calibration time is virtually identical to stan-
dard GPTQ.
4.3. Ultra-Low Bit Transcription Accuracy
Tables 1 and 2 present the WER of DiffAQ and baselines under
3-bit and 2-bit quantization.
At 3-bit precision, DiffAQ achieves the lowest mean WER
across all datasets and model sizes. For Whisper Medium, the
gains over GPTQ are modest (e.g., 6.08% vs. 6.21% on test-
other), indicating that 3-bit precision retains sufficient capac-
ity for standard methods. The gap widens for smaller mod-
els, which have less parameter redundancy and are therefore
more sensitive to how calibration distributes quantization error;
for Whisper Small on FLEURS, DiffAQ reduces WER from
11.37% (GPTQ) to 8.69%.
The advantage of DiffAQ becomes evident under the tighter
constraints of 2-bit quantization. RTN and AWQ lack Hessian-
based error compensation and suffer severe degradation at ultra-
low bit-widths, often producing degenerate outputs that inflate
the WER beyond 100%. Notably, AWQ yields error rates nearly
identical to RTN; we observe that at this precision, AWQ’s top
1% activation selection often identifies only a single channel
group, providing insufficient compensation.
While standard
GPTQ partially mitigates this through Hessian-guided recon-
struction, DiffAQ further reduces errors by concentrating Hes-
sian weight on dynamic phonetic boundaries. For the Medium
model, DiffAQ lowers the 2-bit WER from 17.53% (GPTQ) to
12.93% on the LibriSpeech test-other split.

floor helps ensure numerical stability of the Hessian.
5. Discussion
While DiffAQ shows consistent improvements, three limita-
tions are worth noting.
First, the temporal difference is not
speech-selective; transient non-speech sounds such as back-
ground noise or sudden environmental events can produce large
activation differences and receive unintended high importance
during calibration. Incorporating a voice activity detection sig-
nal could improve robustness in noisy conditions.
Second,
for smaller models such as Whisper Base, combining DiffAQ
with layer-wise mixed-precision allocation, where sensitive lay-
ers retain higher bit-widths, may offer a practical path forward
when uniform 2-bit quantization exceeds the model’s represen-
tational capacity. Third, our evaluation is limited to Whisper,
which we chose as it is one of the most widely used open-source
ASR models and whose fixed 30-second context window repre-
sents a worst case for the padding trap. Because the underly-
ing temporal redundancy of mel-spectrogram features is shared
by all encoder-based ASR models, we expect the principle to
transfer to streaming and CTC-based [23] architectures, though
empirical verification remains future work.
6. Conclusions
We proposed DiffAQ, a training-free modification to GPTQ that
addresses the mismatch between standard PTQ calibration and
speech inputs by weighting each frame’s Hessian contribution
according to its frame-to-frame activation difference. Across
two benchmarks and three Whisper model sizes, DiffAQ con-
sistently reduced WER at both 2-bit and 3-bit precision, with
the largest gains at 2-bit: on Whisper Medium, DiffAQ lowered
the test-other WER from 17.53% (GPTQ) to 12.93%, while
standard methods frequently produced degenerate outputs on
smaller models. These results demonstrate that explicitly ac-
counting for the temporal structure of speech during PTQ cal-
ibration is a practical step toward reliable ultra-low-bit ASR
quantization. At 2-bit precision, the weight memory of Whis-
per Medium shrinks from approximately 1.5 GB (FP16) to un-
der 200 MB, bringing practical ASR closer to mobile and em-
bedded deployment. Extending DiffAQ to other encoder-based
ASR architectures, combining it with mixed-precision layer al-
location, and exploring joint weight-activation quantization are
promising directions for future work.
