Task-Phase-Conditioned Expert Routing Is Not a Reliable Proxy
for Quantization Sensitivity in MoE FFN Tensors
Yifan Liu
UIUC MCS Student yifanl25@illinois.edu
October 16, 2026
Abstract
We investigate whether per-layer, per-task-phase (prompt/reasoning/response) expert-
routing statistics in a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) language model can guide layer-wise
mixed-precision quantization of routed-expert FFN tensors (down/gate and up). To attain
layerwise routing statistics, we build a routing-capture and profiling pipeline for Qwen-3.6-
35B-A3B in ik llama.cpp [Kawrakow et al., 2024], derive a composite sensitivity score from
routing entropy/decisiveness, coefficient of variation (CV), Jaccard/phase-fragility, and top-1
expert strength, and use coordinate descent to tune a layer allocation that respects a fixed
average bits-per-weight budget. Notably, the profiling pipeline is applicable to standard
top-k MoE architectures. Across independent experimental conditions (∼4.0 bpw flat, ∼2.7
bpw steep, and ∼2.7 bpw random allocation as a control), reallocation fails to outperform
the uniform baseline within each quality tier. In the flat regime, reallocation is statistically
neutral (z ≈0.9). For the steep tier, the random reallocation performs within 1 SE of the
routing-guided reallocation at the same tier, indicating a structural loss at this quality rung
caused by mandatory downgrades and Kullback-Leibler Divergence (KLD) tail injection.
This aggressive-budget condition shows a decisive loss (z > 4 SE) replicated across three
independent weight vectors. We argue that the deeper issue is that routing-behavior statistics
are an unvalidated proxy for true per-layer quantization cost.

Introduction
As MoE models have become ubiquitous in deployment, there exists a need for the development
of targeted quantization strategies to achieve better KLD and Perplexity (PPL) values. A
notable trend has been models becoming increasingly large, where users frequently turn to
aggressive quantizations to run these models locally.
We construct a test harness to evaluate whether MoE routing behavior through different
experts for token types and phases can guide budget-neutral reallocation of precision per layer.
Specifically, we characterize these phases as prompt, Chain of Thought (CoT) reasoning, and
response. We employ the high-quality IQ quantization types present in ik llama.cpp [Kawrakow
et al., 2024]. A natural hypothesis arising from MoE routing is that there exist MoE layers with
more distinctive phase-dependent or concentrated routing behavior and that these layers should
receive more quantization precision while less distinctive layers should receive more aggressive
quantization; we test whether this hypothesis holds. We initially allocated extra precision to the
distinctive/fragile layers; however, our best-measured point for KLD actually resulted from a
flip in allocation strategy placing more precision on the uniform/robust layers. That direction
flip still failed to beat the uniform baseline.
Previous work on MoE quantization has mainly focused on activation-frequency or expert-
importance mixed-precision MoEs [Duanmu et al., 2025, Deng et al., 2026, Chitty-Venkata
et al., 2025], classical rate-distortion (water-filling) bit allocation, and sensitivity-based mixed
precision in dense LLMs with direct measures, such as the Hessian and single-layer ablation.

These methods condition on either a behavioral activation or importance signal that is not
phase-aware (MxMoE, GEMQ) or on a direct sensitivity measurement (MoPEQ and the dense-
model methods); none tests whether a phase-conditioned routing proxy can substitute for a
direct measurement.
We do not describe a method that beats existing quantization types and methods. We lay
out the precise coordinate descent experiments to find coefficients for our allocation function
that tests the hypothesis and analyze the likely causes for failure. During the evaluation of the
quantized candidates devised from the reallocation strategy against a corpus of coding text,
there were several positions with dramatically large KLD; the 99th-percentile KLD rose from
0.44 (uniform) to ∼0.49 on the reallocated candidates. Since mean-KLD is tail-dominated the
injection of these values increased KLD and PPL against the uniform baseline and decreased
top-1 token agreement. The magnitude of these effects varied across quant tier. In the flat
regime, the difference in KLD with the uniform baseline was within noise (a neutral result for
KLD z ≈0.9), whereas the steep regime shows a decisive loss across all these metrics (KLD
z > 4 SE).
The rest of this paper will explore the data used, the profiling pipeline, the composite score,
and the coordinate-descent search in Section 3. We report the two budget regimes and the
random-selection control in Section 4. Finally, we provide an analysis of why budget-neutral
reallocation loses and its implications for routing statistics as a quantization signal in Section 5.

Related Work
MoE mixed-precision quantization. Mixed-precision quantization has become the standard
for post-training quantization (PTQ) of MoE LLMs. Existing approaches differ in the signals for
bit-width allocation. MxMoE [Duanmu et al., 2025] combines two strategies, including linear-
block sensitivity estimates and a systems approach that leverages expert activation frequency for
hardware co-designed allocation, tailoring to the computational capabilities of the deployment
environment. GEMQ [Deng et al., 2026] focuses on global, expert-level importance ranking in a
progressive refinement loop to achieve extremely low-bit quantizations, arguing that per-layer
local importance estimation does not optimally account for routing, which is perturbed by
quantization. MoPEQ [Chitty-Venkata et al., 2025] uses Hessian trace approximation to allocate
varying bit-widths for experts, noting that expert activation frequency does not predict sensitivity.
EAQuant [Fu et al., 2026] addresses the problem of activation outliers by employing expert-aware
activation smoothing and router-logit alignment, while also focusing on improving calibration
data for MoE LLM PTQ. MoEQuant [Chen et al., 2025] also leverages calibration sampling while
contributing affinity-guided quantization to quantify the interplay between individual samples
and experts. Both EAQuant and MoEQuant do not propose a sensitivity-based allocation scheme.
None of these methods conditions its sensitivity or importance signal on task phase (prompt,
reasoning, and response). Building on MoPEQ’s finding that expert activation frequency does
not predict sensitivity, our approach instead tests whether a phase-conditioned routing-behavior
signal can serve as a lightweight proxy for the direct measurements (Hessian trace, per-expert
ablation) that MoPEQ and dense-model methods employ.
Classical rate-distortion allocation. Optimal bit allocation given a total-rate constraint is
a classical problem in rate-distortion theory [Cover and Thomas, 2006]. Notably, the water-filling
solution [Shoham and Gersho, 1988] allocates marginal bits to equalize marginal distortion-
reduction per bit across all dimensions when given a per-dimension distortion-rate function. The
allocation problem for distributing a fixed average-bpw budget across layers is structurally the
same as water-filling; however, the routing-behavior signals (decisiveness, CV, phase-fragility,
and top-1 expert strength) we leverage in our composite score are an indirect proxy for the true
distortion-rate curve for a layer.
Sensitivity-based mixed precision in dense LLMs. In dense-model quantization,
2


Hessian-trace and eigenvalue approximation methods such as HAWQ [Dong et al., 2019] and
GPTQ’s [Frantar et al., 2023] Hessian-guided error compensation, along with single-layer ablation
measurements to isolate the contribution of one component to output error serve as the standard
methods to directly estimate per-weight or per-layer sensitivity. The accuracy of these direct
measurements depends on a backward pass or ablation sweep per layer. Other more ubiquitous
methods include imatrix [Kawrakow et al., 2024], which can be applied to both MoE and dense
models by calculating per-channel activation statistics through calibration sampling. This paper
relies on a purely behavioral routing statistic computed through forward passes alone (somewhat
similar to imatrix) to substitute for direct sensitivity measurement in a MoE setting.
3
Methods
3.1
Model and Setup
The model employed throughout all the experiments is Qwen-3.6-35B-A3B [Qwen Team, 2026].
This model has 40 layers plus a separate MTP head (blk.40); dynamic allocation applies to
blk.0-38, with the terminal layer blk.39 held at baseline. The model is quantized with an
imatrix and run in ik llama.cpp [Kawrakow et al., 2024], the engine that we modify for our
experiments. The imatrix supplies per-channel activation statistics from a calibration set to
guide quantization scaling; it is the standard llama.cpp calibration mechanism and is separate
from the routing-capture allocation signal developed in Section 3.4.
3.2
Data Pipeline and Bug Fixes
We curate a pool of 186 coding prompts from HumanEval-X [Zheng et al., 2023] (Python, C++,
Java, JS, Go), HumanEval-Rust [Diverso AI Lab, 2023], and LiveCodeBench Lite [Jain et al.,
2024], spanning easy, medium, and hard difficulty. For the profiling pass through the modified
ik llama.cpp engine, we stratify-sample 30 prompts from this pool, balanced across source,
language, and difficulty.
We patch ik llama.cpp to capture the per-token top-k expert selections and gate probabilities
at each MoE block, allowing us to characterize the routing behavior of Qwen-3.6-35B-A3B. For
efficiency gains, the strategy we use is surrogate routing capture. The model is first quantized
to ∼4 bpw per tensor with a Q8 0 precision router and attention/SSM tensors to ensure high
fidelity routing with the routed experts quantized to IQ4 KS (down) and IQ3 K (gate/up). Since
the Q8 0 router decides the actual top-k experts which fire per token, the signal is still faithful
when the routed experts are quantized. The objective of surrogate routing capture was to ensure
that it was possible to capture routing data on systems where running the full quality BF16 is
not possible.
We resolve several bugs regarding our patch. Initially, we encountered issues with identifying
the boundaries of prompts in the captured data. To be able to separate each prompt from
the routing capture data, we added per-request IDs for each prompt profiled. One bug caused
routing capture data to be dropped without error. We discovered that the fix was to turn
graph reuse off. This change decreases decode performance to ensure proper routing capture
data. Another separate bug also caused silent failures for the pipeline to capture prompts of
certain lengths. The source of this bug was when prompt token length exceeded the value set
for ubatch-size which corrupted the capture. It was essential to set both the batch and ubatch
size to be longer than the maximum token count of the 30 coding prompts.
During the collection phase, we obtain expert coverage percentage, top-1 fraction, normalized
entropy, and cross-prompt Jaccard (mean/std) and cross-phase pairwise Jaccard. These scores
are combined into four signals to describe per-layer and per-phase statistics. These signals
include entropy (decisiveness), CV (concentration), cross-phase Jaccard (phase-fragility), and
top-1 gate prob (confidence). These become the four scores which we assign coefficients and

3.3
Segmentation
The profiling is segmented into prompt, reasoning, and response through two boundary rules.
The prompt is designated as the prefill region and defined by the request’s prompt tokens length,
whereas the reasoning is the span from the end of the prompt up to and including the first
</think> token. The remainder of the generation is the response. If any generation lacks a
</think> token, then all post-prompt content is folded into the response; in practice, all 30
prompts emit a close delimiter. We constrained the model’s server-side reasoning budget to
8192 tokens. The maximum total tokens per request was set to 32768, ensuring that the model
4


finished its reasoning and emitted a full response. Since each segment differs substantially in
token count (8.5k / 128k / 26.7k), per-layer statistics were computed and z-scored within each
segment.
3.4
Composite Sensitivity Score
To allocate bits and different quantizations to routed-experts tensors (down, and gate/up), we
devise a composite score function. This score consists of four signals each representing a property
of a layer’s expert-usage distribution, including decisiveness (sharp routing), concentration,
phase-fragility (how differently the model routes across phases), and confidence.
With each segment having a different aggregate token count (differing up to ∼15x) each signal
needs to be z-scored for normalization within its own scale. Since we take the maximum composite
score (max-aggregation) across all three segments, the z-scoring prevents any composite score
from dominating through volume of tokens processed, facilitating fair comparison across the
different segments.
The weighted composite function is shown in Equation (1). The weights allow each signal’s
relative importance to be tuned. Iterative coordinate descent is applied to determine the optimal
weights.

Because the downstream allocation in Section 3.5 consumes only rank(Sl), the ordering of

inputs. We exploit this characteristic to fix wd ≡1, so the composite score is parameterized by
a four-component weight vector w = (wd, wc, wp, wt) with wd held at 1; the search is therefore
over the three free ratios {wc, wp, wt} only. The 3-axis coordinate descent in Section 3.6 is
consequently well-posed.
3.5
Allocation Mechanism
For every profiled layer, we choose a down tier and gu tier pair. Specifically, we bundle the gate
and up tensors together and quantize them at the same tier. The byte model is down + 2 · gu
per layer. The multiplier 2 is deliberate since gate and up are separate tensors.
To designate different quantization types to allocate to the layers, we draw from ik llama.cpp’s
IQK family; the per-tensor bpw of every tier used in this work is listed in Appendix A (Table 6).
Each layer’s allocated tier is within a small band (±1-2 rungs ≈0.25-0.5 bpw per rung) of that
regime’s baseline so the reallocation is a local rearrangement. We analyze the effects of dynamic
layer-wise quantization at two quality regimes.
We run a budget-fit algorithm that consists of the following steps. The layers are first
ranked by their composite-score percentile. The layers are then split into a fragile pool of the
low-ranked layers and a robust pool of the high-ranked ones. We downgrade the layers of the

The importance of budget-neutral quantizations cannot be understated, as it forms the
basis for comparable and fair quality (KLD and PPL) comparisons between the quantizations
generated from the budget-fit algorithm and the uniform baseline. We define budget-neutral as
size-matched to the uniform baseline of that quality regime with approximately the same amount
of bits just rearranged. This definition is enforced in the algorithm with the two gates, including
a pre-quantize dry-run projection check for the final size of the model post quantization and
a post-quantize measured-byte check. Both of these checks are compared against the uniform
baseline’s bytes.
Another parameter we set is whether the extra bits go to the fragile layers or the robust
layers. We ran experiments in both directions; the favorable direction is discussed in Section 4.
To avoid re-benchmarking identical allocations, we canonicalize each tier map to a sorted JSON
form, hash it with SHA-1, and use the resulting hash as the cache key. If a tier map is already
in the cache, its metrics are reused with no re-quantization, while a miss triggers a single
quantize-and-evaluate. Two distinct weight vectors that yield the same tier map therefore
collapse to one entry so redundant allocations are never re-quantized.
3.6
Coordinate Descent Search
The coordinate descent process optimizes the 4 weights in the composite score; however, we only
tune the 3 free weight ratios from Section 3.4 with wd anchored to 1. We tune each free ratio
with coordinate descent cyclically, one axis at a time, where the objective is minimum KLD. The
top-1 token agreement and PPL are also recorded but not used to dictate descent direction. Each
weight vector maps to a tier map, as mentioned in Section 3.5, and each tier map is evaluated
one time to obtain the KLD.
A different grid of multipliers is implemented for each round. In the first round, which
is coarse, each axis tests multipliers {0.5, 1.5, 3.0} of its value. In the second round, which is
adaptive, the grid of an axis is chosen by the winner of the first round. An interior winner is
assigned a grid of {0.75, 1.0, 1.25}. A high-edge winner gets a grid of {1.0, 1.5} which extends the
search up, capped at 4.5x. A low-edge winner gets a grid of {1.0, 0.5, 0.25}, extending the search
down. In the worst case, the coordinate search explores at most 18 distinct quantizations (3
axes x 3 candidates x 2 rounds). The number of quantizations is usually fewer after the caching
strategy in Section 3.5 and early-stop is applied. The early-stop strategy defines a stopping
condition as a full pass that changes nothing, where there is no KLD improvement. As a control
experiment, we use a seed-frozen, depth and budget-matched random-layer-selection variant
evaluated through the same pipeline as a control.
Our coordinate descent implementation is resumable and cache-aware, as intermediate states
are written to temporary JSON tracking files as checkpoints. This design allows for pausing
and resuming the GPU-driven evals. Since the objective is a plateau, many weight vectors map
6


to the same tier map and hence the same KLD. The search is also dedupe-aware, skipping any
candidate whose tier map is already measured. An example of the full logs can be found in
Appendix B.
3.7
Evaluation Methodology
To evaluate each quantization generated by the budget-fit algorithm, we first construct the high-
precision KLD reference by running the model quantized uniformly to Q8 0 over a coding corpus.
We use Q8 0 as a proxy for BF16 quality; prior work on Qwen3 quantization reports near-lossless
performance at moderate-to-high bit-widths including 8-bit precision, with degradation emerging
primarily under ultra-low-bit settings [Zheng et al., 2026], and independent KL-divergence
measurements on Qwen-3.6 similarly find that 8-bit quantization tracks BF16 closely, with
residual divergence roughly an order of magnitude smaller than 4-bit quantization [McLeod,
2026].
We measure each candidate with a single llama-perplexity run using --kl-divergence and
--kl-divergence-base against the Q8 0 reference logits. This single run yields mean KLD, PPL,
and top-1 agreement, along with the KLD tail: the 99th and 95th percentiles, the median, and
the maximum. The llama-perplexity flags are shared across the Q8 0 reference, the candidates,
and the uniform baseline through a single function, so all three are computed with identical
context, layer placement, and batch settings to ensure direct comparability.
The evaluation corpus is drawn from The Stack v3 by streaming the dataset from Hugging-
FaceCode [Lozhkov et al., 2026]. It is held out from the profiling data, which originates from
HumanEval-X and LiveCodeBench, so no prompt in the evaluation set was seen during routing
capture. To reduce training-data contamination we exclude repositories created before June
2024, restrict to permissively-licensed code, and drop vendored third-party files. To promote
representational diversity, a per-language token budget balances coverage across 21 languages,
including niche languages, while seeded shuffling and multi-shard sampling provide additional
diversity during data selection. The final corpus is ∼331K tokens (∼647 chunks at context 512),
comparable in size to the widely-used wikitext-2 test set [Merity et al., 2016].
3.8
A Methodological Note on Validation-Set Bias
The coordinate-descent search is a plateau objective over a small weight space. Initially, we run
the evaluations on a frozen 12K-token slice (∼24 chunks at context 512) to keep GPU evaluation
cheap. We unfortunately note that the slice does not serve as an accurate proxy for the true
full-corpus KLD. Specifically, the uniform baseline’s KLD at the IQ3 K quality regime was
0.019325 on the 12K slice but 0.014725 on the full 331K-token corpus. The full-corpus KLD is
∼24% lower than the slice estimate. Since the relative ranking of candidates against the uniform
baseline also did not transfer from the slice to the full corpus, we report all final results on the
full corpus and acknowledge that the slice is only used to drive the descent.

4.3
Random-Selection Control
To determine if our method held any statistical significance, we compare our score guided
approach against two quantization recipes generated with random seeds. Holding depth, tiers,
and budget fixed and randomizing the assignment of quantization types to the layers, the random
candidates land within ∼1 SE of (and marginally below) the score-guided ones. This result
informs us that the reallocation structure is responsible for the loss, where the mandatory IQ2
downgrades that were required to fund the upgraded-quality layers inject KLD-tail positions.
Decisively, the composite score adds no selection signal over random layer selection. Table 5
reports these random candidates alongside the score-guided set and the uniform baseline.

5
Discussion
5.1
Why Budget-Neutral Reallocation Loses
Fundamentally, budget-neutral reallocation loses because of its zero-sum structure: every upgrade
is funded by downgrades on other layers. In the steep regime, for example, upgrading 17 layers
by one rung (+1.25 bpw per layer) required downgrading 19 layers to the IQ2 floor tier (−1.12
bpw per layer), with 3 layers staying at baseline; the gains and costs cancel almost exactly,
so every candidate lands at the uniform budget. This finding leads to the fact that the net
∆KLD = P
l gl −P
l cl, where gl and cl are the per-layer marginal gains and costs. To produce
a win against the uniform baseline, the reallocation criterion would need to rank layers by their
true marginal KLD-per-byte.
Water-filling theory states that optimal allocation equalizes marginal distortion-reduction per
bit. Our proxy composite score utilizes routing behavior measures (entropy, CV, phase-fragility,
top-1 strength) which are not a direct measurement of quantization distortion. The premise
of our experiments was based on the hypothesis that layerwise phase-distinctive routing had a
degree of correlation with the cost of reducing or increasing the bpw for a specific layer. There
was never a theoretical basis for this expectation, and our empirical results showed it to be
unsupported as well.
In the flat regime, the effect of the net ∆KLD can be seen directly. At this quality tier
all layers are near optimum on the uniform baseline. Therefore, any marginal gains from our
allocation are immediately nullified by the marginal costs. The overall KLD nets to ∼zero, with
all 8 candidates actually scoring slightly worse (z = +0.94 to +1.79). In the steep regime, there
was a convex cost curve and the IQ2 floor cliff. This characteristic led to downgrades costing
disproportionately more than upgrades gain; therefore, any nontrivial reallocation loses.
Naturally, we questioned the entire premise with the random-selection control. If the routing
statistics tracked quantization sensitivity even weakly, the score-guided allocations would have
beaten random selection at matched depth and budget. Since random selection lands within ∼1
SE of (and marginally better than) the score-guided candidates, at 0.03773/0.03781 KLD versus
0.03814–0.03825, we acknowledge that the composite score adds no selection signal over random.
5.2
Relationship to Floor-Tier Cliff
As we discussed in Section 5.1, there are two compounding effects: the per-tensor IQ2 floor
quality cliff, and the proxy failing to identify differentially-sensitive layers.
The floor-tier cliff can be quantified by its tail KLD injections. With 19 out of 39 profiled
layers requiring mandatory downgrades, the 99th percentile KLD rises from 0.437 to 0.483–0.495
on every reallocated candidate, score-guided and random alike. The baseline KLD distribution
is already heavy-tailed: median 0.0016, 95th percentile 0.119, maximum 26.4, hence the ∼20×
mean/median gap reported in Section 4.2. The mandatory downgrades act on this tail rather
9


than on the bulk. The median moves only from 0.0016 to at most 0.0019, and the maximum
stays within 26.4–27.5, so neither the typical position nor the single worst case is what degrades.
Instead, the distribution develops greater mass in the tail, with more positions entering the
catastrophic regime, and this shift accounts for the mean-KLD loss of 0.0034–0.0039.
The random-selection allocations lose to the uniform baseline because the cliff toll is incurred
per tensor rather than per layer. At this depth, any budget-neutral reallocation must therefore
downgrade 19 layers to the floor tier, regardless of which layers are selected. Swapping which
layers receive the floor-tier IQ2 quality does not change the cost, because the tail injection is a
property of the tier itself rather than of the specific layers that carry it. The implication is that
below the elbow of the Pareto curve, reallocation within a fixed budget does not help. The only
mitigation of catastrophic tail injection is to raise the floor globally, providing more budget.
5.3
Limitations
A primary limitation of our study was the application of the composite score budget-reallocation
strategy to a single model, Qwen-3.6-35B-A3B, which is a hybrid linear-attention MoE. We do
not claim that our results can generalize to other models or scales. A cross-model transfer to
the much larger GLM-5.3 [GLM-5 Team, 2026], a MoE language model with 753B total and
40B active parameters, was planned but not executed.
Since our per-layer, phase-based routing profiling ran on a surrogate quantization (down
experts at IQ4 KS, gate/up at IQ3 K, with a Q8 0 router), it is possible that the routing was
itself perturbed by quantization. There was no comparison performed to check the routing
agreement versus a full-precision BF16 version of the model.
There was also no direct-sensitivity ground truth. We disclose the limitation of having no
single-layer ablation study and the lack of Hessian correlation values. We are unable to bound
what a direct quantization-sensitivity signal would have achieved; we can only conclude that
this proxy achieves nothing.
For the data, we constructed additional datasets for multiple topics (reasoning, roleplay,
chat, and trivia); however, the experiments in this paper only utilized the code task domain
for both profiling and evaluation. The number of samples profiled was 30, and no tests were
done to validate whether this was the optimal number of profiling samples. During the layerwise
quality allocations, we opted to hold the terminal layer (blk.39) at the same quality as the
uniform baseline; therefore, 39 of the 40 layers were profiled and received allocations. It is
possible that including the terminal layer in the allocation could have influenced the results
slightly. Segmentation also depended on the </think> delimiter to split generations into prompt,
reasoning, and response, which all 30 profiling prompts happened to emit; a segmentation using
only the prompt and response segments (e.g., for models without a reasoning trace) could have
produced different sensitivity signals for the composite score.
6
Conclusion
Task-phase-conditioned routing statistics, despite a carefully instrumented pipeline spanning
routing capture, composite scoring, and budget-neutral coordinate descent, do not translate
into a useful equal-budget allocation criterion for layer-wise mixed-precision quantization of the
routed-expert FFN tensors. The score-guided allocations are neutral at ∼4 bpw and decisively
harmful at ∼2.7 bpw. We underscore that the mechanism behind these results is structural:
mandatory downgrades cause the KLD tail injection. The random-selection control shows that
the heuristic is not at fault. We report these results to show that the intuitive connection
presumed to exist between routing behavior and layer-wise quantization sensitivity does not hold.
Future work would need to explore the connection at a higher level of granularity, potentially
allocating precision to individual experts within a layer, and to validate candidate signals against
10


directly measured layer sensitivity.
