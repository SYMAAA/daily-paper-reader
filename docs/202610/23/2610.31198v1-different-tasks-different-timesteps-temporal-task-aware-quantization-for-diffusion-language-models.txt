Abstract 

Diffusion Language Models (DLMs) introduce unique quantization challenges due to their it-erative denoising process. In this work, we reveal that quantization sensitivity is both task-and time-dependent, with different downstream tasks exhibiting distinct precision requirements across denoising steps. We further show that these patterns can be predicted without ground-truth labels or task-specific evaluation met-rics. Building on these findings, we intro-duce T2Quant , a task and time-aware mixed-precision quantization framework that uses a label-free sensitivity signal to optimally allo-cate activation precision across denoising steps under a fixed bit-width budget. Extensive ex-periments across three DLMs and six bench-marks demonstrate that T2Quant consistently improves the average accuracy of existing quan-tization methods, achieving gains of up to 6.7 percentage points under the same budget. 

1 Introduction 

Diffusion Language Models (DLMs) have emerged as a compelling alternative to autoregressive (AR) language models, generating text through itera-tive denoising with bidirectional context and par-allel token prediction (Nie et al., 2025; Ye et al., 2025). However, iterative denoising requires re-peated model evaluations, making efficient infer-ence increasingly important. Quantization is a widely adopted technique for reducing the memory footprint and computational cost of large language models. However, methods developed for AR models may not directly transfer to DLMs due to their distinct step-wise decoding dynamics. Recent studies have identified DLM-specific quantization challenges, including activa-tion outliers, timestep-dependent distributions, and accumulated quantization errors (Lin et al., 2026; Xu and Yang, 2025; Yan et al., 2026; Huang et al., 2026). Furthermore, we find that the benefit of additional precision can be predicted without ground-truth labels or downstream metric evaluations. Specifi-cally, we develop a label-free sensitivity signal that combines quantization-induced divergence with the downstream influence of each denoising stage, enabling the identification of task-specific preci-sion requirements using only unlabeled calibration prompts. Building on these findings, we introduce 

T2Quant (Task and Time-Aware Quantization ), a mixed-precision framework that allocates acti-vation precision across denoising steps accord-ing to task-specific sensitivity patterns (Figure 2). T2Quant leverages the proposed sensitivity signal to determine an optimal precision schedule under a fixed average bit-width budget through budget-constrained dynamic programming. Our frame-work is complementary to existing post-training quantization methods and can be integrated with different quantization approaches without modi-fying their underlying quantizers. Extensive ex-periments across three DLMs and six benchmarks demonstrate consistent improvements in average accuracy under a fixed budget, outperforming the strongest evaluated quantized baselines by up to 6.7 percentage points. Our main contributions are summarized as follows: • Task and time-dependent precision sensi-tivity. We reveal distinct temporal precision sensitivity patterns across downstream tasks in DLMs. • Label-free sensitivity prediction. We pre-dict precision importance using internal model signals, without ground-truth labels or task-specific evaluation metrics. • Task and time-aware mixed-precision quan-tization. We introduce T 2Quant, which op-timally allocates activation precision under a fixed bit budget and improves existing quanti-zation methods. 

2 Related Work 

DLM Quantization. DLMs such as LLaDA and Dream generate text by iterative masked denoising with bidirectional context rather than left-to-right decoding (Nie et al., 2025; Ye et al., 2025), which raises quantization challenges absent from autore-gressive LLMs. DLLMQuant introduces diffusion-aware calibration and quantization (Xu and Yang, 2025); STaR-Quant uses state-guided transforma-tions and temporal compensation (Yan et al., 2026); FAIR-Calib targets fragile denoising states (Huang et al., 2026); and Quant-dLLM explores sensitivity-based mixed precision (Zhang et al., 2026a). These methods show that diffusion state and temporal error propagation matter, yet all keep a fixed activa-tion bit-width across the denoising trajectory. We instead treat activation precision as a resource al-located over denoising steps under a fixed average bit budget .

Task-Aware Quantization. Task-Aware Quan-tization (TAQ) estimates layer importance from a small task-specific calibration set and assigns higher weight precision to task-relevant layers under a fixed bit budget (LeVi et al., 2026), and Task-Circuit Quantization (TaCQ) preserves task-relevant weight circuits, identified from 2quantization-induced weight changes and gradient-based impact estimates, at higher precision (Xiao et al., 2025). Both show that the best alloca-tion of a quantization budget depends on the task, but they allocate precision spatially , across layers or weights of autoregressive LLMs. In contrast, T2Quant explores temporal task dependence, allo-cating activation precision across denoising steps according to task-specific sensitivity. 

Temporal Precision Scheduling. Temporal vari-ation is a recognized factor in image diffusion 

quantization: Q-Diffusion uses timestep-aware cal-ibration for shifting activation distributions, and correction methods compensate for errors accu-mulated across denoising steps (Li et al., 2023). Most closely related, TMPDiff assigns different precisions to different denoising timesteps in im-age diffusion models , modeling the final latent de-viation as an approximately additive sum of per-timestep errors and searching over schedules built from single-timestep sensitivities (Lewandowski et al., 2026). DLMs differ qualitatively: generation consists of discrete, often irreversible token commit-ments, latent reconstruction error does not directly reflect downstream correctness , and a timestep’s importance depends on which task-relevant tokens it commits. Rather than a single global schedule, 

T2Quant allocates activation precision per task ,linking where quantization disrupts task-relevant generation to where additional precision should be spent. 

3 Task-Dependent Precision Sensitivity Across Diffusion Steps 

The iterative generation process of DLMs raises a fundamental question for quantization: when does numerical precision matter most? Each denois-ing step operates on a different partially generated state, with token commitments potentially influenc-ing subsequent predictions (Nie et al., 2025; Ye et al., 2025). Consequently, the impact of quanti-zation may depend not only on the denoising step but also on the downstream task. Understanding these variations, and whether they can be predicted, motivates three research questions: • RQ1: Where does quantization sensitivity oc-cur in time? • RQ2: Is temporal sensitivity task-dependent? • RQ3: Can we predict where additional preci-sion is most valuable? We address RQ1 and RQ2 through temporal di-vergence analysis (Section 3.1), and RQ3 through a label-free precision-allocation proxy (Section 3.2). 

Experimental setup. We evaluate LLaDA-8B-Instruct (Nie et al., 2025) on three tasks with dis-tinct output structures: GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021), HumanEval (Chen et al., 2021), and MMLU-Pro (Wang et al., 2024). We use 512-token gen-erations over 128 denoising steps with a single 512-token block, partitioning the trajectory into 16 non-overlapping windows of eight steps. We use the standard low-confidence remasking sam-pler of Nie et al. (2025) with temperature 0 and no classifier-free guidance. The quantized model uses QuaRot-style rotations (Ashkboos et al., 2024), 4-bit GPTQ weights (Frantar et al., 2023), and dy-namic per-token 4-bit activations. The target preci-sion is Atarget = 4 bits and the upscaled precision is Aup = 8 bits. Further details are given in Ap-pendix E. 

3.1 Temporal Sensitivity Diagnostic Temporal sensitivity. Figure 1 shows that quan-tization sensitivity is highly non-uniform across the denoising trajectory. A uniform distribution would assign each of the 16 eight-step windows 6.25% of the task-relevant divergence. Instead, on GSM8K the first window alone carries 27% (4.4 × uniform) and the first quarter 55%, while on HumanEval five mid-trajectory windows (steps 32–71) carry 52%. Even MMLU-Pro, the most evenly spread task, varies by nearly 2 × between its most and least sensitive windows. This con-centration suggests that activation precision need not be allocated uniformly over time and motivates selectively increasing precision at the most sensi-tive stages, as explored for image diffusion models (Lewandowski et al., 2026). Key Takeaway Quantization sensitivity varies across denoising steps. 

Task-dependent sensitivity. The location and spread of these sensitive regions vary substantially across tasks. GSM8K is strongly front-loaded, with its peak in the first 8 denoising steps and a rapid decay thereafter. MMLU-Pro is also early, but broad: its first four windows (steps 0–31) are all 3within 10% of its peak, after which sensitivity de-clines gradually. In contrast, HumanEval is in-sensitive early and exhibits a broad mid-trajectory peak, roughly around steps 40–63. Thus, a single task-independent temporal precision schedule is unlikely to suit all tasks. Whether these sensitiv-ity profiles can predict where additional precision actually improves downstream performance is ex-amined in Section 3.2. Key Takeaway Sensitivity patterns differ across tasks, challenging fixed temporal preci-sion schedules.

5.3 Ablation Study 

Figure 4 evaluates three aspects of T 2Quant on LLaDA-8B-Instruct and GSM8K under W4A4 (uniform W4A4: 44.1, T 2Quant: 49.4, FP16: 52.9). 

(a) Calibration size: Just 16 prompts recover roughly 75% of the full gain, with stable schedules consistently prioritizing early denoising steps. (b) Tokens per step: The schedule transfers from 4 to 2 tokens per step, maintaining an approximately 5-point improvement, while gains vanish at 8 tokens per step as overall accuracy deteriorates. (c) Acti-vation budget: T2Quant achieves 48.4% accuracy with only 3.75 average bits, outperforming uniform W4A4 (44.1%) despite using fewer bits. More-over, T 2Quant consistently outperforms random schedules at the same bit budget, while increasing precision beyond 4 bits provides negligible addi-tional benefit. These results demonstrate that effec-tive precision allocation matters more than simply increasing the bit budget. 6 Discussion 

Our findings demonstrate that quantization sensitiv-ity in DLMs depends on both the downstream task and the denoising step, challenging uniform preci-sion allocation. Building on this observation, we introduced T 2Quant, a task- and time-aware mixed-precision framework that predicts precision impor-tance using unlabeled calibration prompts and al-locates activation precision under a fixed bit-width budget. Across three DLMs and six benchmarks, T2Quant consistently improves average accuracy when integrated with existing quantization meth-ods, with gains of up to 6.7 percentage points under W4A4. These results highlight the importance of considering both task requirements and temporal generation dynamics when quantizing DLMs. By improving accuracy under fixed precision budgets, T 2Quant offers a promising direction for making DLM deployment more computationally efficient and accessible, potentially reducing mem-ory and energy requirements. Its compatibility with existing quantization methods may further facili-tate practical adoption. However, quantization can also alter model behavior beyond benchmark ac-curacy, including safety-related responses (Hong et al., 2024; Egashira et al., 2024). Future work should therefore examine the effects of task- and time-aware precision allocation on safety, robust-ness, and real-world inference efficiency. 8Limitations 

Although T 2Quant demonstrates strong results across three DLMs, six benchmarks, and three quantization methods, further evaluation of deployment-related aspects, including hardware ef-ficiency (inference speed and energy consumption) and potential impacts on model safety, remains for future work.

E Experimental Setup and Implementation Details 

E.1 Model, Sampler, and Quantization 

Unless stated otherwise, all experiments use LLaDA-8B-Instruct with 512-token generations over 128 denoising steps in a single 512-token block, so four positions are committed at each step. We use the standard low-confidence remask-ing sampler (Nie et al., 2025) with temperature 0 and no classifier-free guidance, so decoding is de-terministic. End-of-sequence handling follows the official LLaDA evaluation settings for each bench-mark. Rotations follow QuaRot (Ashkboos et al., 2024): the residual stream is rotated by a random Hadamard matrix, and online Hadamard transforms are applied before the attention output and MLP down projections. Weights of the transformer lin-ear layers are quantized to 4 bits with GPTQ (Fran-tar et al., 2023) (per output channel, asymmetric, MSE-optimal clipping, act-order), calibrated on 128 WikiText (Merity et al., 2016) sequences of 512 tokens with random masking. The embeddings and language-model head remain in high precision. Activations are quantized dynamically per token, with a clipping ratio of 0.7, at the inputs to the QKV projection, the attention output projection, the MLP up/gate projections, and the MLP down projection. Attention scores and softmax remain unquantized. We refer to the configuration with 4-bit weights and 4-bit activations as W4A4. Sampler. Every row uses the models’ upstream sampler unchanged: 4 tokens are unmasked per step with low-confidence remasking, at tempera-ture 0, with the output lengths of Table 5. Rows therefore differ only in the quantization applied to the network. 

Quantization setup. Quantization is simulated (the quantized integer values are used in bf16 arith-metic), the standard protocol for post-training quan-tization studies (Ashkboos et al., 2024; Frantar et al., 2023). Weights are quantized to 4 bits per channel with GPTQ after a Hadamard rotation of the hidden dimension (QuaRot-style, including the online rotation of the attention-output input). Acti-vations, the inputs of all linear layers, are quantized dynamically per token; for widths below 8 bits the per-token range is clipped to 70% of its maximum. This rotated, GPTQ-quantized W4 model with per-token A4 activations is the shared base of every quantized row except RTN, which uses round-to-nearest 4-bit per-channel weights and per-token A4 activations without rotation or GPTQ. T 2Quant keeps the base and varies only the activation width along the denoising trajectory: the trajectory is di-vided into 16 windows, each window uses one of {3, 4, 6, 8} bits, and the allocation is chosen on the calibration pool under an exact average of 4.00 bits (the method section gives the allocation). The random schedule assigns window widths at random under the same 4.00-bit average; one fixed draw (seed 0) is used for every model and benchmark. 

Baselines. DLLMQuant (Xu and Yang, 2025), FAIR-Calib (Huang et al., 2026) and STaR-Quant (Yan et al., 2026) are reimplemented on the common rotated W4 base, so that ev-ery row shares the same weights, sampler and prompts and only the activation treatment differs: DLLMQuant’s calibration over sampled denoising states, FAIR-Calib’s calibration prior, and STaR-Quant’s training-free state-aware channel scaling with its attention correction are each calibrated on denoising states produced by the full-precision model from the calibration prompts. “Base + T2Quant” applies our window allocation on top of the baseline’s calibration, which is left unchanged. 18
