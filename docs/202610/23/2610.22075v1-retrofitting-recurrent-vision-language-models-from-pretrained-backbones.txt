## ABSTRACT 

Recent work shows that depth recurrence can be retrofitted into pretrained lan-guage models. In this work, we adapt this paradigm for visual understanding, using depth recurrence to reduce the parameter footprint of a pretrained vision-language model (VLM). To this end, we prune layers from the language back-bone of the VLM and loop over a subset of the remaining layers. At each step of the loop, a linear adapter merges the block’s output with the token represen-tations from the preceding layers and projects the fused representation back into the block. As the pruning impacts the original model performance, we propose a three-stage recovery training: first, we leverage a language-only pretraining to recover the performance of the language backbone, followed by an alignment of the vision-language projector, and a full fine-tuning of the model via visual in-struction tuning. In the first and the last stage, we distill the outputs of the pre-trained non-recurrent model into the recurrent model, which further improves the recovery. We evaluate the proposed approach on the TinyLLaVA-1.4B backbone without introducing any new vision-language data during retrofitting. Our exper-iments demonstrate that the retrofitted recurrent model outperforms the unpruned baseline trained with the same recovery data while storing 23.3% fewer language parameters. We further show that the proposed retrofitting recurrence outperforms the setup of converting a recurrent LM into a VLM on the same data. Additionally, our ablations show how design choices, such as the adapter between recurrences and a larger recurrent block, lead to the improved performance of our approach. Finally, we find that our proposed model gains on reasoning-oriented benchmarks, whereas its performance on basic visual understanding benchmarks remains on par with the baseline. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Existing approaches for test-time compute scaling in vision-language models comprise chain-of-thought traces, latent reasoning tokens, searching over candidate answers, and test-time reinforcement learning (Zhang et al., 2024b; Qin et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2025; Singh et al., 2025; Wan et al., 2026). These methods usually come with higher inference costs as each generated token requires a full forward pass and expands the context window. To address this limitation, 

depth recurrence (Geiping et al., 2025; McLeish et al., 2025) has been explored as an alternative for test-time scaling in language models. Depth recurrence allocates test-time compute to iterating a block of layers rather than generating tokens, thereby maintaining smaller context sizes and a smaller memory overhead independent of reasoning length. But depth recurrence has also gained traction in visual understanding. Here, recurrent VLM archi-tectures are built in two ways: first, either by starting from a recurrent language model and post-hoc adding a vision backbone, or, second, by looping an existing VLM without removing any of its layers. The first approach is i.a. adopted by HIVE (Zhang et al., 2026), which extends a recurrent language model pretrained from scratch by a vision backbone. Examples for the second approach are, RecursiveVLM (Xu et al., 2026) and ReLoop-UME (Wang et al., 2026), which loop layers in pretrained VLMs without pruning, with a near-constant number of parameters. 1arXiv:2610.22075v1 [cs.CV] 15 Oct 2026 Preprint. In contrast to these two regimes, we pursue a third option of retrofitting a pretrained VLM with recurrence, following a prune-then-loop strategy. Namely, we remove some intermediate blocks of the language backbone of the VLM and loop over a subset of the remaining layers. This strategy is supported by two complementary research findings: first, one set of recent works shows that a fraction of a VLM’s layers can be removed at a limited cost in accuracy (Huang et al., 2026; Madinei et al., 2025; Ma et al., 2025), and, second, another set of works shows that the layers of a pretrained model can be skipped or repeated at inference (Li et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026). We thus hypothesize that iteratively executing the subset of layers of a pruned VLM can recover the capacity lost due to pruning. We test this hypothesis by retrofitting , i.e., by converting a pretrained non-recurrent VLM into a recurrent one while reusing its existing weights rather than pretraining a recurrent model from scratch. We refer to the resulting model as retrofitted recurrent VLM (RR-VLM). We prune a block of intermediate layers from the language backbone of a pretrained VLM and designate a contiguous subset of the remaining layers as a recurrent block (McLeish et al., 2025). To feed the block’s output back into its input for successive iterations, we concatenate the output with the tokens from the layers preceding the recurrent block and train a linear layer to compress this concatenated representation to its original dimensionality (McLeish et al., 2025). An overview of the process is given in Figure 1. This surgery, however, degrades the pretrained language backbone, and although text-only training repairs it, the repair leaves the language model misaligned with the original projector. We resolve this with a three-stage training curriculum: first, we apply a text-only training to repair the backbone, second, we apply a projector-only training to restore the image-text alignment, and third, we fine-tune the entire model on visual instruction data. In the text-only and the instruction-tuning stage, we add a knowledge distillation (KD) term, with the pretrained model as teacher, to avoid the language backbone from drifting too far from the original representation that the projector was aligned with. We evaluate the proposed method on the TinyLLaVA-1.4B backbone (Zhou et al., 2024), training it on 60B tokens of text as well as 493k image–caption pairs and 660k instruction tuning samples from the base model’s training data, without introducing any new vision-language data. We compare the resulting model against the pretrained TinyLLaVA-1.4B model and four baselines trained on the same data: (1) the unpruned model subjected to the identical recovery training, (2) the unpruned model without the text-only stage, (3) the pruned model without recurrence, and (4) a recurrent lan-guage model that has been extended to a VLM. Our experiments show that our proposed retrofitted recurrent model outperforms the unpruned baseline while having 23 .3% fewer language-model pa-rameters and show that this gain is due to the recurrence rather than the recovery training. The gain concentrates on reasoning-oriented benchmarks, whereas performance on basic visual under-standing benchmarks remains on par with the baseline. Compared to a recurrent language model extended to a VLM, our retrofitted model achieves a higher average accuracy. We further ablate each component of the retrofit: the training stages, the layer allocation, the knowledge distillation term, KV-cache sharing at inference and the adapter. We find that the text-only stage S0 is neces-2Preprint. Under review. sary for the recovery, as removing it costs 3.68 pp, primarily on the reasoning-oriented benchmarks. Specifically, we evaluate our linear adapter against three variants: a gated adapter, the Parcae adapter (Prairie et al., 2026), which multiplies the recurrent state with a learned per-channel factor within 

(0 , 1) so that the hidden state remains bounded across recurrences, and no adapter at all. Our abla-tions show that, among the adapter variants, the linear adapter yields the best performance, whereas the gated adapter and the Parcae adapter score below the variant without an adapter. Furthermore, we show that at equal compute, a larger recurrent block results in better performance. We also show that distillation from the pretrained model improves recovery training and increases the performance of the retrofitted recurrent model. Finally, we find that a model trained at a fixed depth only works at the depth it was trained at, whereas a model trained with sampled recurrence works at other depths, but its performance does not improve beyond the trained depth. We also find that sharing the KV cache across recurrences during inference costs accuracy, which contrasts with the results reported for a model pretrained with recurrence (Geiping et al., 2025). We summarize our contributions as follows: (1) We introduce the first prune-then-loop retrofit of a pretrained VLM evaluated on VQA, supported by a three-stage curriculum that repairs the pruned language backbone and restores projector alignment using the base model’s vision-language training data. (2) We demonstrate that retrofitting recurrence directly into a pretrained VLM outperforms the alternative of adding a vision encoder to a pretrained recurrent language model. (3) We perform an extensive evaluation of the retrofitted recurrent VLM and quantify the impact of each design decision. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Depth recurrence and architectural retrofitting. Depth recurrence reuses a block of layers mul-tiple times, increasing the effective depth of the model without adding any additional parameters (Dehghani et al., 2019). Previous attempts at creating recurrent LMs include Huginn (Geiping et al., 2025) and Ouro (Zhu et al., 2025). Huginn splits the model into three components: a prelude that embeds the input into a latent state, a recurrent block that iterates on that state, and a coda that de-codes the final state into output logits. Ouro iterates the entire set of layers, and Loopie (Gao et al., 2026) applies each layer recurrently instead of performing block-level looping. In all three cases, the model is trained from scratch without any pre-initialization by existing models. Two findings in-form our design: a looped block benefits from a stable learned connector between successive passes (Prairie et al., 2026), and an iterated layer contributes only a fraction of the capacity of a stored one (Schwethelm et al., 2026), thus bounding what recurrence can recover from pruning. As an alternative to full-corpus pretraining, recurrence can instead be achieved by retrofitting a pretrained model. Within this regime, there exist two lines of work: (1) looping a middle block of the stack without pruning, for latent reasoning (Koishekenov et al., 2025), and (2) looping after reducing the depth, either by keeping a block of the surviving layers (Bae et al., 2025; McLeish et al., 2025) or by merging neighbouring layers into a few blocks that are then looped (Kwon & Park, 2026). We adopt the latter approach, retrofitting recurrence after structural pruning. Specifically, we take inspiration from McLeish et al. (2025), who drop layers 4 to 9 of TinyLlama-1.1B to obtain a 4/8/4

layer allocation looped through a linear adapter. 

Recurrence in vision-language models. Current works either loop layers of a pretrained VLM without pruning, with a near-constant number of parameters (Xu et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026), or build one on a natively recurrent language backbone (Zhang et al., 2026). HIVE (Zhang et al., 2026) builds a VLM on the pretrained recurrent language model Huginn-3.5B (Geiping et al., 2025) by attaching an InternViT-300M vision encoder and training the combined model on 6.5M training samples in three stages. As such HIVE is based on a significantly larger model and trained on five times more training samples than the proposed model, preventing a direct comparison to those checkpoints. We therefore take inspiration from the pipeline in HIVE to replicate this setting as a baseline to compare to rather than comparing against their model. Beyond standard vision-language tasks, depth recurrence has been explored for robotic action policies, either in the action head of an intact VLM (Tur et al., 2026) or by pruning a VLM and looping a small block (Shen et al., 2026). However, these methods operate in a fundamentally different domain, and the pruned variant of Shen et al. (2026) lacks a prune-only baseline. Our setting departs from those existing approaches by following a prune-then-loop pattern, removing layers before the loop is installed. To the best of our knowledge, this work presents the first prune-then-loop retrofit of a pretrained VLM evaluated on VQA. 3Preprint. Under review.

KV cache. Each pass through the recurrent block computes its own keys and values, so the KV cache holds mr entries per token compared to the m entries for the original model. We evaluate two ways of sharing the cache across passes. The first, from Geiping et al. (2025), keeps n copies of the block’s cache per token and lets pass i use copy i mod n. With n=1 , every pass reads the entries that the last pass wrote, and the cache shrinks to p + nb + c entries per token. This variant changes only the inference procedure and requires no training. The second lets every pass reuse the keys, or the keys and values, that the first pass computed, so that the cache shrinks to p + b + c entries per token. The retrofitted model of Sec. 3.1 requires training for two reasons. First, the pruning removes the layers between the prelude and the recurrent block, leading to a misalignment between the repre-sentations produced by the prelude and those expected by the recurrent block. Additionally, the remaining layers have to be adapted to the modified architecture. Second, the recurrence introduces an adapter A that fuses the output of the prelude with the output of the recurrent block using a newly initialized linear layer that needs to be trained as well. 

Three-stage Training. We propose a three-stage curriculum that restores the VLM and trains the adapter: First, in stage S0 we focus on repairing by training only the pruned language backbone on text-only data, freezing all other components. Second, as the retrained text backbone can now slightly deviate from the original one, we retrain the vision-language projector in stage S1 to re-store image-text alignment, and finally optimize the entire model via end-to-end training with visual instruction data in stage S2, interleaving image-text and text-only training data. 

Knowledge Distillation. Retraining the modified language backbone inevitably shifts its internal representations away from those of the pretrained model. In a VLM this shift is problematic, since the vision-language projector has been aligned to the representations of the original backbone. We therefore seek to adapt the language backbone to the new architecture while preserving its original representations as far as possible. To this end, we incorporate a distillation term at stage S0 training, using the original, unpruned baseline model F as the teacher to limit the drift of the pruned, recurrent model F r and to allow an easier realignment in stage S1:

L = (1 − λ) LCE + λ τ 2 KL   softmax( F/τ ) softmax( F r /τ ). (4) The distillation objective evaluates a single pass of the teacher against the iterative execution of the student. Note that in stage S0, this is done on text-only training data. We found that such knowledge distillation can further improve performance during stage S2 training, as this combines the vision and language input signal from the teacher.

## 5 EVALUATION 

Benchmarks. We evaluate the models on the following nine benchmarks: GQA (Hudson & Man-ning, 2019), POPE (Li et al., 2023b), TextVQA (TVQA) (Singh et al., 2019), and ScienceQA-IMG (SQA) (Lu et al., 2022), MMBench (MMB) (Liu et al., 2024), RealWorldQA (RWQA) (xAI, 2024), SEED-Bench (SEED) (Li et al., 2023a), AI2D (Kembhavi et al., 2016), and the 2D split of CV-Bench (CV2D) (Tong et al., 2024). We use the VLMEvalKit (Duan et al., 2024) library for all model evaluations. We define the Avg metric as the arithmetic mean of the nine VQA benchmarks. We set a fixed eval seed to make evaluation deterministic. 5.1 COMPARISON TO BASELINES 

Baselines. We evaluate the proposed recurrence retrofitting approach against five baselines and report the results in Table 1. The first baseline is the pretrained TinyLLaVA-1.4B checkpoint without any modifications. The second and third baselines fine-tune this model without pruning, either with the image-text stages S1 and S2 only or with all three stages S0, S1 and S2 of our training strategy. The fourth baseline applies all three stages to the pruned model without recurrence, which corresponds to the single-pass model F 1 of Sec. 3.1. The baselines with the text-only stage apply the distillation term of Eq. 4 in S0, and the unpruned Baseline+ (S0, S 1, S 2) also applies it in S2, so that it is trained with the full strategy of our model. We include those retrained versions to allow a direct comparison between the retrofitted recurrent model and the same non-recurrent backbones trained on the same data. To allow a comparison to a setup that starts from a recurrent language model and adds the vision backbone after recurrent training, we convert a pretrained recurrent TinyLlama checkpoint of McLeish et al. (2025) into a recurrent VLM by adding the pretrained image encoder from TinyLLaVA-1.4B and train the model with the same image-text data on the training stages S1

and S2. For our retrofitted recurrent VLM (RR-VLM), we use a fixed recurrence of r=6 and report results for an 8-layer recurrent block architecture ( F 64/8/4). We see that the training improves the baseline models by around 2.4 to 2.9 pp, while the performance of the pruned model is slightly below that of the base model. The performance of the converted recurrent VLM is in the same range as the retrained baselines. Compared to that, the proposed RR-VLM model is able to outperform all baseline models on average by at least 2.3 pp. Analyzing the performance per dataset, we see that more basic visual understanding datasets, such as GQA, POPE, TVQA and SQA, do not profit as much from the recurrent architecture, and that the RR-VLM performs on par with the baseline model. This can be attributed to the fact that those datasets usually have single-step QA type questions, which do not profit from more complex reasoning. Recurrence adds computation without adding information, so no gain is expected on such questions. Compared to that, we see a clear trend of performance increase on more reasoning-oriented datasets such as MMBench, RealWorldQA, SEED-Bench, AI2D and CV2D. Note that on the latter four datasets, both recurrent models, the converted VLM as well as the proposed RR-VLM model, per-form consistently better than the non-recurrent baselines, with RR-VLM still outperforming the post hoc converted VLM on three of the four datasets. We thus find that retrofitting a pretrained VLM results in better performance than introducing a vision encoder into a recurrent language model to create a recurrent VLM. Training stages. We determine the effect of the text-only stage S0 and the vision-language align-ment stage S1 on the resulting recurrent VLM in Table 2. We find that S0 is crucial to the recovery training, as removing it reduces the performance of the resulting model by 3.68 pp on average. Re-moving S0 primarily affects the reasoning-oriented datasets, with 7.3 pp lost on CV2D, 6.8 pp on MMB and 4.6 pp on AI2D. We attribute this to the fact that without the repair of the pruned language backbone in S0, the stages S1 and S2 have to repair the language backbone and align the projector at the same time. We further find that removing S1 costs 1.28 pp, again primarily on the reasoning-oriented datasets. We attribute this to the fact that S1 performs the vision-language alignment, and without it the stage S2 has to perform both the alignment and the instruction tuning. In summary, both S0 and S1 contribute to the performance, with S0 having the larger impact, which shows that the text-only recovery is necessary for retrofitting recurrence into a pruned VLM. 

Layer allocation. We first ablate the design of the recurrent block in Table 3(a). To this end, we fix the depth of the recurrent model at mr =56 layers, thus smaller recurrent blocks are executed with more recurrences compared to larger ones. All models are trained on a 7.5B S 0 text-token budget. We find that a larger recurrent block performs better than other variants given the same compute setting. Namely, the 4/8/4 allocation outperforms a 6/4/6 allocation at r=11 by 3.19

59 .7 at r=6 to 43 .1 at r=5 and 50 .9 at r=8 , and the output degenerates at all other depths. In contrast, the model trained with a sampled recurrence regime also works at depths other than the one it was trained for, staying within 2 pp of its best value from r=2 to r=16 . This can be attributed to the fact that the sampled regime exposes the model to a different number of recurrences in every training step, so that its readout is trained for every depth in that range. However, we find that its performance saturates and does not improve beyond the trained depth. Consequently, we do not observe test-time scaling. The fixed and sampled recurrence models perform on par at the trained depth, with 55 .75 against 55 .83 on Avg. We therefore find that a fixed recurrence model is the less expensive approach to train a recurrent VLM, but it comes at the price of test-time stability to varying recurrences. ## 6 CONCLUSION 

We introduced the first prune-then-loop retrofit of a pretrained VLM evaluated on VQA. The retrofitted model outperforms the unpruned baseline while storing 23.3% fewer language-model parameters, with the gains concentrated on reasoning-oriented benchmarks. This gain is due to the recurrence itself, as the loop accounts for a significant improvement over the pruned single-pass baseline. At the same time, the recovery requires the text-only training stage, which yields the best performance among all evaluated training strategies. Finally, retrofitting recurrence into a pretrained VLM outperforms adding a vision encoder to a pretrained recurrent language model, which makes the retrofit the best performing route to a recurrent VLM among all evaluated baselines. 9Preprint.
