# ABSTRACT 

Low-bit quantization reduces the memory footprint of large language models, but preserving accuracy with efficient training remains challenging. Scalar quantiza-tion encodes weights independently, while explicit-codebook vector quantization introduces costly search during training. We propose E8-QAT, a 2-bit quantization-aware training framework that uses the E8 lattice as an implicit eight-dimensional codebook. It jointly quantizes weight groups through arithmetic projection without storing or searching an explicit codebook during the training forward pass. A 2-bit core encoding and sparse extension preserve the selected lattice points exactly, adding less than 0.1% to core index storage. On Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, E8-QAT matches the performance of the full-precision model in terms of the average score across the four tasks. E8-QAT improves over the state-of-the-art baseline by 2.03% on IFEval and by 1.51% on LiveCodeBench, under the same data, schedule, and bit width. The 2-bit representation reduces peak memory by 80% and supports a 

4× larger batch. At each representation’s largest feasible batch, E8-QAT reaches 

1.97 × the decoding throughput of BF16. 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Large language models (LLMs) have made substantial progress across language tasks (Wu et al., 2023), but growing demands for privacy-preserving, offline, and cost-efficient deployment have made running them on resource-constrained platforms a pressing need (Yao et al., 2024; Liagkou et al., 2024). Weight quantization addresses this by reducing model size (Lin et al., 2024). Post-training quantization (PTQ) incurs low computational cost but can suffer accuracy degradation at ultra-low bit widths (Frantar et al., 2023). Quantization-aware training (QAT) recovers accuracy by allowing the model to adapt to quantization errors through weight updates, but training requires substantial data, compute, and memory (Jacob et al., 2018; Chen et al., 2025). This motivates quantization schemes that achieve strong accuracy at ultra-low bit widths without prohibitive training overhead. Existing QAT approaches primarily rely on scalar quantization, which assigns quantization levels to each weight coordinate independently (Liu et al., 2025). While scalar quantization supports efficient per-coordinate arithmetic, it limits the flexibility of codeword arrangements across dimensions. Vector quantization (VQ) addresses this by jointly encoding groups of weights through a shared codebook (Gray, 1984), allowing richer codeword arrangements that better capture cross-dimensional weight structure and reduce quantization distortion at a given bit budget (Egiazarian et al., 2024; Tseng et al., 2024). Recent work has therefore explored integrating vector quantization into quantization-aware training (VQ-QAT) (Malinovskii et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2026a). However, conventional VQ-QAT requires an explicit codebook that must be stored and searched at every training step , posing two challenges at scale: (1) nearest-codeword search during the training forward pass introduces substantial overhead, and (2) codebook size grows exponentially with vector dimension at a fixed bit width, making high-dimensional VQ impractical for both training and inference. To address these challenges, we propose E8-QAT , a QAT framework that uses the E8 lattice (Conway & Sloane, 1982) as an implicit eight-dimensional codebook, as illustrated in Figure 1. During the training forward pass, each eight-dimensional weight group is projected onto the E8 lattice by 1arXiv:2610.27699v1 [cs.AI] 20 Oct 2026 rounding, parity correction, and a constant number of candidate comparisons, reducing the per-group computational cost from O(v 2kv ) for exhaustive codebook search to O(v), independent of the nominal codebook size, where k and v denote the bit width and vector dimension, respectively. Exploiting the observed concentration of quantized weight groups within the finite core, E8-QAT combines a 2-bit core encoding with a sparse extension that, at very low additional memory cost, exactly preserves the lattice points selected during training with an identity straight-through estimator (STE) (Bengio et al., 2013). Our main contributions are as follows: • Search-free joint quantization for QAT. We jointly quantize each group of eight weights by closed-form projection onto the infinite E8 lattice in the QAT forward pass. The projection requires O(v) operations per group, independent of the nominal codebook size, without codebook storage, search, or learning. • Exact export beyond the finite core. Lattice projections during QAT can fall outside the finite core, and clipping these points at export would alter the trained quantized weights. We combine a dense core index with sparse extension records to preserve every trained lattice point exactly. At the end of training, the sparse records add less than 0.1% to the 2-bit index payload. • Matched-budget 2-bit evaluation on agentic and long-form tasks. We evaluate the perfor-mance and efficiency of E8-QAT on Qwen3.6-35B-A3B (Qwen Team, 2026) and Llama-3.1-8B (Grattafiori et al., 2024). On Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, E8-QAT improves over LC-QAT by 2.03% on IFEval and by 1.51% on LiveCodeBench, under the same training corpus, schedule, and bit budget. Its four-task average is 73.93, equal to the reported BF16 average. Relative to BF16, the exported model reduces peak memory usage by 80%, enabling a 4× larger batch size. Decoding throughput reaches 1.97 × that of BF16 when each representation is evaluated at its largest feasible batch size. 

# 2 RELATED WORK 

2.1 LOW -BIT WEIGHT QUANTIZATION 

Representative PTQ methods include GPTQ (Frantar et al., 2023) and AWQ (Lin et al., 2024), which use calibration data to guide scalar quantization of individual weight coordinates. Other PTQ methods use VQ to jointly encode groups of weights. QuIP# (Tseng et al., 2024) uses predefined codebooks, while AQLM (Egiazarian et al., 2024) represents each weight group as a sum of codewords from learned codebooks. QAT methods such as EfficientQAT (Chen et al., 2025) and ParetoQ (Liu et al., 2025) incorporate scalar quantization into training, allowing model weights to adapt to quantization errors. 2.2 VECTOR QUANTIZATION -AWARE TRAINING 

Recent work has also explored VQ-QAT, with PV-Tuning and LC-QAT adopting different strategies for updating quantized weights. PV-Tuning optimizes both continuous parameters and discrete codeword assignments. For learned VQ codebooks, it combines gradient-based updates of codeword values with discrete assignment updates for selected weight groups. These assignment updates require nearest-codeword search, or beam search when multiple additive codebooks are used, introducing additional computational cost during training (Malinovskii et al., 2024). LC-QAT avoids explicit codebook lookup in the training forward pass through an affine mapping of discrete vectors (Wang et al., 2026a). However, it rounds and clips each coordinate independently before applying a fixed scale and shift (AI9Stars, 2026). The resulting quantizer retains the per-coordinate structure of scalar quantization, limiting the flexibility of codeword arrangements across dimensions. Consequently, these VQ-QAT designs face a trade-off between training efficiency and quantization accuracy. They either rely on costly codeword search to determine the discrete assignments, or give up the joint encoding that makes VQ accurate. E8-QAT addresses this challenge by using the

# 3 PRELIMINARIES 

At a fixed bit budget, quantization error depends on both the distribution of the weights and the geometry of the codebook. We first explain how Hadamard preprocessing redistributes weight magnitudes, then introduce vector quantization as a way to represent the transformed weights jointly. These considerations motivate the use of the E8 lattice, whose geometric properties and efficient nearest-point projection provide the mathematical basis for E8-QAT. 3.1 HADAMARD PREPROCESSING 

Randomized Hadamard preprocessing mixes weight coordinates before quantization (Tseng et al., 2024). Within a transform block, it maps a weight vector w to 

ew = HDw , H⊤H = I, (1) where D applies independent random sign flips and H is a normalized Hadamard matrix. Each output coordinate is a normalized sum of signed weights from the same block, while the transform preserves the sum of squared weights. The central limit theorem (Durrett, 2019) shows that when many independent signed weights con-tribute to each output coordinate and no single weight dominates, the resulting marginal distribution approximates a Gaussian distribution. To verify this, we sampled 14 weight tensors from a pre-quantized BF16 model and observed that the transformed marginal kurtosis is about 3.02 , close to the Gaussian value 3.Under the zero-mean isotropic Gaussian approximation, the probability density depends only on the distance from the origin and decreases with that distance. We observed that the radial probability distribution of the rotated weights is geometrically similar to a finite E8 codebook (Tseng et al., 2024), which can improve lattice utilization and representation accuracy in vector quantization. 33.2 VECTOR QUANTIZATION 

After Hadamard preprocessing and scaling, we partition each row of a weight matrix into non-overlapping groups of d consecutive weights along the input dimension. Let x ∈ Rd denote one such group. Given a finite codebook C ⊂ Rd with K codewords, vector quantization assigns the entire group to its nearest codeword:

Unlike scalar quantization, VQ is not restricted to a rectangular grid and can better match the distribution of weight groups. This flexibility can reduce quantization error at the same index budget (Gray & Neuhoff, 1998). However, exhaustive nearest-codeword search costs O(dK ) operations per group, with K = 2 kd 

growing exponentially in the group dimension at a fixed per-weight bit budget. This motivates structured point sets whose nearest points can be computed directly rather than found by exhaustive search. 3.3 E8 LATTICE QUANTIZATION 

The E8 lattice is a classical eight-dimensional construction from sphere-packing and lattice-quantization theory (Conway & Sloane, 1999), not a codebook learned from model weights. Its points have either all integer or all half-integer coordinates, with an even coordinate sum: 

E8 = z ∈ Z8 ∪  Z8 + 12 1 : 1⊤z ∈ 2Z , (3) where 1 is the all-ones vector. Its geometry and algebraic structure offer three useful properties. 

E8 combines favorable covering geometry with an algebraic structure that enables efficient exact quantization. At equal cell volume, its smaller covering radius than Z8 provides a tighter worst-case error bound throughout the infinite lattice (Conway & Sloane, 1999), while its structure permits exact nearest-point projection without exhaustive codebook search (Conway & Sloane, 1982). This combination makes E8 attractive for QAT, where weights are repeatedly projected as they evolve during training. We exploit these properties by using an approximately ball-shaped core Ccore ⊂ E8

of 216 low-norm points for compact encoding, while retaining the full lattice as the training-time projection space. This design pairs 2-bit-per-weight indices for core points with the flexibility to project beyond the core boundary; the following section describes how these core-external projections are encoded and their additional cost is accounted for.

Weight reconstruction. The decoded groups in Equation 4 are assembled into W ∈ Rm×n. The effective weight matrix is reconstructed as 

cW = diag( sV ) Hout W Hin diag( sU ), (5) where sU ∈ Rn and sV ∈ Rm are trainable input- and output-channel scaling vectors, respectively. The matrices Hin and Hout are fixed orthogonal block-Hadamard transforms with block size 128 .For an input a ∈ Rn, the layer computes y = cWa .

Finite-core construction. The finite core Ccore consists of the first 216 points of E8 ordered by increasing squared norm, with ties resolved lexicographically. It includes all 56 ,881 points with squared norm at most 10 and the first 8,655 points in lexicographic order from the shell at squared norm 12 . Initialization, export, and decoding use the same point set and index ordering. Since the outermost shell is only partially retained, a norm threshold does not suffice for escape detection; a projected point is classified as an escape if and only if it lies outside Ccore .

Numerical precision. Latent weights are stored in BF16, while projection candidates and squared distances are evaluated in FP32. Gradients through the hard projection use an identity straight-through estimator. Hadamard transforms, affine decoding, and channel scaling lie outside the projector and use ordinary backpropagation.

Comparison with 2-bit methods. To evaluate whether the lattice codebook yields the strongest 2-bit model at a matched bit budget, we compare E8-QAT against the PTQ and QAT baselines on Qwen3.6-35B-A3B. As shown in Table 1, E8-QAT achieves the best quantized score on ARC-Challenge, HellaSwag, and WinoGrande, and on LiveCodeBench and IFEval. Compared with LC-QAT, which shares the same data, schedule, and bit width, E8-QAT improves accuracy by 1.51 percentage points on LiveCodeBench and by 2.03 percentage points on IFEval. On the six-task average, E8-QAT scores 76.95, against 75.97 for LC-QAT. On Llama-3.1-8B, its WikiText perplexity is 13.77, against 16.73 for LC-QAT.Appendix C.4 gives ARC-Easy, BoolQ, and the perplexity for every Llama row. These results demonstrate that replacing the scalar grid with the 

E8 lattice improves both base-model quality and agentic capability at 2 bits. 

Generalization to dense models. To examine whether this advantage transfers across architectures, we further evaluate E8-QAT on the dense Llama-3.1-8B. As shown in Table 5.1, E8-QAT again achieves the best quantized score on all four tasks, outperforming LC-QAT by 2.99% on average. These results confirm that the lattice advantage transfers across architectures once the learning rate is matched in lattice space. 

Inference efficiency. We compare the serving capacity of E8-QAT and BF16 to validate that 2-bit storage allows a single GPU to serve larger batches As shown in Figure 3, E8-QAT reduces peak memory by 80% relative to BF16, supports a 4× larger batch, and achieves 97% higher throughput at each model’s largest feasible batch. These results indicate that the lattice model nearly doubles serving throughput on the same hardware. 

Most accurate initialization. At equal bits, the lattice should reconstruct weights more accurately than a scalar grid or a learned codebook. We measure the layer-wise output distortion , which weighted by routed-token frequency, with the same Hessians for all methods. As shown in Figure 4, E8-PTQ has the lowest distortion in 39 of the 40 layers. Averaged over layers, it is 20.4% below UniSVQ, which runs the same LDLQ pipeline with a scalar grid, and 19.4% below AQLM. These results confirm that the lattice provides the most accurate starting point at matched bits. 

Gains from second-stage QAT. Reconstruction-based PTQ is expected to preserve short-form ability but not long-form generation, which QAT should recover. To verify this, Figure 5 compares each pipeline before and after the 750-step second stage. QAT improves LiveCodeBench by 24.36% and GPQA by 17.67% for E8-QAT. E8-QAT is ahead of the LC-QAT pipeline both before and after this stage. # 6 CONCLUSION 

In this work, we introduce E8-QAT, a QAT framework designed to address the trade-off between training efficiency and quantization accuracy at ultra-low bit widths. By using the E8 lattice as an implicit eight-dimensional codebook, E8-QAT enables joint weight quantization through arithmetic projection without storing or searching an explicit codebook during the training forward pass. An identity STE supports weight updates during training. To preserve the lattice points selected during training, we combine a 2-bit core encoding with a sparse extension, enabling exact encoding at very low additional memory cost in our experiments. Experimental results on Qwen3.6-35B-A3B and Llama-3.1-8B show that E8-QAT achieves higher average accuracy than LC-QATon the four zero-shot benchmarks. # B SPARSE ENCODING AND DECODING VERIFICATION 

Coordinate records. Each eight-weight group retains a 16-bit core index. An escaped group additionally stores its 32-bit tensor-local position and doubled lattice coordinates, 

vg = 2 q⋆g ∈ Z8. (12) Every E8 point has all-integer or all-half-integer coordinates, so vg is integral. Eight signed 16-bit coordinates and one 32-bit position occupy 20 bytes per record. During decoding, the record supplies the absolute lattice coordinates and overrides the fallback codeword, so the exported point is the lattice point selected in training. Export checks that every position fits the selected index representation and that the stored coordinates satisfy 

−215 ≤ (vg )j ≤ 215 − 1, j = 1 , . . . , 8.

Conditional root packing. A smaller record is available when an escaped point differs from a selected core fallback by one E8 root. Define 

R = r ∈ E8 : ∥r∥22 = 2 , |R| = 240 . (13) When the residual belongs to R, decoding becomes 

q⋆g = cig + rjg , rjg ∈ R . (14) An 8-bit root identifier and a 24-bit position within a chunk form a 4-byte record, excluding chunk metadata. All 26 ,435 escaped groups in the 375-step artifact satisfy this condition. Payloads reported in the paper use the 20-byte coordinate record, including the 750-step endpoint. 

Fused decoding. For the corresponding group of input-side transformed activations hg , linearity gives

cz is applied once. The corrected weight matrix is never materialized.
