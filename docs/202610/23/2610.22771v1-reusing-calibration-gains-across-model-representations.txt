# Reusing Calibration Gains across Model Representations 

Xu Cao and Wenxuan Xiao 

Astrmira Tech. x.cao@astrmira.com, w.xiao@astrmira.com 

Abstract 

Scale calibration learns an update for one model representation. We study how much of that update can be reused by another representation of the same pretrained model. Our transfer procedure extracts positive group-wise gains and installs them in the recipient’s weights or native quantization scales without recipient-side optimization. On a quantized recipient, it preserves integer codes, zero points and storage layout. For a Qwen3-1.7B floating-point/GPTQ INT3 pair, bidirectional transfer retains 76.9–90.5% of the text-loss improvement from recipient-specific calibration on fresh WikiText and C4 cohorts excluded from project development. Shuffling the same gain values within each projection worsens both recipients, showing that their learned group assignment matters after transfer. A Llama-3.2-3B control keeps the initial native tensors at all four calibrated projections identical and changes nine projections elsewhere. Cross-installation retains 98.6–99.3% of the text-calibration benefit, alongside a small, resolved preference for each recipient’s own gain. These results quantify both the adaptation carried by a reusable calibration update and the remaining benefit of recipient-specific training. 

# 1 Introduction 

Calibrating a quantized language model can improve its predictions while leaving its integer codes fixed. Group-wise scales provide a compact trainable interface for this purpose, supporting downstream fine-tuning [1] and end-to-end quantization refinement [2]. Once calibration is complete, the learned change can be retained separately from the final checkpoint. Reusing that change would give another precision or quantization configuration of the same pretrained model an adapted starting point without repeating calibration. Aligned weight groups can respond differently to the same gain. Scaling a quantized group changes different weights from scaling its floating-point counterpart. Even identical local weights can operate inside different surrounding networks. A learned update may therefore carry adaptation useful across representations while fitting its training model more closely. We distinguish these outcomes by installing the same update in complete recipients and comparing it with the gain learned on each recipient. We use dimensionless, group-wise gains as that shared description. On a floating-point model, a gain multiplies a weight group. On an affine-quantized model, the same multiplication can be absorbed into the group’s native scale. A quantized donor supplies its gain through the ratio of final to initial scales, while a floating-point donor supplies the multipliers saved during calibration. This construction moves the learned change without replacing the recipient’s original representation. It produces a calibrated recipient with no further optimizer steps and no additional inference-time operation. We evaluate transfer in two crossed experiments, with a common training recipe and data stream for the donors within each experiment. On a Qwen3-1.7B floating-point/INT3 pair, installed gains retain 76.9–90.5% of the negative log-likelihood (NLL) reduction from self-calibration on fresh cohorts excluded from project development. Earlier development evaluations give a similar range of 78.0– 90.2%. Both recipients obtain an additional text benefit from their own gains. # 2 Transferring a calibration update 

2.1 A common multiplicative interface 

Consider representations of the same pretrained model with aligned projection names and weight-group coordinates. We first describe transfer between a floating-point model F and a quantized model Q; the same scale operation applies between two quantized configurations. Let W F 

> 0,g

denote an original floating-point weight group. A positive gain ag produces the calibrated group 

W Fg = agW F

> 0,g

.

For the corresponding quantized group, integer codes cg, zero point zg and positive scale sg decode to 

W Qg = sg(cg − zg).

Multiplying the scale by ag gives (agsg)( cg − zg) = agW Qg .

The same gain therefore acts on both representations through their existing parameters. This is the scale parameterization used in quantization-based adaptation [1, 2]. Its role here is to provide a transferable update: the recipient keeps its own base weights, and the donor specifies how strongly to scale each aligned group. Alignment refers to the original weight coordinates, not merely to matching tensor dimensions. In our GPTQ representation, each group spans 128 consecutive original input columns in one output row. The group mapping retained by activation-ordered quantization is respected during installation. All parameters outside the selected groups remain those of the recipient. Exact absorption into one recipient scale requires a common gain across the nonzero decoded weights controlled by that scale. A coarser or differently arranged recipient grouping therefore requires a compatibility check beyond matching tensor shapes. 

2.2 Extracting and installing the gain 

The floating-point calibration path parameterizes gains as exp( θ) and stores their final values. We denote the saved gain vector by aF . For a quantized donor, we extract the gain from its original and calibrated native scales: 

aQ = sQ, final /s Q, 0.

The ratio removes the donor’s initial scale and retains its learned relative change. It is defined even when some decoded weights are zero, unlike a ratio of individual final and initial weights. For two 2Calibration Gain Transfer 

quantized configurations with identical initial scales at the selected groups, transferring the relative gain reduces to copying the donor’s final scales into the recipient. The recipient still supplies the rest of the model. Transfer then consists of two possible installations: 

F ← Q : W Fg = aQ,g W F

> 0,g

, Q ← F : sQg = aF,g sQ

> 0,g

.

We promote the stored FP32 operands to FP64 for construction and round each installed result to FP32 once. Applying each extracted gain to its own original base reconstructs the saved calibrated endpoint exactly in our implementation. This self-installation check ensures that the transferred object is the update actually learned by the donor. Numerical details are given in Appendix A. The resulting procedure takes an original recipient, a calibrated donor and the donor’s original scales or saved gains. It aligns the groups, extracts the relative update and multiplies it into the recipient’s weights or scales. The output is a materialized recipient model. For Q, the code tensors, zero points, group layout and scale slots are retained. The installed model requires no separate gain application during inference. Installation uses O(G) scale arithmetic for G quantized groups, or O(N ) multiplications to materialize N selected floating-point weights. The Qwen gain vector has 393,216 entries, occupying 1.5 MiB in FP32 before serialization metadata; the Llama vector has 786,432 entries. Reuse presupposes an existing donor and also incurs model-loading and file-I/O costs. The quantized output occupies the recipient’s existing scale slots. Appendix F separates CPU arithmetic, identity checks and serialization for construction of the four-projection update payloads. 

# 3 Evaluation design 

3.1 Cross-precision donors 

We use the public Qwen3-1.7B checkpoint [4, 14], which includes pretraining and post-training, as F and its native activation-ordered, static-group GPTQ INT3 quantization [5] as Q. Both calibration paths update the down projections in layers 6, 13, 20 and 27, the endpoints of four equal-depth partitions. Group size 128 gives 393,216 trainable log-gains. The other 192 projections and all embedding, normalization, bias and output-head parameters retain their recipient-specific original values. Q’s integer codes and zero points are fixed throughout training and transfer. The donors use the same 600-update recipe. Each update combines eight ARC items [9], one WikiText block [6] and one C4 block [7]. The loss is 

L = λALARC + λW LWiki + λC LC4 ,

where the ARC term is the Kullback-Leibler divergence DKL (pF 0∥pθ), from the original floating-point teacher’s option distribution to the calibrated model’s distribution, and the text terms are next-token losses. ARC uses a plain-text question-and-options prompt followed by a bare option label. The saved labels are single tokens without a leading space; their conditional probabilities are renormalized over the valid options, with temperature 1 and no length normalization. Appendix A.4 gives the exact scoring rule and a prompt example for both model families. The loss weights are 0.080624, 0.406788 and 0.512588, respectively. They were determined on the quantized path and reused on F. Both paths use Adam [10], initially zero moments, learning rate 0.01 for 200 updates and a linear decrease to 0.001 over the next 400. 3.2 Separating transfer benefit from source preference 

Each complete recipient is evaluated without calibration, with its own gain and with the other donor’s gain. The two transferred states are fixed before evaluation. The crossed study adds no training or checkpoint selection. Let L(B, a ) be the loss of recipient B with installed gain a, and let a = 1 denote the uncalibrated model. Transfer benefit is measured by L(Q, a F ) − L(Q, 1) and L(F, a Q) − L(F, 1). To compare gain sources while holding the recipient fixed, we use 

dQ = L(Q, a Q) − L(Q, a F ), dF = L(F, a Q) − L(F, a F ).

Both differences use the convention Q-source minus F-source. Thus dQ < 0 and dF > 0 indicate that each recipient favors its own gain. We also report the interaction I = dQ − dF , which measures how the source effect changes between recipients. The two direct differences determine which source each recipient favors. To express the practical size of transfer, we divide its NLL reduction by the reduction from self-calibration on the same recipient. For Q, this retained-benefit ratio is 

rQ = L(Q, 1) − L(Q, a F )

L(Q, 1) − L(Q, a Q) ,

with the analogous definition for F. This is a descriptive ratio of observed mean losses. Paired loss differences, rather than the ratios, provide the statistical comparisons. 

3.3 Controlling the local starting representation 

The Qwen comparison changes both local weights and the surrounding network. To isolate the latter, we use two existing Llama-3.2-3B [11] INT3/g128 configurations, denoted S and J. Each of their 196 projections selects a complete native encoding from GPTQ with 1% damping, round-to-nearest quantization, or GPTQ with 0.1% damping. The configurations differ at nine projections, all outside the four calibrated down projections in layers 6, 13, 20 and 27. Those four projections have identical initial codes, scales and zero points: GPTQ at layers 6, 20 and 27, and round-to-nearest at layer 13. Both donors calibrate the same 786,432 scales for 200 Adam updates, using a common data stream, objective and learning rate. The saved gains are installed in both complete recipients. Because their local initial scales agree, cross-installation copies the donor’s saved FP32 scales exactly. The other 192 projections remain those of the recipient. The four states therefore vary the surrounding configuration and gain source while holding the local starting representation fixed. For each recipient we report cross-gain loss minus own-gain loss. Positive differences on both rows establish an own-gain preference separately for S and J. Their sum measures the matching interaction. This experiment examines context dependence between quantized configurations; the Qwen experiment measures bidirectional transfer across precision.

3.5 Testing group-level correspondence 

We test whether transferred gains depend on their correspondence with the recipient’s weight groups. For each of the four Qwen projections, we flatten the 98,304 row-group gains and apply one permutation drawn with PCG64 and seed 20261014. Both donors use the same permutation at corresponding projections. This intervention preserves each projection’s exact gain multiset, parameter count and log-gain norm while changing which group receives each value. The resulting weight-displacement norm can change because the recipient groups have different weights. In each transfer direction, the aligned and shuffled 600-update gains are installed and evaluated together on the same GPU, using the REPLAY development text set. The contrast is shuffled minus aligned NLL, paired over the same units as Section 3.4. The permutation is fixed before scoring, with no training or checkpoint selection. Its paired intervals describe evaluation-unit variation for this fixed intervention, rather than variation across permutation seeds.

The selection rule and token arrays are fixed before model evaluation. WikiText contributes 256 blocks from 139 articles, excluding recorded project-consumed articles and the official validation and test articles by title and normalized content. Eligible articles are ordered by a salted hash, and complete blocks are retained within articles until the fixed total is reached. # 5 Related work 

Retaining an adaptation separately from its base model is useful beyond quantization. Task Arithmetic represents fine-tuning by an additive weight difference that can be combined or moved in weight space [12]. IA3 learns vectors that scale activations for parameter-efficient adaptation [13]. Our update is a positive, dimensionless gain on aligned weight groups, extracted from saved calibration and absorbed into each recipient’s own weights or native scales. Its coordinate system makes the same relative operation available on floating-point and integer representations; the crossed experiments measure how much of its benefit survives that change of recipient. Scale-based adaptation makes quantization parameters trainable without replacing the integer rep-resentation. PEQA adapts compressed models by learning their scales [1]. EfficientQAT combines block-wise training with end-to-end optimization of quantization step sizes [2]. These methods motivate the calibration interface used here. We study a different operation on its output: extracting a learned relative gain and installing it on another aligned precision version. GPTQ provides our quantized base [5], whose codes, zero points and group layout are held fixed during both calibration and transfer. Reuse across quantization configurations is also the goal of configuration-aware adaptation. CoA-LoRA predicts low-rank adjustments for different per-layer configurations through a trained configuration-aware model [3]. Direct gain transfer uses a fixed update and the recipient’s existing weights or scales, without training a configuration-to-update mapping. Our crossed comparisons measure the benefit and recipient dependence of that direct operation. The Llama control additionally keeps the adapted projections’ initial tensors identical, separating surrounding-model dependence from a change in those local starting tensors. 10 Calibration Gain Transfer 

# 6 Discussion 

When a calibrated donor is available, direct transfer provides an adapted recipient without repeating optimization. The quantized recipient keeps its existing encoding format and inference operations. In the Qwen comparison, transfer recovers most of the self-calibration text benefit, while recipient-specific calibration yields a further improvement. Reuse still requires model loading and installation; Appendix F separates those construction costs from training and research evaluation. The controls distinguish two requirements for reuse. Group alignment makes installation well defined, and the permutation experiment shows that the donor’s assignment of gain values to groups matters for the transferred benefit. The surrounding model affects which gain fits best, as the Llama control demonstrates with identical local starting tensors. Compatibility can therefore be checked at the coordinate level for installation, but the resulting quality must be measured on the complete recipient. Update placement and the evaluation interface also affect calibration outcomes. At Qwen’s 200-update checkpoints, removing one layer’s gain gives higher text loss than shrinking all four gains to the same total log-gain norm (Appendix C). The 200-to-600 checkpoint comparison changes direction across ARC label and chat readouts (Appendix D). These observations, together with the PIQA results, motivate evaluating a reused gain on the recipient’s intended task. The evidence covers one Qwen floating-point/GPTQ pair and one Llama pair of quantized config-urations, each with four calibrated projections and fixed training trajectories. The matching gaps compare saved gains learned under a common recipe. They measure the extra benefit of those recipient-specific gains, without estimating the best calibration achievable on each recipient. 

# 7 Conclusion 

Group-wise gains let a learned calibration be installed in another aligned representation without recipient-side training. On fresh Qwen3-1.7B text cohorts, bidirectional transfer retains 76.9–90.5% of the self-calibration benefit. The permutation control shows that the benefit depends on the learned group assignment, and the Llama control shows that gain preference depends on the surrounding model even when local starting tensors agree. Saving the gains separately provides a reusable update that fits the recipient’s existing parameterization.

# A Implementation and calibration details 

A.1 Native installation 

The installer reads the actual saved FP32 gains and native scales. For the quantized donor, both scale tensors are promoted to FP64 before forming the ratio. For the floating-point donor, the saved FP32 gain is promoted directly. Multiplication uses FP64, followed by one FP32 cast of each installed weight or scale. The implementation checks scale positivity and finiteness, preserves codes and zero points, and matches original input-column groups. Self-installation reproduces the saved source endpoints, including their unchanged model context. The decoding identity in Section 2 holds over real numbers. The stated construction specifies the numerical realization used in this study. The recipient’s complete context is retained: installing a gain into F leaves all unmodified projections floating-point, while installing into Q leaves them quantized. Group shape alone is insufficient to establish compatibility when a quantizer uses a different coordinate mapping. A common gain also need not produce the same weight displacement on the two bases. For one aligned group and scalar gain a, ∆WF = ( a − 1) WF, 0, ∆WQ = ( a − 1) WQ, 0,

and hence ∆WQ − ∆WF = ( a − 1)( WQ, 0 − WF, 0).

This identity describes the difference in the local weight updates. The complete models also differ in their surrounding layers. Source preference in Section 4 is measured on those complete recipients. The 393,216-entry gain vector contains 1,572,864 bytes in FP32. Storing the extracted ratios in FP64 uses 3 MiB, excluding serialization metadata. Once installed on Q, the values occupy the original scale slots. The crossed study’s formal runtime was 759.211 seconds, including new evaluations and surrounding work. That figure is research execution cost, not an installation-latency benchmark.
