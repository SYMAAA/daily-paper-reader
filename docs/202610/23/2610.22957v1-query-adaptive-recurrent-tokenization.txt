## ABSTRACT 

Visual information worth extracting from an image and the token budget it war-rants depend on the text query. Yet modern vision and vision-language models largely encode images independently of the query and allocate a fixed visual token budget to every input. We introduce the Query-Adaptive Recurrent Tok-enizer (QART), a backbone-agnostic module that makes visual representations both query-steerable and budget-adaptive . QART steers the visual representation toward query-relevant evidence while adapting its token budget to the amount of information required. Its key principle is simple. Additional tokens are allocated until the visual features relevant to the query are represented with sufficient fi-delity. Starting from frozen encoder features, QART recurrently constructs a com-pact representation and uses reconstruction quality over the query-relevant visual support to control token allocation. On Visual7W and VQAv2 , QART achieves 

81.96% and 76.37% accuracy using only about 29 visual tokens, reducing token counts by 20–25 × relative to dense VLM inputs. It further reaches 92.0% P@1 on CORE and 36.3% PR-AUC on PODS, outperforming SteerViT on PODS by 8.4%. Together, these results show that visual representations need not be fixed in either content or capacity and can instead adapt to what each query requires. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

“A wealth of information creates a poverty of attention and a need to allocate that attention efficiently among the overabundance of information sources that might consume it.” 

— Herbert A. Simon (1971) An image contains evidence for many possible questions, and the visual information that matters depends on what is being asked Anderson et al. (2018); Hudson & Manning (2019); Pantazopoulos & ¨ Ozyi˘ git (2025). In the same street scene, identifying the color of a traffic light requires evidence from a small region, whereas counting vehicles turning left requires information distributed across objects and their spatial relationships (Acharya et al., 2018; Rajabi & Koˇ seck´ a, 2024; Ishihara et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2026; Sadeghi et al., 2026). Yet SOTA vision encoders (Tschannen et al., 2025; Radford et al., 2021; Oquab et al., 2023; He et al., 2022) produce the same representation of an image regardless of the query (Figure 1(a)). These representations capture broadly useful visual information but cannot explicitly prioritize the requested evidence (Ruthardt et al., 2026). More importantly, visual salience is not akin to query relevance: a prominent object may be irrelevant to the question, while a small detail may determine the answer. A useful visual representation should therefore be steerable : changing the query should change which visual information the representa-tion emphasizes. A separate limitation concerns how much representational capacity is allocated to that evidence. Visual tokenizers commonly produce a fixed-size sequence; ViT-based encoders (Oquab et al., 2023; Tschannen et al., 2025) provides a fixed set of patch tokens, while the Q-Former uses a fixed number of latent tokens regardless of the image or the evidence required by the downstream task (Li et al., 2023). These considerations expose two distinct axes of visual tokenization: which evidence to preserve and how much capacity to allocate ( adaptivity ), as shown in Fig. 1. These properties are increasingly relevant to embodied systems, where agents repeatedly interpret visual observations under changing goals, and efficient processing matters over long interaction horizons (Saxena et al., 2025; Cheng et al., 2024). We seek a tokenizer that connects both axes, allocating capacity according to how well the current representation preserves query-relevant visual evidence. Prior work addresses parts of this problem. Learned resamplers create compact latent representa-tions, steerable encoders condition visual features on language (Ruthardt et al., 2026), and token-pruning methods remove redundant or query-irrelevant patches (Li et al., 2026a; Takezoe et al., 2026). Adaptive image tokenizers additionally vary their sequence length according to image com-plexity (Duggal et al., 2025b; Yu et al., 2024). What remains less explored is an explicit mechanism that couples these choices: rather than prescribing a token count or predicting one directly, can a tokenizer continue allocating capacity until the evidence relevant to the current query has been represented with sufficient fidelity? We introduce the Query-Adaptive Recurrent Tokenizer (QART), a visual tokenizer that converts dense encoder features into a variable-length set of query-conditioned visual tokens. Given frozen image features and a text query, a cross-modal relevance module first identifies the visual evidence relevant to the query. QART begins with a small set of tokens and recurrently refines them using this evidence and the query. After each round, a shared decoder reconstructs the relevant visual features, and reconstruction quality determines whether more tokens are added or computation halts. The resulting tokens form a compact representation whose content and capacity are both shaped by the query, and can be used by a downstream task head or language model. We evaluate QART on prompt-conditioned retrieval (CORE (Ruthardt et al., 2026), GeneCIS (Vaze et al., 2023), PODS (Sundaram et al., 2025)), compositional matching (Winoground (Thrush et al., 2022)), Visual Question Answering (VQAv2 (Goyal et al., 2017)), and grounding (Visual7W point-ing (Zhu et al., 2016). QART learns non-uniform allocations and compresses dense patch tokens by 25 ×. On CORE and GeneCIS, it improves over the query-agnostic SigLIP2 representation by 23.5% and 2.7% respectively. On Visual7W pointing, it outperforms the strongest token-reduction baseline DART Wen et al. (2025) by 22.1% under a matched protocol; on VQAv2, it retains 93.8% of dense accuracy while reducing tokens by 25 × and outperforms a matched 32-token Q-Former, 2while improving end-to-end throughput by 1.53 ×. Together, these results highlight the significance of query-adaptive visual representations. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

2.1 COMPACT AND VARIABLE -L ENGTH VISUAL TOKENIZATION 

Vision encoders commonly represent an image as a dense grid of patch embeddings, motivating learned bottlenecks that summarize this grid with a smaller set of visual tokens (Bachmann et al., 2025). TokenLearner learns a compact set of content-adaptive tokens (Ryoo et al., 2021), while BLIP-2 uses a Querying Transformer (Q-Former) to map frozen image features into a fixed-size latent sequence for a downstream language model (Li et al., 2023). InstructBLIP further conditions the Q-Former on the input instruction, but retains a fixed latent budget (Dai et al., 2023). Matryoshka Query Transformer (MQT) supports multiple output lengths within one model by training nested prefixes of latent queries; however, the desired length is selected externally at inference time rather than inferred from the visual evidence required by each query (Hu et al., 2024). Adaptive-length image tokenizers vary representation capacity with image content (Yan et al., 2024; Duggal et al., 2025a; Chen et al., 2026; Lu et al., 2026). ALIT, the closest architectural precedent to our work, recurrently updates a latent sequence, adds tokens over successive rounds, and uses recon-struction as a learning signal for variable-length image representations (Duggal et al., 2025b). QART changes the unit of adaptation from the image alone to the image–query pair. Its text-conditioned relevance model determines which patch features must be preserved, and the recurrent tokenizer halts when those query-relevant features have been reconstructed sufficiently well. Consequently, the same image can produce different token contents and lengths under different queries. 2.2 QUERY -C ONDITIONED AND STEERABLE VISUAL REPRESENTATIONS 

Other work studies how language can steer visual representation (Mao et al., 2025). InstructBLIP ex-tracts instruction-specific features through an instruction-aware Q-Former (Dai et al., 2023); FLAIR applies text-conditioned attention pooling to local image features for fine-grained retrieval (Xiao et al., 2025); and TIE conditions the image encoder on the text query (Thirukovalluru et al., 2025). SteerViT injects language into intermediate vision-transformer layers to produce dense visual fea-tures that can be directed toward a requested concept (Ruthardt et al., 2026). QMoP uses the query to route visual features among pooling, resampling, and pruning branches (Li et al., 2026b). These methods establish that useful visual evidence depends on the language context; QART is therefore not the first query-conditioned visual representation. Its distinction lies in coupling query conditioning to recurrent capacity allocation. Rather than only changing which information is em-phasized within a dense or prescribed-size representation, QART jointly determines what informa-tion to encode and how many latent tokens are required. The token count follows from an explicit representation-adequacy criterion instead of a fixed budget or a separately predicted length. 2.3 VISUAL TOKEN PRUNING AND MERGING 

Visual-token reduction is also widely studied through pruning and merging (Chen et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025b; Yang et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025a; Shang et al., 2025). ToMe reduces vision-transformer sequences by progressively merging similar tokens (Bolya et al., 2023). In vision-language systems, QG-VTC uses question–patch relevance to progressively compress visual tokens within the vision encoder (Li et al., 2025), while QuietPrune introduces query guidance early in the vision transformer so that pruning preserves prompt-relevant regions (Gao et al., 2026). ATP-LLaVA learns instance- and layer-dependent pruning ratios inside the language model (Ye et al., 2025); LearnPruner combines learned post-encoder reduction with query-guided pruning in intermediate language-model layers (Takezoe et al., 2026); and OccamToken derives image- and query-adaptive pruning thresholds without additional training (Li et al., 2026a). These approaches primarily retain, merge, or discard existing tokens. QART instead constructs and recurrently expands a slot-based representation from frozen patch features until a query-conditioned reconstruction criterion is met, which supplies a shorter visual sequence to downstream models. 3Encoding  Relevance  Recurrent tokenizer  — weights shared across rounds

## 3 METHOD 

QART converts dense encoder features into a variable-length sequence of query-conditioned visual tokens in three stages (Figure 2). A cross-modal relevance module selects query-relevant visual features (§3.1); a weight-shared recurrent Transformer then builds a slot-based representation from them, beginning with K0 query-initialized slots and adding ∆K new ones when reconstruction coverage of the selected features falls below a coverage criterion (§3.2–3.3). The trainable compo-nents are optimized jointly using query-weighted reconstruction and relevance-prediction objectives (§3.4). 3.1 IDENTIFYING QUERY -R ELEVANT EVIDENCE

## 4 EXPERIMENTS 

Our experiments probe the central properties of QART rather than performance on individual bench-marks alone. We ask whether query conditioning can steer a frozen visual representation toward the requested content, whether the resulting compact representation preserves downstream reasoning, whether its behavior genuinely depends on the query, whether it retains information beyond what the query explicitly specifies, and whether its compactness translates into practical inference gains. 4.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Unless stated otherwise, QART uses frozen SigLIP2 vision and text encoders and produces at most 32 visual tokens from 729 patch features. A single pretrained QART checkpoint is used across the representation benchmarks without task-specific finetuning. For VQA, QART replaces the visual to-kens of LLaVA-1.5-7B while both QART and the language model remain frozen; only a lightweight visual projector is trained. (d) On PODS, coarse conditioning preserves fine-grained instance information beyond the prompt. 4.2 DOES QART R EDIRECT A PRETRAINED VISUAL REPRESENTATION ?We first ask whether QART changes what a frozen visual representation encodes. If query condi-tioning is effective, the resulting representation should emphasize the visual evidence requested by the query rather than simply inherit the dominant semantics of the pretrained backbone. 

Controlled evaluation on CORE. CORE Ruthardt et al. (2026) 1 directly tests this behavior by re-quiring retrieval according to a queried object that may occupy only a small part of the image. QART reaches 92 .0% P@1, compared with 68 .5% for the native SigLIP2 global representation (Fig. 3a). Because both representations originate from the same frozen vision encoder, this improvement iso-lates the effect of query-conditioned representation construction. QART therefore does more than compress the underlying patch features; it changes which visual evidence dominates the represen-tation. QART also outperforms open-vocabulary localization methods like Grounding-DINO (Liu et al., 2024) and SteerViT (Ruthardt et al., 2026) showcasing the benefit of query-relevant adaptive representations. 

Does the same behavior transfer to natural images? We next test whether this steering behav-ior transfers beyond the controlled CORE setting. On GeneCIS Focus Object, QART uses the same pretrained checkpoint without GeneCIS-specific tuning. It improves over native SigLIP2 represen-tations by 2.7, 5.0, and 7.1% at R@1, R@2, and R@3, respectively (Fig. 3b). The gain increases with retrieval depth, suggesting that query conditioning consistently promotes condition-compatible images throughout the ranked list rather than affecting only the top prediction. QART also improves over simple Image + Text composition at every rank and approaches Combiner, despite Combiner being designed specifically for composed image-text retrieval. 

Insight QART can steer a frozen visual representation toward query-relevant content, and this be-havior transfers from controlled scenes to natural images without task-specific adaptation.

Replacing the dense visual tokens of LLaVA-1.5-7B+SigLIP2 with QART tokens, QART reaches 76.37% acc., retaining 93.8% of dense accuracy (81.4%) with 24.8× fewer tokens, and outperforms a matched 32-token Q-Former by 1.7%. Grounded reasoning on Visual7W. We next test whether this compact representation preserves the spatially localized evidence required for grounding in Tab. 1b. We specifically focus on the point-ing task as it requires selecting the box that answers a question, so evidence at specific locations must be preserved. Under a matched pipeline (App. A.3), QART reaches 81.96% with 29 tokens, retain-ing 92.6% of dense model’s accuracy and outperforming the strongest baseline, DART, by 22.1%. This larger gap indicates that aggressive compression is more demanding when predictions depend on localized visual evidence. Nevertheless, QART retains substantially more grounding information than existing low-token pruning methods. This suggests that query-conditioned token construction preserves the spatial evidence needed to associate the query with relevant image regions. 

Does recurrence refine the representation? To isolate recurrent refinement from token growth, we evaluate the same QART checkpoint while fixing the representation to two slots throughout recurrence, such that no additional slots are added. VQAv2 accuracy increases from 46 .27% after one round to 50 .93% after three rounds and remains around that number thereafter (Fig. 4a). Since both the query-relevant support and token count remain fixed, this improvement shows that recurrent updates can refine the representation independently of increasing its capacity. Additional qualitative results are provided in App. C.2.

A representation may become highly focused on the prompt while discarding visual detail that the prompt does not explicitly mention. We test this on PODS, where QART receives only a coarse supercategory prompt, such as “mug” or “shoe”, but must distinguish a particular object from visually similar instances of the same category. QART reaches 36 .3% PR-AUC and 61 .3% NDCG, improving PR-AUC by 8.4% over SteerViT under the same supercategory prompting setting and by 6.7% over unconditioned DINOv2 (Fig. 3d). The query itself cannot identify the correct instance. QART must therefore use it to determine which region or semantic content is relevant while still preserving visual cues that are more specific than the language supervision. The improvement on PODS shows that steering does not collapse the representation to only what is explicitly stated in the prompt. Instead, the query determines where representational capacity is focused while fine-grained visual information within that region is retained. 4.6 DOES COMPACTNESS TRANSLATE INTO REAL INFERENCE GAINS ?A shorter visual sequence is useful only if the reduction offsets the cost of constructing it. We therefore measure end-to-end VLM inference as batch size increases. At batch size one, QART’s recurrent tokenization introduces more overhead than is recovered by the shorter language-model sequence, resulting in a 0.87 × end-to-end speed ratio. The balance changes as batch size increases and processing the visual sequence inside the language model becomes more expensive. At batch size four, QART reaches 1.14 × end-to-end and 1.84 × prefill speed-up. We ablate these two roles on CORE, and illustrate the results in Tab. 2. Making the budget decisions query-agnostic has almost no effect on retrieval accuracy, but removes the computational adaptivity of QART. The model reaches 92 .08% P@1, yet activates all 32 slots and runs all six rounds. Using query-relevant evidence for growth and halting preserves essentially the same accuracy while reducing the average budget to 17 .25 slots and 4.25 rounds. This corre-sponds to roughly 46% fewer active tokens and 29% fewer recurrent rounds. The query-relevant support itself plays a different role. When the relevance threshold τ is set to zero, all patches participate rather than focusing recurrence on the queried evidence. When there is no query-conditioning for the visual support, P@1 drops to 79 .7% , still outperforming the SigLIP2 number. Thus, query conditioning of the visual support is important for steering the representation, whereas query conditioning of the reconstruction coverage enables efficient allocation of capacity. 

Insight Query conditioning serves two complementary roles in QART. It enhances steering of the representation toward the requested visual evidence, while query-aware growth and halting avoid spending capacity on information that the query does not require. 

## 5 CONCLUSION 

We introduced QART, a recurrent visual tokenizer that combines query steerability with budget adaptivity. QART steers its representation toward query-relevant evidence and allocates additional visual tokens only when that evidence is not yet represented with sufficient fidelity. Across retrieval, grounding, compositional matching, and visual question answering, QART produces compact rep-resentations with substantially fewer tokens while preserving strong downstream performance. Its gains are strongest when predictions depend on localized visual evidence, while on general VQA it remains competitive with existing token-reduction methods. Overall, these results show that visual representations need not be fixed in either content or capacity, and can instead be shaped by what each query requires. 9AI U SE STATEMENT

## A IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS 

A.1 DATASETS 

Tokenizer pretraining data. We pretrain QART on a mixture of referring-expression, scene-understanding, and region-description datasets comprising RefCOCO Yu et al. (2016), RefCOCO+ Yu et al. (2016), RefCOCOg Yu et al. (2016), gRefCOCO Liu et al. (2023a), Mapillary Vistas Neuhold et al. (2017), and Visual Genome (VG) Krishna et al. (2017). The complete mixture con-tains 2,078 ,367 image–text annotation records; this number counts annotations rather than unique images. Table 3 summarizes the sampling distribution. Training examples are sampled with replace-ment according to the specified mixture weights. 

Query-relevance supervision. During tokenizer pretraining, we use dense query-region targets to supervise the relevance predictor, following Ruthardt et al. (Ruthardt et al., 2026). For the COCO-family referring-expression datasets (Yu et al., 2016), relevance targets are derived from segmenta-tion annotations. For Mapillary Vistas (Neuhold et al., 2017) and Visual Genome (Krishna et al., 2017), we use cached dense query-region alignment maps. The resulting heatmaps are retained as soft targets in [0 , 1] rather than being converted to binary bounding boxes. These targets provide training supervision for the relevance predictor and the query-weighted reconstruction objective, but are not used to define the visual support or the recurrent growth and halting decisions. The recurrent tokenizer instead operates on the support predicted by the relevance module, A = {i | pi > τ },both during training and inference. 

Vision–language alignment data. For integration with LLaVA-1.5-7B Liu et al. (2023b), we train the visual projector in two stages. The first stage performs one epoch of Visual Genome-based alignment using native-image VG region descriptions. The region phrase conditions QART, while the language model receives a generic region-description instruction and predicts the corresponding phrase. The second stage trains the projector for one epoch on the full LLaVA-Instruct-665K mixture Liu et al. (2023b). QART and the language model remain frozen throughout both stages, and only the visual projector is optimized. A.2 SETUP 

Backbone and input representations. Our main configuration uses the frozen vision and text encoders from google/siglip2-so400m-patch14-384 . Images are processed at 384 ×384 

resolution. The visual encoder produces a 27 × 27 grid of N = 729 patch features, each with dimension 1152 . We precompute and cache these frozen visual features in FP16. A bias-free linear layer projects the visual features to the tokenizer dimension D = 768 .15 The frozen text encoder provides token-level query representations, which are projected to the same 

768 -dimensional space when necessary. Padding tokens are masked, and a masked mean of the query features is used to initialize the slot bank. Tokenizer pretraining uses a maximum text length of 32 tokens. The reconstruction target is the projected visual feature map used by the tokenizer. Specifically, the frozen backbone features are first projected to the tokenizer dimension, and the decoder reconstructs these projected visual features rather than RGB pixels. 

Relevance predictor. The relevance predictor consists of five visual-to-text cross-attention layers with eight attention heads. Visual patches act as queries and text tokens as keys and values. Each layer uses pre-layer normalization (Nguyen & Salazar, 2019) and a residual connection without an intermediate feed-forward block. A final linear projection followed by a sigmoid predicts one relevance score pi ∈ [0 , 1] for each visual patch. Queries and keys are normalized independently within each attention head after their learned pro-jections. At inference, the query-relevant visual support is defined as

Training uses a global batch size of 256, BF16 mixed precision, and global-norm gradient clipping at 0.5.The training objective combines query-weighted feature reconstruction and relevance prediction as described in Sec. 3.4. We use λrec = 1 and λrel = 0 .5. The implementation additionally includes a slot-count term with weight 0.01 . Because slot allocation is discrete, this term does not propagate gradients through the allocation decisions.

## C ADDITIONAL RESULTS AND ANALYSES FOR QART 

C.1 THRESHOLD SENSITIVITY AND ADAPTIVE BUDGETING 

The relevance threshold τ controls the spatial support selected for recurrent tokenization. Chang-ing this threshold therefore affects both which image regions are represented and the amount of representational capacity subsequently allocated by QART. We analyze this interaction on CORE, GeneCIS, and Winoground using the same SigLIP2 QART model. Table 5 reports the primary metric for each task together with the resulting mean active-slot count when available. For CORE, we report P@1; for GeneCIS, R@1; and for Winoground, Group accu-racy. The optimal operating point differs across tasks. CORE performs best at τ = 0 .25 , whereas GeneCIS reaches its highest R@1 at τ = 0 .20 . Winoground also favors an intermediate thresh-old rather than either extreme. More importantly, increasing the selected visual support and the resulting token budget does not guarantee improved performance. On GeneCIS, lowering the threshold from 0.20 to 0.05 increases the mean slot count from 25 .38 to 30 .04 , yet R@1 decreases from 15 .10% to 12 .81% . A similar pattern appears on Winoground, where τ = 0 .10 uses 31 .07 slots on average but obtains lower Group accuracy than τ = 0 .25 , which uses only 27 .49 slots. These results highlight that QART’s budget should not be interpreted as a simple “more tokens is better” trade-off. The relevance threshold jointly controls the spatial extent and composition of the selected evidence, while the recurrent tokenizer allocates capacity to represent that evidence. Different tasks therefore favor different combinations of visual coverage and token budget. 20 Insight Effective visual representations require adapting both spatial support and representational capacity. 

Broader coverage generally increases the available token budget, but additional tokens do not mono-tonically improve downstream performance. The task-dependent optima suggest that a single fixed visual budget or uniformly holistic representation is unlikely to be optimal across different visual reasoning settings. C.2 DOES RECURRENCE REFINE THE REPRESENTATION ?QART does not construct its representation in a single step. The active slots are repeatedly refined using the same query and visual evidence, allowing their spatial support to change across recurrent rounds. Figure 5 shows representative examples of this behavior. The progression is particularly clear when the initial representation attends to a nearby distractor or only coarsely identifies the relevant region. For the query “person in foreground with backpack,” the early support is spread over nearby foreground content before progressively concentrating on the queried person and backpack. A similar shift occurs for “guy in plaid,” where the support moves toward the queried individual over successive rounds. For “number 13,” the representation initially responds to nearby players before becoming localized around the jersey number of the target player. These examples suggest that recurrence provides more than additional representational capacity. Later rounds can revise where the active slots attend, progressively moving their support toward evidence that is better aligned with the query. This is useful when the relevant region is visually ambiguous or surrounded by similar content, where a single tokenization step may commit too early to an incorrect or overly broad region. 

Insight Recurrence allows QART to revise, rather than simply expand, its representation. Across rounds, slot support can move from coarse or distracting regions toward the queried visual evi-dence, providing a mechanism for iterative query-conditioned refinement. C.3 IS QART QUERY -STEERABLE AND BUDGET -ADAPTIVE ?Figure 6 qualitatively illustrates how the conditioning query changes the visual representation. For the same input image, QART shifts its support from the left pizza to the right pizza when the query changes. Importantly, the representation does not only move spatially: the active token budget also changes from K = 8 to K = 20 . This provides a direct example of query steerability and budget adaptivity within the same image. The allocated capacity also varies substantially across different image–query pairs. Figure 7 shows representative examples spanning K = 2 to the full K = 32 slot budget. Localized targets such as “zebra on far right” and “top right bowl” require only a small representation, whereas other queries receive substantially more capacity. Queries involving broader or spatially distributed evidence can activate most or all of the available slots. The token count is not determined by the number of queried objects alone. Instead, it reflects the visual evidence that QART needs to preserve for a particular image–query pair, including its spatial extent, appearance, and surrounding context. Together, Figs. 6 and 7 show that QART adapts both the content and the capacity of its representation rather than applying a fixed visual budget to every query.

The current formulation also uses a small number of thresholds to control relevance selection, slot growth, and halting. As shown in our sensitivity analysis, the preferred operating point can vary across tasks. Exploring fully learned or calibration-free allocation criteria could further simplify deployment across heterogeneous downstream settings.
