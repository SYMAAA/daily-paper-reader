Abstract 

Mixture-of-experts (MoE) language models ac-tivate only a few experts per token, yet all ex-pert weights must be stored. Mixed-precision quantization reduces this storage cost by assign-ing higher bit-widths to sensitive experts and lower bit-widths to resilient ones, but expert rankings alone do not determine how strongly to weight their errors. To address this gap, we propose NatScale, a contrast-calibrated alloca-tion framework for mixed-precision MoE quan-tization. It uses a model-specific power trans-formation to calibrate gradient-based expert weights to an information-theoretic contrast target while preserving rankings. We jointly evaluate each expert’s gate, up and down con-figurations to estimate its output error. We then combine these errors and calibrated weights with an existing prior based on weight spectra, variance and precision decay to select config-urations under a storage budget. Across three models, NatScale achieves the lowest native-to-quantized KL divergence and the highest top-token agreement in all nine settings against five allocation baselines. It also yields the lowest perplexity in seven settings. These findings highlight the importance of objective-aware precision allocation. 

1 Introduction 

Sparse Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) language mod-els decouple parameter count from computational cost by routing each token to a small subset of ex-perts (Jiang et al., 2024; DeepSeek-AI et al., 2024). Although this routing limits per-token computation, all expert parameters must still be stored. Mixed-precision quantization offers an effective way to compress these models: under a strict memory budget, it allocates higher precision to sensitive weights and lower precision to robust ones. Prior work uses gradients or curvature informa-tion to estimate how quantization errors affect the model’s loss, providing a basis for sensitivity-aware quantization (Kim et al., 2024, 2025). Choosing precisions under this budget requires two ingredi-ents: estimating the quantization error of candidate configurations and evaluating the model’s sensitiv-ity to those errors. Existing techniques rank experts by importance using diverse heuristics, including routing statistics and reconstruction errors (Huang et al., 2025a), block sensitivity combined with acti-vations (Duanmu et al., 2025), dynamic importance updates during quantization (Deng et al., 2026), and weight spectra priors (Yang et al., 2026). While these signals establish expert rankings, a ranking alone does not determine memory alloca-tion: it indicates which experts are more impor-tant, but not how strongly to weight their errors. Consider two sensitivity score vectors: (2 , 1) and 

(100 , 1) . Both vectors rank the first expert ahead of the second, but assign different relative weights to their errors. We refer to the magnitude of this relative separation between sensitivity scores as 

contrast . Under a global storage budget, contrast controls how strongly experts compete for storage. AlphaQ adapts the curvature of its spectral impor-tance mapping to model-weight statistics (Yang et al., 2026). We study this weighting strength for gradient-based expert sensitivities. In this work, we propose NatScale , an alloca-tion framework designed to explicitly control sen-sitivity contrast in mixed-precision MoE quanti-zation. NatScale applies a power transformation to gradient-based expert sensitivities, choosing the exponent so that normalized squared weights reach a prescribed Jeffreys (symmetric KL) divergence from a uniform distribution over positive-score ex-1arXiv:2610.20353v1 [cs.LG] 14 Oct 2026 perts. Because a power transformation is order-preserving, this calibration leaves the expert rank-ings untouched, and the prescribed Jeffreys diver-gence expresses each model’s overall contrast on a common information-theoretic scale. To score candidate configurations with these cal-ibrated sensitivity weights, NatScale incorporates two additional design elements: (1) Joint Projec-tion Error Measurement . Because gate and up out-puts interact before the down projection, separately measured errors may miss their joint effect on the output. We measure output error with all three pro-jections quantized jointly on fixed original-model inputs. (2) Spectral Structural Priors . The prior itself summarizes each block’s weight structure through three factors: weight spectra, variance and precision decay. Multiplying this prior by the joint errors and calibrated sensitivities yields a score for each configuration, and these scores determine the selection under a global storage budget. The contributions of our work are: • We propose NatScale for mixed-precision MoE quantization. Rather than using importance scores directly or applying fixed power trans-formations, NatScale calibrates expert sensitiv-ity contrast to an explicit information-theoretic target, allowing for more effective allocation of quantization precision under a fixed budget. • We show that expert rankings alone do not de-termine precision allocation: sensitivity contrast controls how strongly experts compete for stor-age. We further demonstrate that preserving na-tive outputs and minimizing perplexity can favor different contrast levels. • We evaluate NatScale on WikiText-2 across Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, Qwen3.8-Flash-Next, and DeepSeek-V4-Flash-0731 at three storage tar-gets, achieving the best native-output fidelity among the tested methods in all nine settings. We additionally evaluate FlashNext on AIME, GPQA, TruthfulQA, and LiveCodeBench. 

2 Related Work 

Post-Training Quantization. Post-training quantization (PTQ) reduces quantization error: GPTQ (Frantar et al., 2023) and AWQ (Lin et al., 2024) use calibration activations, while QuIP# (Tseng et al., 2024) and AQLM (Egiazar-ian et al., 2024) employ codebooks for low-bit quality. Sensitivity-aware quantization methods like BRECQ (Li et al., 2021), SqueezeLLM (Kim et al., 2024), GuidedQuant (Kim et al., 2025), and KronQ (Lee et al., 2026b) compute importance via gradients or curvature, with DASH-Q (Kim et al., 2026) mitigating noisy curvature. For preci-sion allocation, SliM-LLM (Huang et al., 2025b) assigns bits to groups according to their salience, whereas CoopQ (Zhao et al., 2026) uses layer inter-actions to allocate precision across layers. These dense-model formulations do not directly specify expert-level choices under sparse routing. 

MoE Quantization. Extending precision alloca-tion to Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models requires capturing expert importance and interactions. MC-MoE (Huang et al., 2025a) scores experts via ac-tivation frequency, routing probabilities, and er-ror. MODE (Chen et al., 2026) estimates modality-specific importance, and GEMQ (Deng et al., 2026) updates sensitivity dynamically. MxMoE (Duanmu et al., 2025) and FRI-MxMoE (Qi et al., 2026) al-locate block-level precision using reconstruction costs or fuzzy interpolation, while AlphaQ (Yang et al., 2026) uses weight spectra and adapts the cur-vature of its importance mapping to model statistics. Recent work refines granularity: SFMoE (Qi et al., 2027) models cooperation, CAMERA (Xu et al., 2026) constructs micro-experts, and Q-Strata (Lee et al., 2026a) uses model-level objectives. NatScale calibrates gradient-based expert weights to a pre-scribed Jeffreys divergence. It jointly evaluates ex-pert configurations, combining output errors with calibrated weights and AlphaQ’s structural fac-tor, trading coordinate-level gradient alignment for global tractability. 

Calibration and Robustness. Compression per-formance also depends on calibration data and ro-bustness. Dataset selection critically impacts qual-ity (Williams and Aletras, 2024), including self-generated text (Williams et al., 2025), sequence length (Oh and Oh, 2025), and language diver-sity (Chimoto et al., 2026). In multimodal mod-els, MBQ (Li et al., 2025) uses output gradients to balance reconstruction across vision and lan-guage tokens. For MoE models, MoEQuant (Chen et al., 2025) improves calibration coverage through expert-balanced sampling and uses sample–expert affinities during quantization. Related work also addresses differences across data groups: Group DRO (Sagawa et al., 2020) optimizes worst-group loss, while FairQuant (Woergaard and Selvan, 2026) uses group-conditioned importance and fair-ness regularization for mixed-precision quantiza-2tion. NatScale averages frequency-weighted RMS errors across folds. While its contrast target reg-ulates weight separation, it remains dependent on calibration coverage and distribution shifts.

4 Theoretical Analysis 

To substantiate NatScale’s design, this section pro-vides theoretical answers to two fundamental ques-tions: 

1. Why is contrast calibration well-posed, and why choose τ = 1 nat? Raw sensitivity distribu-tions can differ in their overall contrast. NatScale 4puts this contrast on a common information scale by mapping scores through a power transformation to a prescribed symmetric KL divergence τ .For any positive, non-constant gradient scores 

gi, the symmetric KL divergence J (q(β), u ) is a monotonically increasing and continuous function of exponent β ≥ 0 mapping from zero to infinity. Consequently, each target contrast τ > 0 uniquely determines a single positive exponent βτ . Chang-ing τ smoothly scales the relative separation (con-trast) between expert weights without altering their rank ordering. To set a default without tuning the contrast tar-get, we map a two-parameter Weibull fit of ampli-tude scores ri = √gi to a reference distribution whose squared amplitudes have maximum entropy at unit mean. Combining this map with the ordinary maximum-likelihood equation gives the following finite-sample identity: 

Corollary 4.1 (Weibull Reference Target) . For any positive, nonconstant amplitude scores ri,the unweighted two-parameter Weibull maximum-likelihood fit satisfies β1 = bk/ 4, yielding a cali-brated contrast of exactly J (q(β1), u ) = 1 nat. 

The derivation in Appendix A applies to any positive nonconstant sample, without assuming a Weibull data distribution. Thus, τ = 1 nat acts as an information-theoretic anchor, while task-specific performance determines whether a tighter or wider contrast is preferred.

5 Experiments 

We evaluate NatScale on three MoE language mod-els under three routed-weight storage budgets. Our experiments address four questions: (i) how well does contrast-calibrated allocation preserve model predictions relative to existing allocation strategies; (ii) how does the contrast target affect quality when expert rankings are held fixed; (iii) which score components contribute to performance; and (iv) whether the benefits extend to downstream tasks and whole-model compression. 

5.1 Experimental Setup Models. We study Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, Qwen3.8-Flash-Next, and DeepSeek-V4-Flash-0731, with reported total parameter counts of 35B, 125B, and 284B, respectively (Qwen Team, 2026a,b; DeepSeek-AI, 2026b,a). They activate approxi-mately 3B, 6B, and 13B parameters per token and route each token to 8 of 256, 10 of 512, and 6 of 256 routed experts, respectively, in addition to a shared expert. The models span different sparse architectures and native weight formats. Details are specified in Appendix D. 

Quantization and calibration. We allocate for-mats to the gate, up, and down projections of routed experts at nominal targets of 2.0, 2.5, and 3.0 bits per weight (bpw). All methods draw from the same precomputed candidate weights produced by MixedFlowQuant, while preserving their respective allocation rules. NatScale constrains encoded pay-load bytes and accounts for metadata separately; the adapted baselines include header overhead in their bit budgets. The targets specify nominal routed-weight storage. We use 512 fixed sequences of 2,048 tokens from Magpie, UltraChat, and Writ-ingPrompts, divided into six equally weighted cali-bration folds (Xu et al., 2025; Ding et al., 2023; Fan et al., 2018). Baselines. We adapt five allocation methods to these shared candidates: MxMoE (Duanmu et al., 2025), FRI-MxMoE (Qi et al., 2026), Al-phaQ (Yang et al., 2026), MODE (Chen et al., 2026), and GEMQ (Deng et al., 2026). The GEMQ com-parison evaluates its allocation criterion without GPTQ, progressive quantization, or router fine-tuning; its Qwen3.8-Flash-Next and DeepSeek-V4-Flash-0731 results use corrected gradient scoring. The adaptations are specified in Appendix D. 

Evaluation. We measure perplexity (PPL), native-to-quantized KLD, and native-model top-token agreement (Top1) on WikiText-2 (Merity et al., 2016). Within each model, all methods use the same test stream and native reference. We score 255 next-token positions (256–510) in each in-dependent 512-token window. Unlike the fixed-input local error estimates used to construct alloca-tion costs, evaluation runs the complete quantized model, allowing quantization-induced changes to propagate through hidden states and routing deci-sions.

5.3 Ablation Studies Expert weight contrast. To isolate the effect of contrast, we vary τ ∈ { 0.5, √0.5, 1, √2, 2} while retaining each model’s sensitivity scores, candidate weights, local errors, and storage ceiling. Each target sets the separation of expert weights on the same information scale across models. Expert rank-ings remain fixed, so the sweep isolates the effect of weighting strength on allocation quality. Figure 2 shows that contrast materially affects output fidelity. On DeepSeek-V4-Flash-0731 at 2.0 bpw, increasing τ from 1 to 2 reduces KLD from 0.7293 to 0.6780 (7.03%) and increases Top1 by 1.02 percentage points. 6 Conclusion 

We presented NatScale, a mixed-precision MoE al-location framework that explicitly calibrates the contrast between expert sensitivities instead of relying on their rankings alone. It combines a 8model-specific, rank-preserving power transforma-tion with joint gate, up, and down output errors and a structural precision prior to allocate weight for-mats under a storage budget. At the fixed one-nat target, NatScale achieves the lowest KLD and high-est top-token agreement in all nine comparisons with five adapted baselines, and the lowest PPL in seven. Downstream and whole-model evaluations provide additional evidence of its utility. The con-trast sweeps show that native-output fidelity and perplexity can favor different targets. 

Limitations 

While effective, NatScale requires additional com-putation to estimate expert sensitivity and select precision levels, adding to the cost of preparing a quantized model. Its allocation decisions also depend on how well the calibration data reflect the inputs encountered during deployment. Changes in input distribution or expert usage may therefore affect the quality of the resulting allocation. In ad-dition, the error estimates used to guide precision selection are approximations and may not fully cap-ture interactions between quantized components or changes in downstream task performance. Future work could explore more efficient calibration pro-cedures and evaluate the stability of precision allo-cations across different data distributions. It could also examine whether calibration statistics can be reused when adapting a model to related applica-tion domains. Another direction is to study how the choice and amount of calibration data affect precision selection in task-specific settings.

B Residual Modeling and Sensitivity 

The allocation cost in Equation 8 combines cali-brated sensitivity weights with candidate residuals. To explain this combination, we examine raw-RMS stability and relate the gradient and error statistics of Sections 3.2 and 3.3 through native first-order influence. 

Stability of raw RMS. We begin with the effect of small residual perturbations. For a residual vec-tor e ∈ Rd, let R(e) = ∥e∥2/√d. The reverse triangle inequality gives 

|R(e + δ) − R(e)| ≤ ∥ δ∥2/√d. (22) Centering the MSE before taking its square root changes this behavior. Deterministic boundary rounding. To construct the LP solution and a feasible discrete allocation, the solver first orders each expert’s candidates by ascending payload and then loss, preserving the original order for exact ties. It removes dominated points and constructs the lower convex hull, whose edges represent objective reductions obtained by spending additional bytes. The solver sorts these edges by decreasing reduction per byte. Since the hull slopes are ordered, this sorting preserves the required order of upgrades within each expert. The LP consumes complete edges until the first bound-ary edge exceeds the remaining budget, then takes the affordable fraction of that edge. If all upgrades fit, the endpoint allocation is already integral. When the boundary is fractional, rounding starts from its lower-payload endpoint. The solver skips the boundary edge and scans the remaining edges in the same stable order, accepting an upgrade only when the current expert configuration is its pre-ceding endpoint and the additional bytes fit. Each accepted move therefore preserves feasibility. If a feasible parent configuration from the same catalog has a strictly smaller objective, the solver retains it. The procedure returns a feasible map together with its measured gap to the LP optimum. 

D Evaluation Setup 

To complement the comparison in Table 1, this ap-pendix describes the model architectures, shared evaluation protocol, and baseline adaptations for Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, Qwen3.8-Flash-Next, and DeepSeek-V4-Flash-0731. 

Model scale and architecture. The three mod-els span increasing reported total and activated parameter counts: 35B/3B for Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, 125B/6B for Qwen3.8-Flash-Next, and 284B/13B for DeepSeek-V4-Flash-0731 (Qwen Team, 2026a,b; DeepSeek-AI, 2026b,a). Qwen3.6-35B-A3B has 40 layers and 256 routed experts per layer, activating eight plus one shared ex-pert. Qwen3.8-Flash-Next has 48 layers and 512 routed experts, activating ten plus one shared ex-pert. DeepSeek-V4-Flash-0731 has 43 layers and 256 routed experts, activating six plus one shared expert. These reported parameter counts follow each model’s specification. For Qwen3.8-Flash-Next, the reported 125B excludes the additional 51B n-gram embedding parameters and 4B multi-token-prediction (MTP) parameters. In contrast, the of-ficial 284B Flash specification includes its MTP configuration, while the 0731 checkpoint also con-tains the DSpark speculative-decoding module. Re-ported specifications, complete checkpoint parame-ter counts, and routed-only byte budgets therefore describe different scopes. 

Calibration. For each model, we use a frozen 1,048,576-token calibration pool rendered with its native chat template and assign equal weight to the six folds. Across NatScale score variants, we reuse the native gradient second moments, rout-ing frequencies, candidate weights, and complete joint reconstruction errors. Changing the threshold or budget therefore requires no new collection of statistics. 

Shared candidate formats. All methods use can-didates produced by the MixedFlowQuant (MFQ) backend. Its integer and vector-quantization for-mats are denoted by NINT and NVQ, respectively, with suffixes identifying the fixed candidate en-codings. Qwen3.6-35B-A3B and Qwen3.8-Flash-Next use NINT2, NINT3, NINT4, NINT5, NINT6, NINT8, NVQ2J, NVQ2J-L, NVQ2J-XL, NVQ3J, NVQ3J-512, NVQ3J-L, NVQ1-S, and NVQ1-L. DeepSeek-V4-Flash-0731 uses NINT2, NINT3, the six NVQ2J/NVQ3J formats, native MXFP4, NVQ1-S, and NVQ1-L. The resulting catalogs con-tain 14, 14, and 11 entries, respectively. DeepSeek-V4-Flash-0731 is not assigned NINT4/5/6/8 can-didates above its native MXFP4 endpoint. Within these catalogs, NatScale evaluates complete mixed gate/up/down configurations while keeping non-routed weights native. To compare allocation methods over the same candidates, the five external baselines share these weights and the native evaluation protocol. Each baseline retains its own scoring rule and allocation constraints; the adaptations are described below in the order used in the result tables. 

MxMoE. MxMoE assigns precision to linear blocks using their reconstruction sensitivity (Du-anmu et al., 2025). Our adaptation measures layer-output L2 error after a single-projection pertur-bation with r = 1 , ties the gate and up formats within each expert, and uses a global storage bud-get. Hadamard transforms and GPTQ are disabled. 17 FRI-MxMoE. FRI-MxMoE uses fuzzy rule in-terpolation to estimate reconstruction costs from a subset of profiled experts (Qi et al., 2026). We evaluate the resulting whole-model al-locations against four released GSQ-RCO checkpoints (ISTA-DASLab, 2026). With these evaluation inputs and metrics fixed, each method runs on its corresponding back-end.
