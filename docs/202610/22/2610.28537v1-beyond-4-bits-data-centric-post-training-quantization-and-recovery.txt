## ABSTRACT 

Post-training quantization (PTQ) compresses the weights of a trained model with limited compute and data. Down to 4 bits it is near-lossless, but at 2 bits scalar PTQ configurations fall to chance-level accuracy. We study three choices usually treated as implementation details that matter little at 4 bits but decide 2-bit quality: the quantization representation, the calibration data, and the recovery fine-tuning data, across six dense and mixture-of experts models (ranging from 3.8 to 32 Bparameters). Representation is the largest factor and vector codebooks restore us-able models where scalar PTQ pipelines collapse. Most of the remaining loss in accuracy is on generative tasks, and the amount of performance drop is model-dependent. Furthermore, at a short recovery budget, calibration and recovery data are coupled. The best recovery data depends on the calibration data, so the two must be chosen jointly. The resulting recipe involves a vector codebook represen-tation, a small task-aligned calibration mixture, and an inexpensive codebook-only recovery stage. Including codebooks, it uses about 2.3 effective bits per weight and retains 89 to 97% of full-precision question-answering (QA) accuracy where scalar PTQ is at chance, and, including recovery, uses 1.4 to 30 × less compute than the strongest quantization-aware-training (QAT) baseline. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Post-training quantization (PTQ) is standard for serving large language models under tight memory budgets. At 4 bits (W4A16: 4-bit weights, 16-bit activations), it costs only a few accuracy points (Frantar et al., 2023; Lin et al., 2024; Shao et al., 2024; Ashkboos et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025b). At 2 bits, scalar PTQ falls to chance-level accuracy (Egiazarian et al., 2024; Chee et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024a; Figure 1a), and quantized reasoning models can overthink and loop (Lotfi et al., 2026). Richer codebooks (Van Baalen et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024a; Egiazarian et al., 2024; Tseng et al., 2024a) or learned quantization (Liu et al., 2024b; Du et al., 2024; Dadgarnia et al., 2026) trade compute for quality, yet 2-bit models still lag their 4-bit counterparts. Three choices usually treated as implementation details govern 2-bit quality: the quantization rep-resentation , the calibration data that shape quantization, and the recovery data used to fine-tune the quantized model. They matter little at 4 bits but become consequential below 4 bits, where calibration and recovery data also interact, so tuning them separately can be misleading. We study 2-bit quantization and recovery across six dense and mixture-of-experts (MoE) models ( 3.8 to 32 Bparameters) and propose an actionable recipe (Section 5) for low-budget 2-bit quantization. Our primary observations are: • Representation dominates at 2 bits. Every scalar PTQ pipeline we test falls to random chance-level, while vector PTQ at ∼2.3 effective bits keeps 89 –97% of a full-precision model’s question-answering accuracy without training, using 39 –304 × less compute than trained scalar methods (Table 4). • Loss of reasoning ability is model-dependent. Question-answering retention is similar across models, but HumanEval (codegen) retention spans 45% (Phi-4-mini, Qwen3-8B) to

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Scalar PTQ. GPTQ (Frantar et al., 2023) sets the near-lossless 4-bit weight-only baseline via ap-proximate second-order error minimization; AWQ (Lin et al., 2024) and SmoothQuant (Xiao et al., 2023) rescale salient channels, while QuaRot (Ashkboos et al., 2024) and SpinQuant (Liu et al., 2025b) rotate weights before rounding. The scalar PTQ configurations we evaluate collapse at 2 bits without any further quantization-aware training or recovery (Section 4.4). 

Vector and ultra-low-bit quantization. Codebooks give better rate-distortion below 3 bits. QuIP (Chee et al., 2023) adds incoherence processing to adaptive scalar rounding; QuIP# (Tseng et al., 2024a) pairs it with lattice codebooks and fine-tuning. AQLM, GPTVQ, VPTQ, and QTIP (Egiazar-ian et al., 2024; Van Baalen et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024a; Tseng et al., 2024b) use additive, hier-archical, or trellis-coded codebooks. YAQA (Tseng et al., 2026) adds a Fisher-weighted rounding objective. Non-uniform (Kim et al., 2024) and 1-bit (Huang et al., 2024; Yuan et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2024b) methods also target lower precision to go even beyond 2-bits. 

Trained scalar quantization. OmniQuant (Shao et al., 2024) learns clipping thresholds block-wise, and GSQ (Dadgarnia et al., 2026) learns scalar-grid assignments through a Gumbel-Softmax relaxation; we use GSQ as an intermediate-cost scalar baseline between PTQ and QAT. 

Calibration data. PTQ usually calibrates on a few hundred C4 or WikiText-2 sequences (Frantar et al., 2023; Lin et al., 2024; Shao et al., 2024; Egiazarian et al., 2024). Calibration data affect 4-bit models (Williams & Aletras, 2024) and pruning (Bandari et al., 2024; Ji et al., 2025), and self-generated text can replace curated corpora (Williams et al., 2025). We find that calibration data 2Preprint. Under Review. effects 2-bit PTQ methods much more than on 4-bit methods and this effect is consistent across representations and recipe grids (Appendix A.6). 

Evaluating quantized LLMs. Quantization costs generation, reasoning, and knowledge-intensive tasks more than perplexity or multiple-choice QA as suggested by (Jaiswal et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024a; Jin et al., 2024; Kurtic et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025a), and robustness depends on model size and token density (Dettmers & Zettlemoyer, 2023; Kumar et al., 2025; Ouyang et al., 2025). We confirm this gap at 2 bits and show that the degradation of generation quality depends on calibration, recovery data and model-scale. 

Recovery for quantized models. Distillation-based recovery (Hinton et al., 2015) narrows the quan-tization gap without full QAT. LLM-QAT and BitDistiller (Liu et al., 2024b; Du et al., 2024) retrain all weights; LoRA and QLoRA (Hu et al., 2022; Dettmers et al., 2023) add high-precision parame-ters; closest to ours, EfficientQAT (Chen et al., 2025) trains all weights block-wise, then quantization parameters end to end; ParetoQ (Liu et al., 2026) studies extremely low-bit QAT. AQLM and QuIP# fine-tune codebooks or continuous parameters after quantization. PV-Tuning (Malinovskii et al., 2024) additionally also learns discrete assignments to the codebook vectors. In this work we use codebook-only recovery and show that its best data flips with the calibration data, so the two must be chosen jointly (Section 4.2).

## 4 EXPERIMENTS AND RESULTS 

4.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Models and quantizers. We evaluate Phi-4-mini (Abouelenin et al., 2025) (3.8B, instruct), Llama-3.1-8B (Grattafiori et al., 2024) (base), and Qwen3-30B-A3B (Yang et al., 2025) (reasoning MoE, 

∼3B active). Qwen3-8B (reasoning) checks the calibration and recovery choices; OLMoE-1B-7B (Muennighoff et al., 2025) (instruct MoE) and Qwen3-32B (reasoning, dense) extend the compar-isons to smaller MoEs and large-scale dense models, respectively. The scalar baselines we use are SpinQuant (Liu et al., 2025b) and QuaRot (Ashkboos et al., 2024) at group size 128. Furthermore, we observe that learned rotations do not help at 2 bits (Appendix A.3). The vector-quantization base-lines we study are VPTQ (Liu et al., 2024a) ( 216 -entry codebook over 8-dimensional vectors) and YAQA (Tseng et al., 2026), a Fisher-weighted trellis quantizer, both in the same incoherence-rotated frame; we report native (unrotated) VPTQ where it is stronger. We also compare vector quantization against GSQ (Dadgarnia et al., 2026) (scalar PTQ with learned assignments) and BitDistiller (Du et al., 2024) (scalar QAT). In our experiments we quantize attention and MLP projections, including every MoE expert. Routers, embeddings, norms and the language modeling head stay in half preci-sion. Our ablation on the router precision in Appendix A.10, suggests that the router is pretty robust to quantization. Counting indices, codebooks, and scales, every method uses 2.09 –2.33 effective bits per weight. In the MoEs, VPTQ shares codebooks across experts, while YAQA operates with a single large codebook for all weights. We evaluate dequantized weights, report packed-size stor-age estimates, and count dominant model passes, amortizing Hessian collection over the calibration sweep. Configurations and protocols are in Appendices A.3, A.2. 

Calibration and evaluation. For calibration data we primarily focus on web, instruction, math (OpenR1-Math), code, and their mixtures. We report an AvgQA metric which averages over seven QA tasks: MMLU (5-shot) (Hendrycks et al., 2020), WinoGrande (Sakaguchi et al., 2021), and LAMBADA (Paperno et al., 2016), scored by accuracy, and ARC-Easy, ARC-Challenge (Clark et al., 2018), HellaSwag (Zellers et al., 2019), and PIQA (Bisk et al., 2020), scored by length-normalized accuracy (all 0-shot except MMLU). We report an AvgGen metric which averages GSM8K (5-shot, strict match) (Cobbe et al., 2021) and HumanEval (0-shot, pass@1) (Chen et al., 2021); we also report IFEval (0-shot, prompt-strict) (Zhou et al., 2023). 4.6 CODEBOOK -ONLY RECOVERY IS MODEL -DEPENDENT 

Table 8 pairs each 2-bit base with long codebook-only recovery on the same recipe data. On Phi-4-mini, recovery changes QA little but roughly doubles HumanEval. On Qwen3-MoE it changes both little; on Llama-3.1-8B base model, QA decreases in all three long runs and generation has no consistent gain. IFEval falls on most Phi-4-mini recipes under completion prompts and is mostly, but not uniformly, preserved under the chat template (Appendix Table 35). Furthermore recovery does not help 2-bit models which hit the random floor. Scale-only recovery on a collapsed scalar 2-bit model leaves it near the floor (Appendix A.7). 

Under the deployment prompt, short recovery need not cost instruction following. With the chat template, web recovery of the math-calibrated Phi-4-mini base raises GSM8K from 55 .0 to 

58 .9, HumanEval from 34 .1 to 43 .9, and IFEval from 40 .3 to 44 .5 (four recovery seeds); math recovery raises GSM8K further ( 61 .5) but lowers IFEval to 37 .9 (Table 36). The best recovery data still flips with calibration and math recovery gives the highest AvgGen from the web base and web recovery from the math base. 

Recovery data matter more than distillation hyperparameters. The type of knowledge distil-lation loss, the teacher precision, temperature, and top-k truncation change AvgQA little. A 4-bit teacher matches a BF 16 one, and dropping the teacher keeps most generation gain (Appendix A.7). With fixed web recovery data, generation gains 6.3 points by 3,200 examples and then plateaus, while QA rises slowly with more data (Figure 5). > Recipe: five steps to an accurate 2-bit model
> 1. Use a vector codebook (e.g., YAQA); scalar PTQ collapses at 2 bits. 2. Calibrate on target-domain data ; mix in broad web text when QA matters. 3. ## 6 CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK 

Conclusion At 2 bits, quality depends heavily on representation, calibration data, and recovery. A vector codebook restores most collapsed accuracy and a task-aligned calibration mixture and short codebook-only recovery stage narrow the generation gap at much less compute than QAT. The data choices are not separable at a short recovery budget: calibration domain drives generation, and the best recovery data depends on it. Across three models and a held-out composite, math recovery helps a web-calibrated base but not a math-calibrated one (clearest on Phi-4-mini). Tuning the two data choices separately can therefore select the wrong recovery data, and whether to recover must be validated per model. Most remaining loss is in generation, whose measurement depends on prompt format and numerical precision. 

Limitations and Future Work. We measure base and data-sample variability only on Phi-4-mini, and replicate bases share one rotation and one recovery seed. The interaction covers one domain pair and one quantizer (YAQA) at a short budget; we did not re-run the 2 × 2 design at long budgets. We leave these studies for future work.

QuaRot and SpinQuant (scalar PTQ). QuaRot and SpinQuant apply an incoherence rotation before scale-only ≈ 2.12 -bit GPTQ quantization (Appendix A.2). QuaRot uses a fixed randomized Hadamard transform with activation ordering, Hessian-ordered columns, error feedback, and damp-ing 0.1. SpinQuant uses residual-stream and per-head rotations ( R1, R2) at randomized-Hadamard initialization, with experts rotated separately on Qwen3-MoE. Both methods consume 128 calibra-tion sequences and add no trained weights. 

Learned-rotation in Spinquant. We test learned SpinQuant rotations on the two dense primary models (Table 12). We optimize R1 and R2 with Cayley SGD for 100 steps on the same 128 calibra-tion sequences from each domain. We use learning rates 1.5 and 0.25 , then the same GPTQ quan-tization. Learned rotations leave all 2-bit models at the random-accuracy floor and barely change best-domain 4-bit AvgQA (Table 12). The scalar cliff is not from the unoptimized rotation. We could not fit the full-precision copy needed for Qwen3-30B-A3B, so its SpinQuant entries keep the Hadamard initialization. 

VPTQ (vector PTQ). VPTQ maps each 8-dimensional weight sub-vector to one of 65 ,536 (216 )codebook entries ( 2.00 index bits). Dense models use one 1 MiB FP 16 codebook per linear layer (2.12 –2.33 effective bits). MoEs use expert-shared codebooks ( 2.09 –2.14 ). VPTQ uses channel-independent second-order optimization over a shared Hadamard frame with input Hessians collected by calibration domain. The rate control of §A.4 uses one model-wide 262 ,144 -entry ( 218 ) codebook, giving 2.25 index bits and 2.26 effective bits. 

YAQA (vector PTQ). YAQA uses a trellis codebook with Fisher-weighted, Kronecker-factored curvature from the full-model KL objective and 2.25 index bits. Sign vectors and a shared lookup table bring the rate to 2.26 –2.31 effective bits. YAQA also retains a fine-tuning-ready codebook under the same incoherence rotation, with curvature estimated from 128 –2,048 sequences per cali-bration domain.

Figure 15 presents the effect of repetition penaty on accuracy and 4-gram lopp score at bf16 and 2-bit precisions. 

Qualitative examples. Two representative MATH-500 generations from the 2-bit Qwen3-MoE checkpoint illustrate bounded solving and looping under default greedy decoding. 

2-bit, bounded and correct (Level-4, gold = √5)

“I need the radius of a right cylindrical tank filled at 20 π m3/hr while the level rises 4 m/hr. Since 

V = πr 2h, dV dt = πr 2 dh dt , so 20 π = πr 2 · 4 ⇒ r2 = 5 ⇒ r = √5.” The model emits a bounded 

1,740 -token chain and terminates with the correct \boxed {√5}.

2-bit, looping failure (Level-5, gold = 6 − 5i)

“I need to rotate z around c by π/ 4: w = ( z − c)eiπ/ 4 + c. Let me compute z − c. . . ” The chain reasons coherently at first. It then collapses into a degenerate repetition of one algebraic line (loop score 0.94 ). It fills all 8,192 tokens without ever emitting a boxed answer , so it is scored wrong. With a repetition penalty of 1.3 the same model solves this problem.
