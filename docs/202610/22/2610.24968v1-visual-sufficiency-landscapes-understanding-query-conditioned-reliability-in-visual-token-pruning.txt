# Visual Sufficiency Landscapes: Understanding Query-Conditioned Reliability in Visual Token Pruning 

> Zhenghan Li, Kexin Sun, Wei Zheng ∗, Peng Hu, Xiao Wei, Anbo Huang
> School of Software, Northwestern Polytechnical University, Xi’an 710129, China

Abstract 

Different visual token pruning strategies retain different subsets while main-taining similar task performance. This motivates a combinatorial question: how many visual subsets can preserve model behavior, and how does their abundance depend on the query? We introduce visual sufficiency landscapes to characterize this abundance. At a fixed layer and budget, visual sufficiency probability is the fraction of uniformly sampled subsets that jointly preserve the dense model’s an-swer, reference-answer likelihood, and output distribution. Independent sampling replicates separate input variation from estimation noise. Across Qwen2.5-VL-7B, InternVL2-8B, and LLaVA-v1.5-7B, differences between inputs on GQA and TextVQA exceed replication error, and different questions about the same GQA image produce distinct landscapes. Sufficiency probability also improves failure prediction for full pruning methods. On LLaVA, adding this probability increases AUC by 0.1151 for FastV, 0.0683 for VisionZip, and 0.1401 for SparseVLM, while reducing log loss and Brier score. Several architecture-adapted configurations on the other two models reproduce these gains. Under controlled selection rules, pre-dictors trained on the original images reduce log loss on new images by 9.0%, 13.0%, and 14.1%, respectively. These results establish successful-subset abun-dance as a measurable dimension of local prunability and connect it to the identi-fication of high-risk pruning conditions. 

Keywords: Vision-language models, Visual token pruning, Visual sufficiency, Query dependence, Reliability estimation 

1. Introduction 

Vision-language models connect image content to language models through vi-sual tokens [1, 2]. Longer visual sequences increase inference cost, making visual token pruning an important route to efficient multimodal inference. Attention-based filtering, redundancy reduction, and diversity-based selection construct re-tained sets using different criteria, yet all can reduce visual computation while maintaining task performance close to that of the dense model [3–5]. Why can different retained visual subsets succeed? This motivates us to examine the mul-tiplicity of effective visual combinations and the flexibility in subset selection for a given input.

A successful pruning decision may occur where many subsets preserve be-havior, or it may depend on a few viable combinations. The selected sets succeed in both cases, but the flexibility in selection differs. This raises a combinatorial question: for a fixed input, layer, and budget, how abundant are successful combi-nations? Different questions about the same image require different visual infor-mation, so how does this abundance change with the query? Existing research examines input conditions and pruning behavior from sev-eral perspectives. Query-conditioned methods incorporate instruction relevance into subset construction [6, 7]. Information Horizon and CoRePrune examine how visual information changes with depth and how removability depends on the surrounding deletion context, respectively [8, 9]. Research on confidence further connects pruned outputs to risk-controlled budgets [10]. We study the candidate subset space to characterize how many retention choices succeed under a given condition, providing a combinatorial reference for understanding when different selection strategies can work. We introduce visual sufficiency landscapes , which characterize local prunabil-ity through the proportion of successful subsets in a fixed-budget combinatorial space. Given an image, question, intervention layer, and retention count, we uni-formly sample visual subsets of a fixed size. We define visual sufficiency prob-ability , 𝑝 suf , as the probability of jointly preserving the dense model’s answer, reference-answer likelihood, and output distribution. This joint event captures both the generated answer and probabilistic behavior, while the uniform measure provides a common combinatorial reference across inputs. Higher sufficiency probability indicates more abundant successful combinations. Arranging these probabilities across layers and budgets yields a landscape (Fig. 1), extending the evaluation of individual pruning outcomes to a measurement of successful-subset abundance. We first use two independent sampling replicates to distinguish input variation from replication error. Multiple questions about each image then hold visual con-tent fixed while varying the query condition. Across three models, between-input curve differences exceed replication error. In the GQA [11] multi-question exper-iment, all 32 images in each model have positive point estimates of the net query curve difference. Supplementary experiments find query differences at all three tested budgets. These results quantify query dependence as variation in the space of successful combinations. Specific selectors favor particular regions of the subset space. We therefore use paired predictors to test whether sufficiency probability under the uniform mea-sure provides additional risk information. On LLaVA, adding sufficiency proba-bility improves AUC, log loss, and Brier score for predicting joint-preservation failure under FastV, VisionZip, and SparseVLM. On a separate image cohort, alerts based on low sufficiency probability yield an equally weighted risk differ-ence of 0.1719 across the three methods. Several architecture-adapted configura-tions on Qwen2.5-VL and InternVL2 reproduce the predictive gains. Experiments with controlled selection rules further show that sufficiency probability adds in-formation beyond single-token information, visual-removal responses, and query-representation summaries. These gains persist when fixed predictors are evaluated on new images, connecting combinatorial measurements to the identification of high-risk inputs for specific methods. 2. Related Work 

2.1. Visual token compression and query-conditioned selection 

Visual token compression reduces multimodal computation by selecting, merging, or reconstructing retained representations. ToMe merges tokens by fea-3ture similarity, providing a representative approach to redundancy reduction [12]. LLaVA-PruMerge and VisionZip apply selection and aggregation to visual rep-resentations in multimodal models [4, 13]. Within the language model, FastV filters visual positions using attention from early layers [3]. Subset construction can also follow objectives beyond position-wise attention ranking: DivPrune em-phasizes diversity within the retained set [5], while TopV formulates pruning as inference-time optimization and considers compatibility with efficient attention and caching [14]. The query determines which visual content matters for the current task. PuMer combines text-guided pruning with merging within each modality [15]. TRIM, SparseVLM, and CROP guide compression using cross-modal relevance, rel-evant text tokens, and query-relevant regions, respectively [16–18]. Recent methods incorporate this conditioning into set-level objectives. CDPruner maxi-mizes instruction-conditioned diversity through a determinantal point process [6]; HAWK combines attention-head importance with text guidance to evaluate visual tokens [19]; and QCPruner constructs retained sets through a query-conditioned coverage objective [7]. Budget allocation and subset selection are closely related. ATP-LLaVA adapts retention ratios across instances and layers [20]. FitPrune derives pruning plans for a prescribed budget from attention-distribution statistics [21], and OccamToken uses evidence testing to adapt the retention budget [22]. These approaches offer ways to construct pruning actions from input conditions. Our work complements them by measuring the action space itself: at a fixed layer and budget, we estimate the fraction of successful subsets to characterize how many combinations preserve behavior for a given input. 

2.2. Depth and context dependence of visual information 

The propagation of visual information through multimodal computation changes the conditions for compression across layers. VTW exploits the transfer of visual information into text representations to withdraw explicit visual tokens in later layers [23]. PyramidDrop performs staged compression based on depth-dependent visual redundancy [24]. EmbedLens examines input-token sparsity and redundant visual computation through the semantic structure of visual rep-resentations [25]. These studies extend pruning analysis from input length to representation content and the stage at which it is processed. More detailed analyses examine how token responses change with context. In-formation Horizon defines information through the change in target-output prob-ability when retaining a single token relative to removing all visual tokens [8]. CoRePrune analyzes the effects of representation depth and surrounding dele-tion sets on removability [9]. DSTP tracks shifts in relevant visual information during decoding and adapts the retained content accordingly [26]. We focus on combinatorial responses under fixed intervention conditions and compare them across images and across questions about the same image. Single-position re-sponses and successful-subset abundance are distinct statistical objects: for a gen-eral set-response function, observations of the empty set, singletons, and full set do not uniquely determine the success fraction of intermediate-size subsets (Ap-pendix D.3). 42.3. Subset interventions and predictive sufficiency 

Whether model behavior persists after retaining only part of the input is also central to explanation evaluation. ERASER evaluates sufficiency through predic-tion changes after retaining a rationale and comprehensiveness through changes after removing it [27]. Recent analyses of rationale learning show that sufficiency scores depend on non-rationale context, and that their relationship to correct clas-sification and learning from rationales varies across tasks and models [28]. Suffi-ciency must therefore be interpreted with respect to the intervention and behavioral reference. We retain a fixed number of ordinary visual positions at an intermedi-ate model layer and jointly evaluate answer preservation, reference-answer likeli-hood, and output distribution against dense behavior. A uniform subset measure then defines sufficiency probability as the fraction of combinations satisfying joint preservation, enabling comparisons of successful-region abundance across input conditions. 

2.4. Behavioral preservation and pruning risk 

Task performance and behavioral reliability capture different aspects of prun-ing outcomes. FEATHER shows that preserved performance on some common tasks can coexist with degraded fine-grained abilities such as localization [29]. Research on confidence calibration also distinguishes predictive confidence from empirical accuracy [30]. For visual pruning, recent work analyzes confident errors following evidence removal and uses post-pruning confidence to trigger full-token inference, with conformal risk control constraining expected deviation from dense outputs [10]. These confidence-based approaches assess risk from outputs produced by selected subsets. Visual sufficiency landscapes characterize success probability mass over the candidate subset space at a fixed budget. In Section 5, we test the incremental predictive information of this combinatorial measure beyond input conditions, dense behavior, and existing information summaries, and examine its value for risk alerts under full pruning methods and on new images. This provides a testable link between successful-combination abundance and the failure risk of specific selection strategies. 

3. Visual Sufficiency Landscapes 

Visual sufficiency landscapes extend the success or failure of an individual pruning decision to the fraction of successful subsets at a fixed budget. This mea-surement requires a specified subset intervention, a behavioral preservation cri-terion, and an interpretation of differences under finite sampling. We define the joint-preservation event and sufficiency probability, derive their successful-set in-terpretation, and use independent replicates as an error reference for landscape comparisons.

4. Experimental Analysis 

We test whether sufficiency landscapes reveal input variation beyond average depth trends. We first compare between-input differences with independent repli-cation error, then hold images fixed to examine query conditions, and finally vary the retention budget to determine whether query differences persist across com-pression levels. 

4.1. Experimental setup 

Experiments cover Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct [2], InternVL2-8B [32], and LLaVA-v1.5-7B [1], abbreviated as Qwen2.5-VL, InternVL2, and LLaVA. We evaluate GQA [11] and TextVQA [31]. For each model, the single-question ex-periment uses 32 images per task with one question per image. The within-image multi-question experiment uses another 32 GQA images with three questions each. Direct comparison of image IDs confirms that the two cohorts are disjoint. Inputs are paired across models. The main retention ratio is 𝜌 = 0.25 . The main curve grid spans L4–L24 at six layers for Qwen2.5-VL and L5–L30 at seven layers for InternVL2 and LLaVA. Cross-model effect comparisons use six relative-depth anchors. A budget supple-ment evaluates 𝜌 ∈ {0.125, 0.25, 0.50} on 12 preselected GQA images with three questions each. The controlled-selection prediction experiment in Section 5.3 uses a seven-layer grid, specified separately from the main curve measurements here. Each model completes 64 valid single-question inputs and 96 questions in the multi-question cohort; cohort construction is detailed in Appendix E. All intervals are percentile 95% intervals from 5,000 image-cluster bootstrap resamples [33]. Directional support is the proportion of resampled estimates that retain the stated direction.

6. Discussion and Limitations 

Interpretation of the measurement. Visual sufficiency extends the analysis of pruning reliability from individual retained sets to the abundance of successful combinations. It measures preservation of dense behavior after a given compu-tational prefix and can therefore preserve an incorrect dense answer. The joint event also captures probabilistic changes beyond the generated answer. In the main-cohort experiments with full methods, 19, 26, and 24 questions satisfy task preservation under FastV, VisionZip, and SparseVLM, respectively, but fail the NLL or KL condition (Appendix C.3). Answer preservation and joint preservation thus reveal different aspects of behavioral change after pruning. 

Query conditions and landscape variation. The within-image results show that visual content alone does not determine the sufficiency landscape. Query condi-tions encompass jointly varying factors such as length, semantics, referents, and answer format. Local decreases in individual curves are observed under finite sampling and a discrete layer grid. The first high-sufficiency position summarizes 17 threshold attainment, while the full curve retains more information about variation with depth. 

Scope of the predictive results. The full-method experiments show that one suffi-ciency probability measured on base LLaVA provides additional risk information for three native pruning pipelines. Experiments across architectures reproduce this increment in several adapted configurations on Qwen2.5-VL and InternVL2. Under controlled selection rules, predictors are fitted independently within each model, and evaluation of fixed predictors on new GQA images supports gener-alization across images. Together, these experiments establish an empirical link between successful-subset abundance and specific pruning risks. The controlled central-position rule uses a proxy grid. Predictive gains on relation questions also suggest that joint preservation of multiple visual elements may contribute infor-mation; testing this mechanism requires direct analysis of joint responses. 

From offline measurement to pruning decisions. Sufficiency landscapes provide a measurable target for risk-aware pruning: identifying inputs with few successful combinations that require more careful subset selection. The current measurement runs 128 random interventions per condition and uses reference answers to com-pute NLL and KL, making it suitable for offline analysis. Turning it into an online decision signal requires inexpensive sufficiency estimators and evaluation signals available at inference time. It also requires jointly evaluating task performance and total computational cost when alerts trigger fallback inference or budget ad-justments. 

7. Conclusion 

Visual sufficiency landscapes characterize the flexibility in selecting effective retained sets through the abundance of successful combinations. This provides a combinatorial perspective on the conditions under which different pruning strate-gies can succeed. Experiments on three vision-language models show that land-scape differences across inputs and across questions about the same image ex-ceed independent replication error, with query differences persisting across mul-tiple budgets. Sufficiency probability also adds information beyond existing fea-tures for predicting joint-preservation failure under full pruning methods and con-trolled selection rules. Replication across architectures, direct evaluation on new images, and cross-image risk alerts support the practical value of this measure-ment. Successful-combination abundance and its query dependence are therefore important dimensions of visual token pruning reliability and provide a basis for risk-aware pruning design.

Implementation, Predictor Features, and Evaluation Details 

A.1. Model and task implementation 

GQA [11] uses the validation balanced instructions and corresponding images; TextVQA [31] uses the validation split. Both tasks request a single word or short phrase, with a maximum of 16 generated tokens, stochastic generation disabled, and the KV cache disabled. The common prompt is “Answer the question using a single word or short phrase. Question: {question} Answer:, with line breaks and model chat templates preserved by each adapter. Qwen2.5-VL and InternVL2 use bfloat16, and LLaVA uses float16. Language decoders use eager attention. The input pixel range for Qwen2.5-VL is 200704–1003520. InternVL2 uses at most 12 dynamic image tiles plus a thumbnail; LLaVA uses padding-based preprocessing and 576 visual tokens. Cross-model comparisons retain each model extquotesingle s native preprocessing. The two original sampling seeds are 2026091711 and 2026091712; the boot-strap seed for the main landscape analyses is 2026091714. Each intervention re-moves only ordinary visual positions at the current layer, with subsequent compu-tation following the model-specific physical compression pipeline. Special bound-ary tokens are excluded from the sampling pool. Engineering checks before mea-surement cover dense inference, full-retention and repeated-execution consistency, visual-position ranges, physical subset interventions, and task parsing. Seeds, sampling lists, and statistical configurations for supplementary stages are recorded with their run outputs. 

A.2. Controlled selection rules and probability features 

The three controlled rules select by query similarity, feature distance, and central position. Query similarity uses the cosine similarity between the final prompt-position representation and visual representations. Feature distance uses Euclidean distance from the mean visual representation. Central position uses a row-major proxy grid constructed from the token count and aspect ratio. For the subset 𝑆 𝑎 produced by selector 𝑎 , sufficiency probability is estimated using inde-pendent random interventions and smoothed as ̃𝑝 suf = 𝑛 success + 0.5 129 . (A.1) Fixed logistic regression and layer-aware modeling use identical image groups and selector labels. They assess incremental information under a fixed linear mapping and a richer representation of layer effects, respectively. 

A.3. Features and coverage 

The feature ablation uses four nested L2-regularized logistic regression mod-els. 𝑀 0 includes relative layer position, retention ratio, visual token count, actual retention count, question length in characters, dense reference-answer NLL, dense output entropy, and the gap between the top two output probabilities. D.1. Proof of successful-subset disagreement across queries 

Using the common subset space Ω and uniform distribution 𝑈 from the main text, write the two success labels as 𝑌 1(𝑆) and 𝑌 2(𝑆) . Since each label is 0 or 1, |𝑌 1(𝑆) − 𝑌 2(𝑆)| is exactly the indicator of disagreement between preservation 28 outcomes. Hence,

E. Cohort Construction and Study Design 

E.1. Task selection 

Experiments focus on GQA and TextVQA. Preliminary work also considered RefCOCOg [38]. One input in the Qwen2.5-VL run produced an invalid dense output, and none of the six LLaVA interface-check inputs produced parseable co-ordinates. Localization measurements were subsequently discontinued. 

E.2. Original cohort and new images 

Sampling of the original cohort did not exclude all images used in histori-cal pilot experiments. The Qwen2.5-VL budget supplement and original-cohort prediction study were follow-up analyses conducted after inspecting some main-experiment results. The two prediction analyses use the same images and selector 30 labels, assessing incremental sufficiency information through different feature rep-resentations and fitting procedures. Images, questions, and analyses for the new cohort were specified before mea-surement. Final predictors for the original cohort were trained on September 24, 2026 and applied directly to the new cohort later that day. Nested refitting within the same new cohort was performed the following day. Direct evaluation tests the original predictive relationships on new images; refitting evaluates newly fitted predictors on that same image set. Direct comparison of image IDs excluded 725 previously used IDs recorded in 73 readable historical sources, together with the original training images for all three models. This comparison cannot cover historical exploration absent from those records. Each model completed 96 question–answer pairs, 672 conditions, and 2,016 main-selector records, with no measurement failures or replacement images. The 12 images for single-token summaries were selected before measure-ment. 31
