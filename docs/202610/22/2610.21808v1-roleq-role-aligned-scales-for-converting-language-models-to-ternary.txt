# ABSTRACT 

Weight quantization below four bits shrinks memory and bandwidth at a perplexity that looks acceptable, but often at the cost of factual knowledge and task accuracy. Ternary weights have mostly been obtained by training from scratch or long fine-tuning. We introduce RoleQ, a quantization framework that places scales by the functional roles of Transformer units rather than by the layout of the weight ma-trix, as conventional formats do. Vectors that read from the residual stream take one scale per output unit; those that write to it, one per input unit. The rule is structural, needs no search, calibration or stored state, and holds on integer and ternary grids. In a rotated basis it matches a per-layer axis search at half the solver work, and at ternary it cuts perplexity by up to half against layout-based placement in two families at every size from 0.6B to 14B, where per-row scales leave MMLU at chance. Our lightweight post-training pipeline converts an existing checkpoint to 1.59 bits per weight in a few GPU-hours, a fraction of the cost of learned ternarization, and leads every post-training ternary method at matched or lower memory-resident bits on perplexity, zero-shot accuracy and MMLU, most widely on knowledge. A sparse second ternary plane placed by a per-unit Fisher saliency keeps the matmul multiplication-free and recovers what ternary loses first: at 8B, a fraction of a bit returns about half the MMLU that conversion costs, well ahead of random or Hessian-based placement at equal bits. 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Weight memory and bandwidth constrain language-model deployment on phones and in browsers. At batch size one, decoding is typically bandwidth-bound, making bits per weight a key determinant of latency (Wang et al., 2025; Wei et al., 2025), motivating ultra-low-bit quantization. Below four bits, however, acceptable perplexity can conceal severe losses in factual knowledge and task accu-racy. We therefore evaluate quantization with three complementary measures: WikiText2 perplexity, a seven-task zero-shot average and five-shot MMLU. Ternary weights replace weight multiplications with additions and subtractions. Pretraining (Wang et al., 2023; Ma et al., 2024), quantization-aware training (Liu et al., 2025c), code fine-tuning (Ma-linovskii et al., 2024) and learned ternarization over many epochs (Wang et al., 2026c;b) preserve much of the model’s knowledge at substantial computational cost. Post-training conversion (Zhang et al., 2026; Yan et al., 2026a; Guo et al., 2026) is much cheaper but loses factual knowledge: on Qwen3-8B-Base, these methods score 26–35 on MMLU, versus 77 at full precision. The challenge is to approach training-based retention at post-training conversion cost. Our central insight is that quantization should allocate bits by function. We introduce RoleQ, a format that assigns scales to functional units: vectors that read from the residual stream receive one scale per output unit; vectors that write to it receive one per input unit. This structural rule applies to integer and ternary grids and requires neither calibration data nor stored axis choices. RoleQ-T combines this format with a data-free learned rotation, ternary GPTQ (Frantar et al., 2023) with an exact per-vector step, and brief scale distillation with frozen codes (Figure 2). Qwen3-8B-Base (Yang et al., 2025) reaches 1.59 bits per weight (bpw), 11.33 perplexity, 60.3 zero-shot accuracy and 47.6 MMLU in a few GPU-hours, a fraction of the tens of A100-hours for CAT-Q’s learned ternarization at this size (Section 4.1). 1Figure 1: RoleQ aligns scales with the roles. (A) Readers read from the residual stream through rows, writers write to it through columns. (B) RoleQ places the scales on the unit axis, per row for readers and per column for writers, never on the stream axis. (C) Qwen3-14B, ternary at equal bits: the placement alone lifts MMLU from 25.4 (chance) with per-output-channel scales to 38.5. Functional units also guide the allocation of extra bits. At a tight budget, selecting units by quantization-error magnitude can perform worse than random; per-unit Fisher saliency estimates how strongly each unit’s error affects the loss. RoleQ-T therefore optionally adds a sparse second ternary plane to Fisher-selected units, preserving multiplication-free matrix products. At 2.16 bpw, Qwen3-8B-Base reaches 9.30 perplexity, 65.9 zero-shot accuracy and 62.8 MMLU. On post-trained Qwen3-8B, 1.99 bpw surpasses CAT-Q on all three measures for 0.28 additional bits. We make three contributions. 1. Role-aligned scale placement (Section 3.1). In a rotated basis, RoleQ matches AdaDim’s (Heo et al., 2024) reconstruction quality at half the solver work of a two-way search, without cali-bration data or stored axis choices. Per-vector INT4 matches MXFP4 at 8% fewer bits; ternary perplexity improves over the best uniform placement by up to 2×, with gains at every tested size from 0.6B to 14B across two families. At 14B, RoleQ raises MMLU from 25.4 (chance) with per-output-channel scales to 38.5. 2. Efficient ternary conversion (Sections 3.2–3.4). RoleQ-T outperforms Qronos, PT 2-LLM and QTEA on all three measures at matched or lower memory-resident rates, with the largest gains on knowledge. At 14B, it surpasses QTEA’s released checkpoint after scale distillation using 0.67 fewer bpw. Applying the same distillation to PT 2-LLM leaves it over four perplexity points behind at a higher rate. 3. Fisher-guided secondary planes (Section 3.5). At 8B, +0.57 bpw recovers about half the MMLU lost on Base. On the post-trained model at 1.99 bpw, Fisher selection beats random by 6.2 points and Hessian ×weight by 9.0. 

# 2 RELATED WORK 

Ternary weights and conversion. Ternary weights {− 1, 0, +1 } date at least to Hwang & Sung (2014); ternary weight networks (Li et al., 2016) popularised the scaled ternary code with a thresh-old rule. Following binary BitNet (Wang et al., 2023), BitNet b1.58 (Ma et al., 2024) showed that language models pretrained with this alphabet can match full-precision counterparts at scale while enabling multiplication-free matrix products. Converting existing checkpoints avoids pretraining from scratch, but the computational cost varies. Quantization-aware training, distillation and code fine-tuning (Liu et al., 2025c; Wu et al., 2025; Malinovskii et al., 2024) still update weights or codes. Learned ternarization also involves sustained optimization: CAT-Q (Wang et al., 2026c) uses 512 calibration samples but optimises its codes for 60 epochs, while ScaleQ-1.58 (Wang et al., 2026b) uses the model’s own reasoning traces. Although CAT-Q is described as post-training quantization, we distinguish its extended code optimization from the lightweight conversion setting considered here. Two-bit formats. QuIP# (Tseng et al., 2024a) and QTIP (Tseng et al., 2024b) use lattice and trellis codebooks, respectively, achieving strong two-bit results on Llama. GuidedQuant’s loss-guided Hessian (Kim et al., 2025) and YAQA’s end-to-end adaptive rounding (Tseng et al., 2026) build on QTIP’s trellis codes. QTIP adopts QuIP#-style fine-tuning, reported at 50–100 GPU-hours for a 70B model. These methods provide strong quality references. Our format is scalar and codebook-free, and its ternary codes also support multiplication-free matrix products. For a comparison within this format class, our equal-bits controls reproduce the uniform grouped grids used by GPTQ (Frantar et al., 2023) and EfficientQAT (Chen et al., 2025). 

Scale axis and rotation. Existing methods choose scale axes empirically or refine scales within a solver. AdaDim (Heo et al., 2024) selects per-input or per-output scales for each module through a reconstruction test without rotation, and reports lower quality when per-input scales are applied uniformly rather than selectively. ShiftAddLLM (You et al., 2024) refits column scales during the solver’s sweep; Z-FOLD (Jeon et al., 2023) adds a folded in-channel scale on a second axis, and D2Quant (Yan et al., 2026b) adds one for a single matrix type. We adopt Z-FOLD’s closed-form 

H-metric refit for readers. These methods use calibration data and do not derive scale placement from vector roles. RoleQ instead selects the axis structurally from each vector’s reader or writer role, without calibration or stored axis flags. Our rotations retain the slots and parametrization of QuaRot (Ashkboos et al., 2024) and SpinQuant (Liu et al., 2025b), using SpinQuant’s notation. OptRot (Gadhikar et al., 2025) and TWLA learn rotations without data, whereas HARP (Zagitov et al., 2026) learns a stored rotation from calibration data before codebook quantization. Our rotation contribution is the choice of objective, evaluated against those of OptRot and TWLA. 

Saliency and mixed allocation. BiLLM (Huang et al., 2024) and ARB-LLM (Li et al., 2025) as-sign residual binary planes to columns selected by curvature scores. FGMP (Hooper et al., 2025) allocates precision using P g2δ2 with a single global percentile, an allocator we adopt at unit granu-larity. MixLLM (Zheng et al., 2026), SqueezeLLM (Kim et al., 2024) and TaCQ (Xiao et al., 2025a) use gradient, Fisher and task-conditioned scores. We condition our score on factual recall and find that, at tight budgets, curvature-based selection can perform worse than random placement. 

# 3 METHOD 

RoleQ places quantization scales by the functional roles of weight vectors on both integer and ternary grids (Section 3.1). RoleQ-T (Sections 3.2–3.4) converts an existing checkpoint to this ternary format in a few GPU-hours, combining a data-free learned rotation, GPTQ adapted to role-aligned scales and brief scale distillation with frozen codes. Optional Fisher-placed secondary planes (Section 3.5) allocate additional precision to units selected by loss sensitivity. Figure 2 summarises the pipeline. Rates count log 2 L bits per code plus all strictly necessary decoding metadata (resident bpw, Appendix A). 3.1 ROLE Q: R OLE -A LIGNED SCALES 

RoleQ assigns one scale to each vector that reads from the residual stream (a row of WQ, WK ,

WV , Wgate , Win ) or writes to it (a column of WO , Wout ). This rule follows a mechanistic view of the Transformer: the residual stream is a shared communication channel, and components access it through dense read and write vectors. An MLP neuron has read vectors in Win and Wgate and a write vector in Wout , corresponding to the key and value of Geva et al. (2021). Attention reads through Q/K/V rows and writes through O columns, forming the QK and OV circuits of Elhage et al. (2021). These vectors interact through the stream (Knittel & Pfister, 2026); each has a magnitude, direction and functional role. Assigning each vector its own scale separates its magnitude from the direction approximated by its quantization codes. In contrast, a scale per residual coordinate (per-OC on writers, per-IC on readers) couples unrelated units through a shared coordinate. RoleQ therefore aligns the quantization grid with the network’s functional decomposition. In a rotated residual basis, this distinction remains intact: the rotation mixes stream coordinates while preserving the unit axis (Section 3.2). Standard per-OC placement suits row-major storage and W8A8 accumulators. Weight-only decod-ing also permits input-axis scales, applied to activations once per token at O(d) cost for input width 

d, though per-IC placement has remained rare. Prior methods choose the axis by layout or search rather than function. RoleQ uses one rule on integer and ternary grids, readers scale per output unit, writers per input unit (Figure 1), with no search, calibration pass or stored per-layer flag. Writer scales lie on the solver’s sweep axis, requiring the adaptation in Section 3.3. At inference, they simply rescale the input activations. 3.2 ROTATIONS 

RoleQ-T rotates weights before quantization using QuaRot’s rotation slots in SpinQuant’s nota-tion (Ashkboos et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025b): r1 on the residual basis, r2 mixing V →O per key/value head, and r4 on the MLP intermediate dimension (Appendix F). Rotations reduce the impact of outliers on low-bit quantization (Chee et al., 2023; Ashkboos et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025b): a random Hadamard alone lowers 1.7B ternary PTQ perplexity from 1245.9 to 119.8. We therefore use a rotated residual basis throughout our quantization runs, placement controls and rerun baselines; only the basis control includes unrotated models. Our contribution concerns the rota-tion objective. Scale distillation (+SD) refits only the scales, parametrised as log a, and norm gains, 0.02–0.08% of the parameters depending on model size and plane budget (0.024% for the 8B body). It min-imises KL divergence from the full-precision teacher’s next-token distribution while keeping codes frozen, preserving the per-unit ternary format. Before quantization, rotation folds RMSNorm’s per-coordinate gains into adjacent weights. SD reintroduces a trainable gain vector at each norm site (two per block and the final norm, +0.0007 bpw), initialised to one, allowing the student to rescale residual coordinates. We use only unlabelled data: 400 steps at batch size 8, or 3,200 windows of 512 tokens from RedPajama-1T-Sample. The teacher’s final residuals are cached once during quan-tization and mapped to logits by its frozen output head, avoiding teacher forward passes during SD. At 8B, the stage takes 2 h on one A100 (Appendix D), against 8–64 A100-hours for CAT-Q’s 60-epoch stage at this size and 160 for ScaleQ-1.58’s (Wang et al., 2026c;b); QuIP#-style fine-tuning, which QTIP adopts, is reported at about 100 A100-hours for a 70B model (Tseng et al., 2024a;b), and PV-Tuning reports days on eight GPUs at 70B (Malinovskii et al., 2024). Applying the same SD to a baseline in Section 4.1 tests whether distillation alone explains the recipe’s advantage. 3.5 FISHER -P LACED SPARSE SECONDARY PLANES 

Sparse secondary planes target the task accuracy and factual knowledge lost in ternary conversion. Existing methods add precision more broadly or on a fixed axis. PTQTP (Xiao et al., 2025b) gives every weight a second ternary plane. TWLA’s released code (Zhao et al., 2026), which matches its reported perplexity and task numbers, adds a second plane to 26% of weights, unmentioned in the paper; and QTEA (Guo et al., 2026) adds FP8 residuals to salient columns, the latter two using layer-local saliency. We instead select whole functional units by loss sensitivity: the scaled vectors of Eq. 2, in every matrix the rows of a reader or the columns of a writer, i.e., neurons and attention dimensions that read from or write to the residual stream. # 4 EXPERIMENTS 

Models and data. We quantize Qwen3-0.6B, 1.7B, 8B and 14B Base and post-trained Qwen3-1.7B and 8B (Yang et al., 2025), with Llama-3.1-8B-Base (Grattafiori et al., 2024) as a cross-family check and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct as the matched-bits comparison with QTIP’s and GuidedQuant’s released checkpoints (Appendix Table 10). Base calibration uses 512 unlabelled C4 sequences of 512 tokens (Raffel et al., 2020). Post-trained models use 272 chat sequences of up to 1536 tokens, half UltraChat conversations (Ding et al., 2023) and half self-generated responses. 

Evaluation. We report WikiText2 perplexity, the average of seven zero-shot tasks (PIQA, ARC-Easy, ARC-Challenge, HellaSwag, WinoGrande, BoolQ and OpenBookQA; Bisk et al., 2020; Clark et al., 2018; Zellers et al., 2019; Sakaguchi et al., 2020; Clark et al., 2019; Mihaylov et al., 2018) under lm-eval (Gao et al., 2026), and five-shot MMLU (Hendrycks et al., 2021). These measures capture complementary effects: modest perplexity changes can accompany substantial losses in factual knowledge (Jaiswal et al., 2024), which perplexity measures only coarsely and quantization degrades on its own axis (Namburi et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2026a), while matching aggregate scores can conceal disagreement on individual predictions (Dutta et al., 2024). 

Comparison protocol. We report memory-resident information bits, including decoding metadata, name rate and stage differences, evaluate baseline reruns and released checkpoints in our harness against published results, and treat our seed spreads as descriptive. Further details are given in Appendix B. 4.1 TERNARY CONVERSION RECIPE 

We first ask what quality a few GPU-hours of ternary conversion can retain. Table 1 compares RoleQ-T with ternary methods whose outputs we could evaluate and memory-resident rates establish (Qronos, PT 2-LLM, QTEA, CAT-Q and TWLA; Zhang et al., 2026; Yan et al., 2026a; Guo et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026c; Zhao et al., 2026), using each baseline’s best available stage. On Qwen3-8B-Base, RoleQ-T reaches 1.590 bpw with scale distillation in three to four A100 hours, and 2.157 bpw with probing and planes in under ten. Four-level (INT2) grids provide a separate equal-bits comparison in Sections 4.2 and 4.3. At 1.590 bpw, RoleQ-T after scale distillation leads Qronos, PT 2-LLM and QTEA on all three measures while using matched or fewer bits: the baselines require 1.591, 1.835 and 2.281 bpw, 6respectively. Adding planes improves all three scores further, still below QTEA’s rate. Knowledge retention distinguishes these methods particularly clearly. Without our distillation, the baselines score 25.9–34.8 on MMLU, close to or modestly above 25% chance accuracy and far below 76.8 at full precision. RoleQ-T retains 47.6 at 1.590 bpw and 62.8 at 2.157 (56.0 before distillation). Perplexity alone therefore gives an incomplete account of the knowledge lost during conversion. Two comparisons delimit this result. First, shared scale distillation does not close the gap: applying it to PT 2-LLM’s codes yields 15.51 perplexity at 1.835 bpw, versus 11.33 for RoleQ-T at 1.590 (Table 1). The advantage thus survives the same distillation procedure, although the quantization recipes differ. Second, comparison with learned ternarization entails a budget trade-off. CAT-Q optimises its codes for 60 epochs on 512 C4 samples, 8–64 A100-hours at 8B by its own bracket (Appendix A), and its released checkpoint scores 52.9 MMLU at 1.71 bpw on post-trained Qwen3-8B on our harness. Our planes reach 61.8 at 1.99 bpw in under ten GPU-hours: +8.9 points for 0.28 additional bits. At CAT-Q’s own 1.71 bpw, the corresponding RoleQ-T-g128 leads it on perplexity (17.00 vs 27.68) and narrowly on zero-shot (61.7 vs 61.0) and ties on MMLU (53.1 vs 52.9). The gains extend to 14B. With planes and scale distillation, RoleQ-T reaches 8.05 perplexity ( 1.26 ×

full precision), 69.2 zero-shot and 68.3 MMLU at 2.156 bpw, versus 11.78, 56.6 and 47.8 for QTEA’s released checkpoint at 2.26. At the matched PTQ stage, before distillation, the planes run still leads on all three measures (10.11, 65.7 and 62.0), with each gap at least 19 × our seed range. This includes WikiText2, QTEA’s calibration corpus. The one-plane run at 1.589 bpw also leads after distillation (9.53 / 65.4 / 59.7), saving 0.67 bpw, but trails at PTQ. The lower-rate advantage therefore depends on distillation; the planes advantage holds at both stages. TWLA (Zhao et al., 2026) is a higher-rate reference: it releases no checkpoint, and its released code, which reproduces its reported Qwen3-8B perplexity, builds a masked two-plane model its paper does not describe, resident at 4.15–4.65 bpw, roughly twice our planes rate (Table 1, Appendix A). Section 5 discusses the stronger trellis-coded results at matched bits on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (Appendix Table 10). 4.2 THE PLACEMENT RULE 

Table 2 isolates scale placement: within each grid and model, the placements share the solver, random Hadamard r1, calibration draw and paired seeds, with no distillation. At ternary, RoleQ has the lowest mean perplexity at every tested size in both families, improving on the best uniform placement by up to 2×, with the same ordering on every seed from 1.7B upward. At 14B, the gain also appears on both task measures: 52.3 versus 45.6 zero-shot and 38.5 versus chance-level 25.4 MMLU for per-OC (Appendix Table 7). The 0.6B and Qwen-8B perplexity gaps remain within the largest seed range. Integer grids show the same mean perplexity ordering, but the margin depends on alphabet size and model. On the four-level grid, the best uniform placement has 1.19 × RoleQ’s perplexity at 1.7B and 1.22 × on Llama-8B, versus about 1.01 × at Qwen-8B and 14B. At INT3 and INT4, the largest gains occur at 1.7B: ratios of 2.19 × and 1.14 ×, respectively. Placement thus matters most at ternary, where the per-unit scale is a unit’s only continuous degree of freedom; here the benefit persists to the largest tested model in each family. Two practical comparisons follow. RoleQ-INT4 matches or improves on MXFP4 (Open Compute Project, 2023; Rouhani et al., 2023) perplexity at 0.6B, 1.7B and 8B while using 8% fewer bits (information bits, Appendix A). The rule also comes within 0.023% of AdaDim’s (Heo et al., 2024) searched H-metric reconstruction error at half the solver work of a two-way GPTQ search, without calibration data for axis selection or stored axis flags. This is a reconstruction comparison, not an end-to-end AdaDim evaluation. RoleQ-T outperforms the tested ternary conversion baselines at matched or lower memory-resident rates, with the largest gains in factual knowledge, which perplexity alone does not capture. Fisher-guided secondary planes further recover knowledge by directing a fraction of a bit to loss-sensitive units. Together, these results offer a practical route to lower memory use and multiplication-free ternary matrix products while preserving useful model capabilities. These findings have a defined scope. The placement rule is supported in a rotated basis; its advantage does not extend to native coordinates. Four Qwen3 sizes and one Llama model do not establish a general scaling trend, and the best uniform placement varies with size and family. Fisher selection’s advantage over random placement narrows with increasing budget in the 1.7B sweep. The gains from adding planes are also generally smaller at 14B than at 8B at the same total rate. At 2 bpw on Llama, QTIP’s trellis codes, alone and with GuidedQuant’s Hessian (Tseng et al., 2024b; Kim et al., 2025), remain ahead (Appendix Table 10), so we do not claim parity with this codebook class. Our evaluations do not cover generation or reasoning, including long chains of thought, where quantization introduces distinct failure modes (Liu et al., 2025a). Our results are weight-only (A16) and measure storage and quality, not inference speed. The ternary inner loop requires no multiplier, codebook or group metadata, making evaluation with lookup-table kernels (Wang et al., 2025; Wei et al., 2025) a natural next step (Appendix A). The broader lesson concerns allocation. At very low precision, both scale sharing and the placement of additional bits shape knowledge retention. Under tight budgets, allocating precision to the right units can matter more than increasing the overall rate. # A NOMENCLATURE AND BIT ACCOUNTING 

Nomenclature. INT b denotes a grid with 2b levels; our symmetric per-vector grids INT3sym / INT4sym / INT8sym have 7 / 15 / 255 levels, so INT4sym costs log 2 15 bits plus one fp16 scale per vector, 3.91 bpw (4.00 in storage bits, so its 8% saving against MXFP4’s 4.25 in Section 4.2 is 6% in storage bits). We write ternary for the three-level code and reserve INT2 for four-level grids, namely our exact-grid control and the external 2-bit methods. Rates are stated in information bits (log 2 L per code) so that a ternary total compares directly with an integer one. We quantize weights only (W ·A16): activations, embeddings and the head stay in fp16, as in every method we compare against (Qwen3-8B: 13.9 GB bf16 body, 1.38 GB at 1.59 bpw, 2.49 GB of fp16 embeddings and head). 

Scale granularity. Scales are shared per tensor, per output channel (one per row of [out , in] ), per (row, group of g) or per input channel (one per column). Side information amortises over the group as b + (16 + bz )/g bpw: INT2-g128 with fp16 scale and zero point is 2.156, MXFP4 is 

4 + 8 /32 = 4 .25 (Open Compute Project, 2023; Rouhani et al., 2023), INT4-g128 is 4.125, and one fp16 scale per vector is 16 /d , which vanishes (0.004 at d = 4096 ). Every dense matrix has a stream axis ( dmodel ) and a unit axis (heads × dhead , or dff ): five kinds read from the residual stream through their rows ( WQ, WK , WV , Wgate , Win ) and two write to it through their columns ( WO ,

Wout ), so per-OC is the unit axis on readers and the stream axis on writers, and per-IC the reverse. 

Resident terms. The information rate counts log 2 L per code plus every term a decoder needs to produce the weight: scales, zero points, act-order indices, group or plane membership, per-group affine terms, rotations that are neither folded into the weights nor regenerable from a seed, and reintroduced norm gains. Folded or seed-regenerable rotations cost 0 bpw. Packing (5 trits per byte = 1.6, 2 bits per trit = 2.0) can only add to the information rate and is not charged. We apply one test to every claimed rate, our own included: can a decoder produce the weight from the counted bits alone? By this test the per-vector scales and reintroduced norm gains move RoleQ-T from 1.585 to 1.590 bpw; TWLA’s per-(row, group) α and μ and its mask membership are consumed at inference, which places it at 4.15–4.65; and QTEA’s 1.7-bit payload is resident at 2.28. Table 5 gives the arithmetic term by term. 16 Stages. We distinguish five stages of increasing cost: calibration-free PTQ; calibrated PTQ (GPTQ); scale distillation , which re-fits the scales and norm gains to the fp model’s next-token distribution with the codes frozen (0.02–0.08% of the parameters, 400 steps); fine-tuning of codes or weights with the quantizer in the loop (Malinovskii et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2026c; Tseng et al., 2024b), from tens of A100-hours at 8B (CAT-Q) to about 160 (ScaleQ-1.58) and about 100 at 70B for QuIP#-style fine-tuning (Section 3.4); and training from scratch (Wang et al., 2023). Tables 1 and 4 record the stage of every run, and every comparison in the paper is either stage-aligned or names both stages. 

The hardware case. Ternary weights offer two savings rather than one. (i) Memory traffic scales with bpw; it is identical for every format at equal bits and is the saving today’s GPUs realise. (ii) An integer weight at any width still multiplies (dequantize-and-FMA or an integer MAC, plus a scale and zero point every group), whereas a ternary weight adds the activation, subtracts it or skips it: no multiplier, no codebook and no group metadata in the inner loop. The per-vector scale costs one multiply per output or input coordinate, O(d) per token against O(d2) additions, and a second plane costs two accumulations and an add. Binary weights share this property but have no zero; four-level and wider grids do not have it. The second saving is realised today on CPUs by lookup-table kernels (Wang et al., 2024; 2025; Wei et al., 2025) and is the premise of add-only hardware, for which BitNet b1.58 estimates about 70 × lower matmul arithmetic energy than fp16 at 7 nm (Ma et al., 2024). We report no latency or energy measurements of our own (Section 5).

Controls. Four controls separate the placement effect from what it could be confounded with. 

The code and the scale count. # E SPARSE PLANES : D ETAILS 

Refit by role. For readers the two scales of a unit with a second plane are re-solved jointly by an exact 2 × 2 weighted least squares in the Σ metric, which decouples per unit because Σ acts on the input axis. For writers there is no joint refit: H acts on the axis the scales index, so the exact refit is one coupled 2n × 2n system and a per-unit shortcut would cancel H out; the coupling is handled inside the GPTQ recursion instead. 

Probe protocol. The scored span is the chat-rendered answer tokens of a knowledge item (end marker included, as the evaluation scores it) or the second half of each 384-token C4 window; prompt tokens serve as context and are never scored. The Fisher is accumulated in fp32 over every quantized weight, one forward-backward pass per item over 720 items, and kept in two interleaved item halves for the split-half check (17 GB of host memory at 1.7B; probes at 8B and above need an H200). The budget over the one-plane body targets 2.156 bpw for the Base rows ( +0 .556 / 0.562 

/ 0.566 / 0.567 code bits at 0.6B / 1.7B / 8B / 14B and Llama-8B) and 1.994 for the post-trained rows ( +0 .40 ); (layer, kind) cells whose selected fraction falls below 2% are dropped as noise, which leaves about 180–200 cells with a second plane. The score is basis-specific for WV and WO under 

s2 and for Wout under r4, so the recipe-basis planes run recomputes its saliency rather than reusing the r1-basis ranking.

Basis labelling. Because the basis has a significant impact on the quantization results, we treat it as part of a run’s identity: every run quantized in the recipe basis s1(l4) + s2(opt) + r4 is labelled as such, runs from the two bases never share a table row, and no plane effect is computed across them. 

r4 uses a block size of 1024 at 1.7B and 8B; its runtime Hadamard is the one a W2A8 path would reuse. 

Why the placement rule depends on the rotation. The structural reason given in Section 3.2, that r1 mixes the residual-stream axis and leaves the unit axis untouched, makes two predictions that the appendix tests. First, the one slot that does mix a unit axis, r4 on the columns of Wout ,24 should compress the writer half of the margin specifically; it does, from 1.63 × to 1.17 × at 1.7B on both seeds (Table 13d). Second, the axis a reconstruction-error search prefers should coincide with the rule’s only after rotation; AdaDim’s argmin agrees with the rule on 96.4% of cells rotated and on 14.3% unrotated (Table 8).
