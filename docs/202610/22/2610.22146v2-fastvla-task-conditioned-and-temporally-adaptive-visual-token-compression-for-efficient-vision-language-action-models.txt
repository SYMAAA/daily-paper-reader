# FastVLA: Task-Conditioned and Temporally Adaptive Visual Token Compression for Efficient Vision-Language-Action Models 

## Jiuyi Xu Colorado School of Mines Chengyuan Qian Texas A&M University Zhengzhong Tu Texas A&M University Yang Sui Microsoft AI Yangming Shi Colorado School of Mines 

## Abstract 

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models present a promis-ing paradigm for general-purpose robot manipulation, but closed-loop deployment is often limited by inference effi-ciency: at each timestep, high-resolution, often multi-view images are encoded into hundreds of visual tokens that dominate transformer computation and latency. Token com-pression via attention is a natural solution but is challeng-ing for VLA control because (1) decision-critical tokens are determined by the task, not generic visual saliency, and (2) the token budget varies substantially across steps, so static metrics and budgets waste computation or discard control-critical tokens. We introduce FastVLA, a training-free, task-aware, and temporally adaptive visual token com-pression framework. At each step, FastVLA scores token importance via task-to-vision attention and adaptively sets the compression rate from the importance-distribution uni-formity, measured by an inverse-Simpson metric with on-line sliding-window calibration. We evaluate FastVLA on OpenVLA, OpenVLA-OFT, and CogACT across LIBERO and SIMPLER, and on a physical Franka Research 3 robot. In simulation, it reduces LLM-backbone FLOPs by 35-59% and action latency by 9-32% (a 1.09 ×-1.46 × speedup) while maintaining task success, achieving a comparable or better efficiency-performance trade-off than existing plug-and-play token compression/pruning/reuse baselines. On the real robot, it cuts action-prediction latency by 20% (1.25 × speedup) at comparable success. Code is available at https://github.com/jiuyixu25/FastVLA .

## 1. Introduction 

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have recently emerged as a compelling paradigm for general-purpose robot manipulation, enabling policies to predict low-level actions conditioned on natural-language tasks and visual observations [4, 7, 11, 15, 30]. By leveraging large-scale pretraining and large vision-language backbones, VLAs demonstrate strong generalization across objects, scenes, and task specifications, making them increasingly attrac-tive for deployment in versatile manipulation systems. De-spite their impressive capabilities, deploying these mod-els in closed-loop control is often limited by inference ef-ficiency [9]. At every control step, high-resolution (e.g., 224 ×224) and frequently multi-view observations are en-coded into hundreds of visual tokens [3, 16] that domi-nate the compute and latency of the vision-language-model (VLM) backbone during action prediction, far outweighing the few text tokens of a short task. Yet not all visual tokens contribute equally to action pre-diction. In a typical manipulation step, only a small subset of tokens carries decision-critical information (e.g., the tar-get object/place or the gripper–object contact region), while the rest are redundant or even distracting [12, 26, 27]. Re-ducing the number of visual tokens processed by the large-language-model (LLM) backbone, without sacrificing task performance, has therefore become a key direction for ac-celerating VLA inference [22, 23, 25]. However, directly applying VLM-specific token com-pression methods [5, 8, 29] to robotic control is often sub-optimal, because these methods are designed for single-shot vision-language understanding, where the objective is to preserve global semantic content for perception tasks such as detection, captioning, or generation. Unlike image-based VLMs that process a single image, or video-based VLMs that encode a fixed sequence of frames, a VLA interacts with the environment through actions that directly shape its subsequent observations: at each step the agent receives a new observation conditioned on its previous action and predicts the next one. Visual tokens are thus not repre-1arXiv:2610.22146v2 [cs.RO] 19 Oct 2026 sentations of a static scene but components of a sequential decision process , which sets token compression for VLA apart from its VLM counterpart in two respects. The first is that token relevance is governed by the task rather than by generic visual saliency (now standard practice in language-guided pruning), which we adopt as our importance signal rather than claim as a contribution. The second, and the focus of this work, is that the amount of decision-critical visual information varies drastically across control steps: some steps (e.g., target localization) rely on broad scene context, whereas others (e.g., grasping or contact) depend on a small, localized region. A single fixed compression rate is therefore inherently mismatched, wasting computa-tion when the useful information is concentrated, and dis-carding necessary context when broader information is re-quired. Motivated by this, we propose FastVLA, a training-free visual token compression framework for efficient VLA inference whose core is a temporally adaptive, per-step compression rate . At each step, FastVLA scores visual-token importance with task-to-vision attention which is a training-free signal that a controlled comparison shows is more reliable for action prediction than vision-to-vision or all-to-vision attention and then, crucially, decides how many tokens to keep from the shape of the resulting im-portance distribution. Specifically, it estimates the ef-fective number of tokens the decision depends on via an inverse-Simpson uniformity measure, and maps this quantity through an online sliding-window calibration to a timestep-dependent compression rate: when the current step concentrates on few tokens relative to its recent his-tory it prunes aggressively, and when the decision draws on broader context it prunes conservatively. By allocating the token budget where each decision needs it, FastVLA reduces LLM-backbone computation, speeds up action pre-diction, and improves closed-loop responsiveness, without caches or training. We evaluate FastVLA on three open-source VLA mod-els including OpenVLA [15], OpenVLA-OFT [16], and Co-gACT [17], across the widely used LIBERO [19] and SIM-PLER [18] benchmarks, and on a physical Franka Research 3 robot. In simulation, FastVLA reduces LLM-backbone FLOPs by 35-59% and action latency by 9-32% (up to a 

1.46 × speedup) while maintaining task success, yielding a comparable or even better efficiency-performance trade-off than existing training-free compression, pruning, and reuse baselines. On the real robot, it cuts action-prediction la-tency by 20% ( 1.25 × speedup) and raises the control fre-quency by up to 25% at comparable success. In summary, our contributions are: • We propose FastVLA, a training-free, plug-and-play visual token compression framework that accelerates closed-loop VLA inference. • We design a step-adaptive compression policy that sets a timestep-dependent token budget from the inverse-Simpson uniformity of the importance distribution under online sliding-window calibration. • We validate FastVLA on OpenVLA/OpenVLA-OFT (LIBERO), CogACT (SIMPLER), and a physical Franka Research 3 robot, achieving substantial compute and la-tency reductions in simulation and a 1.25 × real-robot speedup with comparable task success. 

## 2. Related Work 

2.1. Vision-Language-Action Models for Robot Ma-nipulation 

VLA models predict robot actions directly from natural-language tasks and visual observations. Early work such as SayCan [1] combines language-conditioned planning with learned low-level skills, while RT-1 [6] and RT-2 [30] scale language-conditioned visuomotor learning with large robot datasets and web-scale vision-language pretraining. In par-allel, PaLM-E [10] and Octo [21] highlight the benefits of large pretrained representations across tasks and embodi-ments. More recently, foundation VLA models including OpenVLA [15] and OpenVLA-OFT [16], CogACT [17], and π0 [4] / π0.5 [3] demonstrate strong robustness and open-world generalization via heterogeneous multi-robot data and transformer backbones. These billion-scale models encode high-resolution, often multi-view observations into long visual-token sequences at every control step, which motivates inference-time methods that remove redundant visual tokens while preserving task-critical information. 

2.2. Visual Token Compression for Efficient VLA Inference 

Because most VLA models [3, 4, 15–17] adopt VLM-style architectures, training-free token compressors built for VLMs are broadly applicable: they prune or merge vi-sual tokens by token similarity (ToMe [5]), shallow-layer or cross-modal attention (FastV [8], SparseVLM [29], Vis-Pruner [28]), or token diversity (DivPrune [2]). However, these methods are designed for single-image inference and apply a static pruning policy, so transferring them directly to VLA often degrades control performance. Recent work therefore targets VLA inference along three lines: reuse- and cache-based methods that ex-ploit cross-step temporal redundancy (VLA-Cache [26], FlashVLA [23]); importance-estimation methods that add task-, phase-, or motion-aware cues (SpecPrune-VLA [25], ADP [22], VLA-Pruner [20]); and hybrid or learned schemes that go beyond pruning (EfficientVLA [27], LightVLA [12]). These methods still share limitations that FastVLA over-comes. Reuse and caching introduce cross-step state and 2Figure 1. Overview of FastVLA. At each action-prediction step, FastVLA scores visual tokens with task-conditioned attention, measures the uniformity of the top-scoring importance distribution, and sets the step’s compression rate through an online sliding-window calibration, reducing LLM-backbone computation while preserving action performance. 

cache-management overhead, so wall-clock gains can lag FLOP reductions; learned schemes such as LightVLA re-quire training and are tied to a specific backbone, al-though at equal success they can reach a better com-pute–latency point than training-free methods. In addition, some VLM/VLA compression algorithms commit to a fixed compression ratio, adapting (if at all) only through auxil-iary external signals such as end-effector motion. FastVLA differs on three axes: it is training-free, pruning indepen-dently at each step without caches; it conditions token im-portance on the language task; and, crucially, it makes the compression rate itself adaptive, reading the per-step con-centration of task-relevant evidence via an inverse-Simpson uniformity signal calibrated against a short temporal win-dow, spending tokens where the decision needs them and pruning harder where it does not. 

## 3. Method 

We propose FastVLA, a temporally adaptive and training-free visual token compression method that reduces the num-ber of visual tokens processed by the VLA model’s LLM backbone (Fig. 1). FastVLA proceeds in two stages: it first scores visual-token importance with a task-conditioned at-tention signal, and then, as our core contribution, sets how many tokens to keep adaptively at each step from the con-centration of that importance distribution. We present both stages below. 

3.1. Preliminary: Visual Token Compression in VLA Models 

Methods that reduce the visual-token cost of a VLA back-bone fall into three largely orthogonal families: token prun-ing discards a subset of visual tokens, shortening the se-quence seen by later layers; token merging pools simi-lar tokens into fewer representatives; and reuse/caching 

recomputes only the tokens that changed across control steps, reusing cached token or key-value states for the rest. FastVLA belongs to the pruning family: at every action step it performs a hard, task-conditioned top-K selection of vi-sual tokens, with the retained budget set adaptively per step. 

3.2. Task-conditioned visual token importance 

A VLA policy is explicitly conditioned on the language task, so visual-token usage is driven by the task prompt rather than by generic visual saliency: the tokens that matter are those covering the target object/place and the interaction region named by the task. The design question is therefore 

which attention signal best captures this task-driven impor-tance. Since selecting tokens by attention is already stan-dard in VLM compression, we do not propose it as new but instead re-evaluate three candidate signals for VLA control: 

all-to-vision (naive cross-modal attention from the fused representation to visual tokens), task-to-vision (task tokens attending to visual tokens), and vision-to-vision (visual self-attention capturing perceptual saliency). We progressively pruned visual tokens by each score and measured policy performance on LIBERO with OpenVLA-OFT (Fig. 2). Task-to-vision scoring was clearly the most robust: it retained near-saturated success under light-to-moderate compression (10%–50%) and preserved non-trivial performance even under aggressive compression ( ≥

70% ), whereas vision-to-vision and all-to-vision degraded much faster and approached failure at 90%. We therefore adopt task-to-vision attention as the importance signal in the remainder of the method. Formally, let T ∈ RM ×d and V ∈ RN ×d denote the task- and visual-token representations. 3.3. Adaptive Step-aware Compression Rate 

Having fixed which tokens matter, the central question of our method is how many to keep at each step. In a closed-loop rollout, successive steps rely on visual information to very different extents. For example, approaching or grasp-ing an object depends on a small localized region, whereas locating a target or verifying the scene requires broad con-text. Fig. 3 makes this concrete: within a single trial, the per-step importance distribution swings between nearly uni-form (many tokens contribute comparably) and highly con-centrated (a few tokens dominate). A fixed compression rate therefore wastes computation on concentrated steps and dis-cards needed context on broad ones; the rate should instead adapt to this per-step concentration. ## 4. Experiments 

To evaluate the effectiveness of FastVLA, we conduct experiments in both simulation and real-world environ-ments. We implement FastVLA on three open-source VLA models: OpenVLA [15], OpenVLA-OFT [16], and CogACT-base [17]. Specifically, we evaluate OpenVLA and OpenVLA-OFT on the LIBERO benchmark [19] and CogACT-base on the SIMPLER benchmark [18], and addi-tionally validate FastVLA on a physical Franka Research 3 robot, together covering a diverse set of manipulation tasks. All experiments are conducted on a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU. 

4.1. Experiment Setup 

Compared Methods. We compare FastVLA against a broad range of recent acceleration methods spanning three categories: (i) general VLM token-pruning methods, in-cluding FastV [8], SparseVLM [29], and DivPrune [2]; (ii) VLA-specific token compression, caching, and reuse methods, including VLA-Cache [26], EfficientVLA [27], FlashVLA [23], SpecPrune-VLA [25], and ADP [22]; and (iii) the learned token-pruning method LightVLA [12]. Among these, VLA-Cache and LightVLA provide pub-lished, open-source, and reproducible implementations, which we re-evaluate under our unified setup. For the re-maining methods (marked with ∗ in Tab. 1), we are unable to reproduce them in our environment and therefore quote their published results for reference only. 

Evaluation Metrics. We report success rate, FLOPs, la-tency, control frequency, and CUDA latency, together with the resulting speedup. FLOPs provides a hardware-agnostic estimate of the theoretical computation of the LLM back-bone. Latency denotes the total per-step action-prediction time. Control frequency is the number of environment ac-tions executed per second, H/ latency, where H is the num-ber of actions produced by one prediction call ( H=8 for OpenVLA-OFT, which predicts an action chunk per for-ward pass, and H=1 for OpenVLA and CogACT); it quan-tifies the real-time responsiveness of closed-loop control and makes chunked and single-step policies comparable on one axis. For chunked policies this is an action throughput computed from latency, not a measured rate: OpenVLA-OFT receives a new observation once per chunk, i.e., at most 1/latency ( 8.4→12 .3 Hz in Tab. 1). CUDA latency reports the LLM-backbone inference time on the GPU, and speedup is computed as the ratio of the per-backbone base-line latency to that of each method. 

Choice of ρ. ρ sets FastVLA’s average compute budget and is the only setting chosen per benchmark and backbone; 

τ =0 .1 and T =10 are shared by all experiments. For each suite (each task on SIMPLER) we report the most aggres-sive ρ on a 0.05 grid whose success stayed within 2 points of the uncompressed model.

## 5. Conclusion

Beyond scoring token im-portance with task-to-vision attention, its core idea is to reformulate per-step token budgeting as an online deci-sion, setting the compression rate from the inverse-Simpson concentration of the importance distribution under sliding-window calibration. ## A. Discussion on FLOPs vs. latency. 

We isolated every stage with CUDA-event timing, for FastVLA and for both re-run baselines (OpenVLA-OFT, 4-suite average; Tab. 7). Token scoring and selection add negligible cost: FastVLA’s scoring takes 1.5 ms, 1.8% of a step. The gap has three causes. (i) FLOPs counts only the LLM backbone, which is 55 .7% of the baseline step. (ii) Within the LLM stage, time falls sub-linearly in FLOPs because at batch size 1 the backbone is bandwidth- rather than compute-bound at these sequence lengths. (iii) The vi-sion encoder runs before compression and is unchanged by it; the residual 5–7% spread across methods is run-to-run variation, consistent with clock throttling under the heav-ier baseline step.
