ABSTRACT 

Group-wise post-training quantizers for large language models round weights onto a grid that is not refit to the resulting integer codes. We show that this leaves accuracy on the table: the best grid depends on the codes, input correlations couple the errors of different groups, and useful code changes often involve many codes at once. We propose JARQ ( Joint Alternating Refinement for Quantization), a plug-in refinement that starts from any group-wise quantizer and alternates a joint least-squares fit of all group scales with bounded Babai proposals that move many codes of a group together on the current grid. The problem is a bilinear box-constrained mixed-integer least-squares problem; the solver is backpropagation-free, does not increase the layer-wise objective under exact scale solves, and keeps the host’s bit width, groups, zero points, and inference cost. Across Llama-2, Llama-3, and Qwen models with RTN, GPTQ, OmniQuant, and AWQ hosts, JARQ lowers perplexity in 90 of 96 comparisons, cuts three-bit RTN perplexity by up to 36%, raises mean multiple-choice accuracy in 23 of 24 configurations, and improves QEP, QuaRot, and OJBKQ outputs, at under a minute per 7B block. Code will be available at https://github.com/Euphoria040201/JARQ .

1 INTRODUCTION 

Post-training quantization (PTQ) makes large language models cheaper to serve by storing weights in a few bits, using only a small calibration set (Frantar et al., 2022; Lin et al., 2024; Shao et al., 2023; Xiao et al., 2023; Ashkboos et al., 2024). At three or four bits, a group-wise quantizer stores each weight as an integer code on a grid set by a per-group scale. Widely used methods obtain the codes by rounding onto a grid: GPTQ fixes the grid and rounds with sequential error compensation, AWQ rounds after activation-aware scaling, and OmniQuant learns clipping thresholds by gradient descent and rounds each weight to the current grid (Frantar et al., 2022; Lin et al., 2024; Shao et al., 2023). The final grid is not refit to the codes it produces, and those codes are not revisited. Leaving the grid fixed is suboptimal, because the grid and the codes interact in three ways. First, the best grid depends on the codes: once some codes change, a different scale fits the layer output better, and on that grid other codes become optimal, so a grid fixed in advance goes stale ( grid staleness ). Second, the groups of a channel interact: correlated inputs mix their output errors, so the best scale of one group depends on the scales of all others ( cross-group coupling ). Third, useful code changes are often collective: within a group, correlated inputs create improvements that no single-code change can reach ( single-code stationarity ). OJBKQ captures the third effect by decoding many codes at once, but on a fixed, precomputed grid (Wang et al., 2026). Figure 1 illustrates the first and third effects on one group of two correlated weights, where refitting the scale unlocks a two-code move. We propose JARQ ( Joint Alternating Refinement for Quantization), a plug-in refinement for any group-wise quantizer. Starting from the host’s scales, codes, and zero points, it minimizes the layer’s output reconstruction error by alternating two updates: a joint least-squares fit of all group scales

2 RELATED WORK 

Layer-wise PTQ. GPTQ and OBC reduce layer-wise reconstruction error with second-order se-quential quantization (Frantar et al., 2022; Frantar & Alistarh, 2022); AWQ uses activation-aware scaling and OmniQuant learns clipping and equivalent transformations (Lin et al., 2024; Shao et al., 2023). QEP corrects the target for errors from preceding layers (Arai & Ichikawa, 2025), and QuaRot and SpinQuant rotate weights and activations (Ashkboos et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024). AdaRound and BRECQ learn rounding by layer or block reconstruction (Nagel et al., 2020; Li et al., 2021), and SignRound/AutoRound tunes rounding offsets and clipping with signed gradient descent (Cheng et al., 2024). These methods decide the target, the representation, or the initial codes; JARQ starts from their output and re-optimizes the grid and the codes together. 

Integer search on a lattice. GPTQ, run from the last to the first dimension, is Babai’s nearest-plane algorithm (Babai, 1986) on a lattice defined by the Hessian (Chen et al., 2026); QuIP relates its LDLQ rounding to GPTQ, and QuIP# quantizes to lattice codebooks (Chee et al., 2023; Tseng et al., 2024). OJBKQ applies Babai–Klein decoding with precomputed scales (Wang et al., 2026); Qronos and CoreQ improve sequential rounding with error correction (Zhang et al., 2026; Cha et al., 2026); ADMM-Q alternates continuous updates with discrete projections and notes that the grid can become stale, allowing one grid refresh (Lucas et al., 2026). The other methods decode on a grid that is fixed during the search. JARQ instead refits all group scales of a channel jointly by least squares at every iteration, so each new grid opens new code improvements (Proposition 3.1). 2Joint scale–code optimization. Alternating between codes and their coefficients goes back to multi-bit quantization of recurrent networks (Xu et al., 2018); HQQ fixes the scales and optimizes the zero points without calibration data by half-quadratic splitting (Badri & Shaji, 2023). For LLMs, QuantEase runs coordinate descent over codes on the layer reconstruction objective with the grid held fixed (Behdin et al., 2023). JARQ instead fits all group scales of a channel jointly under the output objective and moves a group’s codes as a block on each refitted grid via Babai proposals. 

3 METHODOLOGY 

We first write refinement of a quantized layer as one reconstruction problem over scales and codes (Sections 3.1 and 3.2). The problem mixes a continuous grid with discrete assignments, so we solve it by alternating two updates, each designed around one of the observations in Section 1: a joint scale fit that accounts for coupled groups, and a Babai proposal that moves several codes at once. We now approximately minimize (2). Scales determine the grid, and codes determine the assign-ments to it. A host settles the grid and rounds the codes onto it; but once the codes move, a different grid fits the output best, and on that grid different codes become optimal. JARQ therefore alternates the two: at iteration k, it refits S for the codes Q(k−1) , then updates Q on the new grid S(k). The objective in (4) separates over output columns, so each column can be refined independently. We call the PTQ method supplying the initial quantized layer the host . It may use rounding, error compensation, learned clipping, or another procedure. We require only its group-wise uniform representation (S(0) , Q(0) , Z), and initialize directly from these scales, codes, and zero points. The two updates are described below, followed by their core loop in Algorithm 1. 4 EXPERIMENTS 

We test perplexity across six models, four hosts, and two bit widths, then evaluate downstream accuracy and examine the sources of improvement, including layer-wise errors and cost. 4.1 SETUP 

We quantize Llama-2-7B/13B (Touvron et al., 2023), Llama-3-8B (Grattafiori et al., 2024), Qwen2.5-7B (Yang et al., 2024), and Qwen3-4B/8B (Yang et al., 2025) to W3A16 and W4A16 with groups of 128 weights, using 128 WikiText-2 training sequences of 2,048 tokens. The host is RTN, GPTQ (Frantar et al., 2022), OmniQuant (Shao et al., 2023) (weight clipping only), or AWQ (Lin et al., 2024); Base is its quantized model and +Ours is JARQ applied to it with the same bit width, groups, and zero points. All refined models use K = 3 iterations with early stopping and 

ν = 0 .6. We report WikiText-2 (WT2) (Merity et al., 2017) and C4 (Raffel et al., 2020) perplexity, five multiple-choice tasks, and generative accuracy on GSM8K and MATH-500 (Appendix A). 4.2 MAIN RESULTS

5 CONCLUSION 

Group-wise quantizers round weights onto a grid that is not refit to the resulting codes. JARQ refits all group scales jointly and moves codes in blocks on the new grid; code updates are more reliable after the grid has moved. It lowers perplexity in 90 of 96 comparisons, most at three bits, with no change to storage or inference cost. Its guarantee is per layer, and gains are smallest for strong hosts at four bits. This appendix specifies the quantization settings, the evaluated model states, and the quality and cost measurements. A.1 CALIBRATION AND HOST QUANTIZERS 

The four hosts in Table 1 use 128 WikiText-2 (WT2) training sequences of 2,048 tokens, seed 0, asymmetric weight quantization with group size g = 128 , 3-bit or 4-bit weights, and 16-bit activations. Quality is evaluated after reconstructing the quantized weights in the model’s floating-point dtype. RTN rounds weights to the quantization grid. GPTQ uses activation ordering and damping 0.1; refinement keeps its group assignments in the reordered coordinates and restores the original coor-dinates when writing back the weights. OmniQuant uses 20 epochs of learnable weight clipping, with learnable equivalent transformations disabled. AWQ uses its official scale search and quanti-zation routines with our calibration inputs. The Qwen OmniQuant and AWQ runs use native scaled dot-product attention. Refinement starts from the host’s scales, codes, and zero points. It updates scales and codes and keeps the zero points fixed. The reconstruction objective is (2) with ν = 0 .6. After each layer is refined, its outputs supply the inputs to the following layer. For every host, the final scales and codes define the returned weights. Numerical settings and early stopping are given in Appendix E. A.2 PERPLEXITY AND ITERATION BUDGET 

All main experiments use an iteration budget of K = 3 with early stopping. This choice follows the budget sweep on Llama-2 in Figure 6, where three iterations capture most of the WT2 perplexity reduction for RTN hosts, with smaller gains from additional iterations. Base denotes the host model (K = 0 ), and +Ours denotes the model refined with K = 3 . The QuaRot runs also use K = 3 and Algorithm 1, as detailed in Appendix E.4. The Llama-2-7B and Qwen3-8B RTN entries in Table 1 are the g = 128 , ν = 0 .6 states of the group-size and regularization ablations (Appendix F). 

Llama-3-8B. We use the original base checkpoint, meta-llama/Meta-Llama-3-8B . Ta-ble 1 reports all four hosts at W3A16 and W4A16. Within each configuration, WT2 and C4 are evaluated on the same model state. Table 2 evaluates the same states and the original FP16 model on five multiple-choice tasks. 

Qwen3-4B. We use Qwen/Qwen3-4B with native scaled dot-product attention. Table 1 includes the FP16 reference and all four hosts at W3A16 and W4A16. Perplexity is measured from the saved full-model states after all 36 Transformer blocks have been processed. Local gains hold on held-out data. Local NMSE decreases in 7,072 of 7,616 module pairs (92.9%) and in at least 90% of pairs for every host, including OmniQuant (Figures 8 and 9). The reduction in the calibration objective therefore carries over to held-out inputs. 

Propagation depends on the host. At the final block, block-output NMSE decreases in 23 of 32 comparisons: 8/8 for RTN, 7/8 for GPTQ, 6/8 for AWQ, and 2/8 for OmniQuant. A likely reason is that OmniQuant optimizes block outputs directly, and refinement toward the layer target can undo part of that compensation. For Llama-2-7B with OmniQuant at W3A16 on C4, all seven local NMSEs of the last block decrease, and down proj falls by 47.51% (Figure 8), while its propagated SSE rises by 37.72% (Figure 10) and the final block-output NMSE rises from 0.08358 to 0.10799. 

Two propagation-sensitive modules. All 14 of the 7,616 module pairs in which refinement more than doubles the propagated SSE come from the down proj of one early block per model: block 2 of Llama-2-7B and block 7 of Qwen3-8B. There, the host’s propagated SSE is 77–1,133 and 6–34 times that of the preceding block, and refinement raises it in 9 and 13 of the 16 host, bit-width, and corpus settings. At block 7 of Qwen3-8B, local NMSE still falls in all 13 of those settings: the refined weights fit better, but these modules amplify the small input drift left by the refined preceding blocks. The main target eXW ignores such drift by design; the propagation-corrected target of Appendix E.5 accounts for it.

Remark 2 (Relation to GPTQ) . Chen et al. (2026) show that GPTQ, executed from the last to the first dimension, is Babai’s nearest-plane algorithm for a closest-vector problem on a lattice defined by the Hessian, on a fixed quantization grid. The code update (10) applies the same recursion to a single group: the lattice is generated by si,j Ri at the current scale, and the target is the conditional residual (7), from which the latest contributions of all other groups are subtracted. With K = 0 ,

JARQ returns the host state, whether or not the host is GPTQ. The differences from GPTQ are as follows. (i) The grid changes: all scales of a column are refit jointly given the codes, and the codes are decoded again on the new grid. (ii) Each proposal is accepted only if it lowers Fj , which gives Theorem 1; GPTQ applies each rounding decision once. (iii) Decoding is repeated across groups and iterations, so a group’s codes respond to later changes in other groups. When the host is GPTQ, its codes are already nearest-plane points of a closely related lattice, which is consistent with the lower Babai improvement rate for GPTQ in Table 4 (Appendix G.4). The lattices differ in the calibration inputs, the regularization, and the grouping, so this is not an identity. 

G.8 COMPUTATIONAL COMPLEXITY 

Let d = din , p = dout , and assume equal-sized groups of g weights, so G = d/g . We count dense arithmetic for QR reduction followed by cached group factors and incremental residual updates. The counts cover one linear layer’s refinement; host quantization, activation collection, and model evaluation are separate costs. 

Shared preprocessing. Thin QR of the (n+d)×d augmented matrix costs O(( n+d)d2). Forming 

Y = UTY as in Algorithm 1, equivalently solving RTY = ATY, costs O(( n + d)dp + d2p). For the main target, Y = AW , so the identity Y = RW permits a target-projection shortcut costing 

O(d2p) instead. The orthogonal residual in (17) is zero in exact arithmetic for this target. QR factorization of each reduced d × g group matrix costs O(dg 2), or O(d2g) across all groups. These factors are reused across output columns and iterations. 

One iteration for one output column. Constructing Bj ∈ Rd×G costs O(d2). Forming its Gram matrix costs O(dG 2), and the scale solve costs O(G3), including the spectral check and Cholesky factorization. The right-hand side costs O(dG ). For code updates, cache the full residual and update it whenever a group proposal is accepted. Projection onto a group’s QR basis and updating its con-tribution each cost O(dg ); bounded Babai back substitution costs O(g2). A complete group sweep therefore costs O(d2 + dg ), dominated by the residual updates rather than the back substitution. 

Total time and workspace. For an iteration budget K ≥ 1, the resulting bound is

Processing one output column at a time requires an additional O(dG + G2 + d) workspace for the scale system and residuals, excluding the stored model, host states, calibration inputs, and outputs. Batching columns increases this workspace with the batch size.
