# Q-MINO: A Minimal-Norm Method for Quantization-Aware Training 

Don Li don@pdx.edu Department of Mathematics & Statistics Portland State University 

## Abstract 

The Straight-Through Estimator (STE) is a widely used heuristic for Quantization-Aware Training (QAT), but its surrogate gradients can exhibit substantial mismatch with the underlying quantized objective, leading to noisy updates and parameter oscillations, partic-ularly in ultra-low-bit regimes. We propose the Quantization-Aware Minimal-Norm Opti-mizer (Q-MINO), a temporal bundle method that combines gradient consensus, state-drift regularization, and an alignment constraint to construct stabilized, minimum-norm update directions from recent optimization states. Q-MINO solves the resulting constrained sub-problem using a warm-started Frank–Wolfe procedure with a feasible fallback initialization. Theoretically, via a stochastic Lyapunov Kurdyka–Łojasiewicz (KL) framework, we show that Q-MINO achieves asymptotic neighborhood convergence. Moreover, we detail numeri-cal experiments with Q-MINO at various quantizations. 

## 1 Introduction 

Modern deep learning has undergone a remarkable expansion in model capacity scaling, with large vision architectures and multi-billion-parameter language models achieving state-of-the-art (SOTA) capabilities across diverse domains [Brown et al. (2020); Kaplan et al. (2020)]. However, the deployment of such models is constrained by severe computational and infrastructure bottlenecks – memory footprint and memory bandwidth constraints [Dao et al. (2022); Pope et al. (2023)], as well as energy consumption [Strubell et al. (2019)], scale rapidly with parameter count. This entails a gap between high-capacity neural networks and resource-constrained deployment targets such as edge devices, consumer hardware, and low-latency inference servers [Banbury et al. (2021)]. Quantization is widely-used as a compression method to address this gap with respect to (large-scale) deep learning models [Gholami et al. (2021)]. Quantization maps high-precision floating-point parameters (e.g., FP32) to low-bit fixed-point representations (e.g., INT8, INT4, or INT2), directly reducing model memory footprint and unlocking accelerated integer arithmetic hardware units. Quantization approaches generally fall into two categories: Post-Training Quantization (PTQ) , which operates directly on pre-trained floating-point weights without full retraining, offering a lightweight and low-compute compression route, and Quantization-Aware Training (QAT) , which simulates low-bit discrete constraints during the training loop, allowing model parameters to adapt to quantization errors. PTQ has the benefits of training speed and efficiency by virtue of eliminating the need for retraining, but often entails decreased accuracy [Hasan (2024)] and is typically unsuitable for lower-bit quantization [Lee et al. (2025)]. Thus, QAT is the alternative to preserve predictive performance in more agressive quantization regimes, with the straight-through estimator (STE) being a critical mechanism for QAT. 

1.1 The Straight-Through Estimator (STE) 

To integrate discrete quantization operations into first-order optimization, Bengio et al. (2013) introduced STE. In ultra-low-bit regimes (e.g., INT4 or INT2), this gradient mismatch can be particularly pathological. Because the true landscape is a discontinuous step function (cf. (Nagel et al., 2021)), the continuous surrogate updates frequently drive latent weights across quantization thresholds without a corresponding smooth change in loss. This misalignment leads to noisy stochastic updates, severe parameter oscillations across decision boundaries, and a significant degradation in optimization stability [Nagel et al. (2022); Malinovskii et al. (2024)], which underlies the need for optimization methods capable of accounting for these trajectory drifts. This is the primary motivation of our proposed method, the quantization-aware minimal-norm optimizer (Q-MINO). 

1.2 Outline & Summary of Contributions 

To address the trajectory drifts and optimization instabilities observed with STE-based QAT, we introduce Q-MINO. Q-MINO stabilizes low-bit optimization by maintaining a temporal bundle of recent surrogate gradients and quantized states, solving a drift-regularized minimal-norm subproblem to compute reliable parameter search directions. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Section 3, we analyze Q-MINO the-oretically within a stochastic Lyapunov Kurdyka–Łojasiewicz (KL) framework. In Section 4, we evaluate Q-MINO via a suite of numerical experiments. ## 3 Theoretical Analysis 

We analyze the theoretical convergence of Q-MINO. Because the true quantized objective f (Q(x)) is non-smooth and discontinuous due to discrete state jumps, first-order methods relying on surrogate STE gra-dients do not optimize f (Q(x)) directly. Instead, we analyze the surrogate objective landscape under mild non-convex smoothness assumptions, modeling the discrepancy between surrogate and true gradients as a bounded approximation error inside a stochastic Lyapunov framework. 

3.1 Assumptions & Mathematical Preliminaries 

We evaluate the parameter sequence {xk}k≥0 generated by Q-MINO on the composite surrogate objective function Φ( x) = f (x) + r(x), where f : Rd → R represents the continuously differentiable loss function whose gradient is approximated via EMA surrogate updates ¯ gk(xk), and r : Rd → R ∪ { +∞} is a proper, lower semicontinuous, and convex regularizer. Because the quantization operator Q(·) is a non-differentiable step function, f (Q(x)) lacks global gradient information. 85 Discussion 

Here, we introduced Q-MINO, a temporal bundle method designed to stabilize Quantization-Aware Training (QAT) in lower-bit QAT regimes . By unifying gradient consensus, quantized state-drift regularization, and a warm-started Frank-Wolfe subproblem solver, Q-MINO effectively acts as a steering stabilizer that handles the parameter oscillations and gradient mismatch inherent to QAT optimizers with STE alone. 

5.1 Practical Guidelines & Hyperparameter Sensitivity 

Our empirical evaluations on synthetic optimization landscapes and vision tasks highlight key operational guidelines for deploying Q-MINO: • Bundle Horizon ( m): Setting the bundle history size to m ∈ [4 , 8] provides sufficient spatial-temporal coverage to smooth subgradient trajectories without incurring excessive matrix-vector multiplication overhead in the subproblem. • Drift Regularization ( λ): A mild penalty coefficient ( λ ≈ 0.05) effectively penalizes large param-eter jump discontinuities across quantization bin boundaries without restricting the latent weight updates required for escaping local minima. • Frank-Wolfe Computational Efficiency ( TFW ): Because the Frank-Wolfe sub-routine is warm-started using the fallback vertex eM , running as few as TFW = {3, 5} Frank-Wolfe iterations appears sufficient to reach a stable descent direction dk.

5.2 Limitations & Algorithmic Overhead 

It should be noted that Q-MINO introduces specific computational and memory trade-offs: • Memory Overhead: Storing Gk, z k ∈ Rd×M requires caching 2 M auxiliary (parameter) vectors, which is likely to be computationally expensive at large-scale, as this memory footprint grows linearly with parameter size (e.g., when scaled to billion-parameter models). • Per-Step Latency: Deploying the Frank-Wolfe sub-routine at every iteration introduces a small computational overhead per parameter update step not required in our baselines (e.g., SGD+STE ,

Adam+STE ). 

5.3 Future Work 

Future directions include developing block-wise or layer-group bundle updates to lower the memory profile for ultra-large architectures (e.g., LLM fine-tuning), developing dynamical schedules for drift coefficient λk

based on local subgradient variance across training epochs, and extending Q-MINO to extreme 1-bit/binary neural networks (BNNs).
