# ABSTRACT 

Post-training quantization (PTQ) methods typically quantize a pre-trained large language model (LLM) sequentially using a small set of calibration samples, achieving high data and computational efficiency. Specifically, the model is parti-tioned into a series of units, such as transformer blocks, with one unit quantized at each stage. State-of-the-art PTQ methods are predominantly learning-based, optimizing auxiliary quantization parameters (e.g., scaling factors, rotation matri-ces, clipping thresholds, and adapters) via gradient descent to minimize a recon-struction loss, thereby mitigating the impact of outliers and reducing quantization errors. In learning-based PTQ, a common practice is to use mean squared error (MSE) as the reconstruction loss function, yet its induced optimization behav-ior remains largely unexplored. In this work, we take a holistic view of sequen-tial quantization and systematically investigate how optimization evolves from the first quantization stage to the last, aiming for a deep understanding of optimiza-tion in learning-based PTQ schemes. Through extensive empirical studies span-ning representative learning-based PTQ methods, model families, model scales, architectures, and quantization settings, we consistently uncover Optimization Im-balance : reconstruction loss magnitudes vary dramatically across stages, accom-panied by highly uneven gradient magnitudes and parameter updates under MSE. We term the cross-stage range of loss magnitudes the reconstruction loss scale .Our analysis reveals that MSE translates the unexpectedly large reconstruction loss scale into highly uneven gradient magnitudes, which in turn lead to uneven optimization strength across quantization stages. This finding suggests a general principle for improving learning-based PTQ: optimization strength across stages should be decoupled from the reconstruction loss scale. Theoretically, we show that root mean squared error (RMSE) naturally realizes this principle through im-plicit gradient normalization, thereby enabling balanced optimization across quan-tization stages. Moreover, RMSE can be naturally defined over different feature groupings, yielding four variants at the sample, channel, token, and element lev-els. As a drop-in replacement for MSE, RMSE consistently improves quantiza-tion performance across language modeling, commonsense reasoning, mathemat-ical reasoning, and coding tasks, with all four variants outperforming MSE and the gains becoming increasingly pronounced under more aggressive quantization. Code will be available at https://github.com/IntelChina-AI/RMSE .

To efficiently quantize LLMs, PTQ meth-ods generally partition the model into a sequence of units (e.g., transformer blocks) and quantize them in a stage-wise manner, processing one unit at each stage while keeping previously quantized units fixed. We refer to this popular stage-wise PTQ paradigm as sequential quantization .Under sequential quantization, existing PTQ methods differ primarily in how they perform feature (i.e., output) reconstruction to quantize each unit. Many methods employ carefully designed analyti-cal or statistics-driven schemes for effective reconstruction, including second-order error compensa-tion, activation-aware scaling, equivalent transformations, and fixed rotations. Representative meth-ods include GPTQ (Frantar et al., 2023), AWQ (Lin et al., 2024b), SmoothQuant (Xiao et al., 2023), QuaRot (Ashkboos et al., 2024), and others (Li et al., 2025; Sanjeet et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026). By contrast, learning-based PTQ brings gradient descent into the feature reconstruction process without full-parameter retraining. At each quantization stage, the pre-trained weights remain frozen, while only a small set of auxiliary quantization parameters is optimized through feature re-construction. This learning-based concept is adopted by most top-performing PTQ methods, which primarily differ in their learnable parameterizations and reconstruction schemes. OmniQuant (Shao et al., 2024) learns scaling factors and clipping thresholds, DuQuant (Lin et al., 2024a) optimizes orthogonal rotations and permutations, FlatQuant (Sun et al., 2025) learns affine transformations, and SliderQuant (Wang et al., 2026) combines scaling and low-rank adaptation with sliding-window reconstruction. Other recent methods further explore related learnable design choices (Liu et al., 2025; Ding et al., 2025; van Breugel et al., 2026; Liang et al., 2026). Despite differences in their formulations, we observe that nearly all learning-based PTQ methods use mean squared error (MSE) as the reconstruction loss function, with the exception of LRQuant (Zhao et al., 2024), which aug-ments MSE with a cosine-similarity-based term in its proposed learnable smoothing strategy to address outliers. Although learning-based PTQ has advanced considerably, the optimization behav-2ior induced by MSE throughout sequential quantization remains largely unexplored. Additionally, sequential quantization implicitly assumes that every unit receives sufficient optimization, yet this assumption has not been systematically examined. Motivated by these two largely overlooked perspectives, we take a holistic view of the sequen-tial learning-based PTQ process, tracking how reconstruction losses, gradients, and parameter up-dates evolve from the first quantization stage to the last. Our empirical studies cover representative learning-based PTQ methods (OmniQuant, DuQuant, FlatQuant, and SliderQuant), model families (LLaMA2, LLaMA3.1, and Qwen3.5), model scales (7B, 8B, 9B, 27B, and 35B-A3B), architec-tures (dense and MoE), and quantization settings (W4A16, W3A16, W2A16, W2A16G128, and W4A4). Across this broad testing spectrum, we consistently uncover Optimization Imbalance , as illustrated in Fig. 1: reconstruction loss magnitudes span an unexpectedly large cross-stage range, which we term the reconstruction loss scale , with the largest often exceeding the smallest by more than 10 ,000 ×. Under MSE, this broad loss scale is accompanied by highly uneven gradients and parameter updates across quantization stages. This imbalance is consequential in sequential quanti-zation because quantization errors propagate and accumulate across stages, meaning that insufficient optimization at one stage can negatively affect the quantization of all subsequent units. Our analysis identifies MSE as the fundamental source of Optimization Imbalance, revealing that it inherently couples optimization strength with reconstruction loss magnitude. Specifically, MSE translates the unexpectedly large reconstruction loss scale into large differences in output-space gra-dient magnitude, which are further reflected in parameter-space gradients and ultimately manifest as highly uneven parameter updates across quantization stages. This loss-dependent coupling di-rectly explains the observed Optimization Imbalance and suggests a general principle for improving learning-based PTQ: optimization strength across quantization stages should be decoupled from the reconstruction loss scale. This principle naturally leads to root mean squared error (RMSE). As a square-root transformation of MSE, RMSE preserves the gradient direction and relative magnitudes among gradient elements while implicitly normalizing the overall output-space gradient magnitude to a constant. RMSE therefore breaks the loss-dependent coupling of MSE and enables more bal-anced optimization across quantization stages. RMSE can be naturally defined over different feature groupings, yielding a unified family of four variants operating at the sample, channel, token, and element levels. These variants preserve the same cross-stage balancing property while applying gradient normalization within different feature groups at each stage. Extensive experiments demonstrate the generality and practical benefits of replacing MSE with RMSE in learning-based PTQ: (1) RMSE consistently improves quantization performance in lan-guage modeling, commonsense reasoning, mathematical reasoning, and coding tasks across repre-sentative learning-based PTQ methods, model families, model scales, architectures, and quantiza-tion settings, with all four RMSE variants outperforming MSE; (2) the gains generally become more pronounced under aggressive quantization, with absolute average accuracy improvements ranging from 5.44% to 10 .83% under W2A16 and from 0.75% to 8.05% under W4A4; and (3) as a drop-in replacement for MSE, RMSE requires no additional trainable parameters or hyperparameters while introducing negligible runtime and memory overhead. More importantly, our analysis and exper-imental results establish Optimization Imbalance as a previously overlooked phenomenon arising from the loss-dependent gradient scaling of MSE in sequential quantization and highlight optimiza-tion behavior as a fundamental design consideration for learning-based PTQ.

While sequential quantization processes one unit at a time, it implicitly assumes that each unit re-ceives sufficient optimization. But is this really the case? To answer this question, we systematically investigate optimization dynamics across units throughout the sequential quantization process, cov-ering representative learning-based PTQ methods, model families, model scales, architectures, and quantization settings. A consistent pattern emerges across this broad spectrum: reconstruction loss magnitudes span a broad cross-stage range, which we term the reconstruction loss scale , even though all units are optimized using the same MSE loss function. In many cases, the largest loss magnitude exceeds the smallest by more than 10 ,000 ×. Fig. 1 presents a representative example, while com-prehensive statistics are provided in Sec. 4.2 and Appendix D. This broad loss scale arises naturally during sequential quantization. As quantization proceeds through the network, errors introduced by previously quantized units accumulate, making subsequent units increasingly difficult to recon-struct. Meanwhile, reconstruction losses are computed directly over unit outputs, whose magnitudes can vary substantially across units. Together, these factors give rise to the broad reconstruction loss scale observed across stages. This broad loss scale raises another question: do units with substantially different loss magnitudes receive comparable optimization strength? We quantify this using the relative parameter update

The relative parameter update measures how strongly a unit is modified during reconstruction and therefore serves as a practical proxy for the optimization strength received by that unit. We observe that the relative parameter update is consistently much larger for units with larger loss magnitudes, whereas units with smaller loss magnitudes receive only minimal updates. The close correspondence between loss magnitudes and relative parameter updates reveals a systematic imbalance in optimization across units, which we term Optimization Imbalance . This imbalance is especially critical in sequential quantization, where each quantized unit remains fixed, so the errors left by an under-optimized unit propagate and accumulate through all subsequent units. # 4 EXPERIMENTS 

4.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Models. For evaluation, we select five representative LLMs spanning different model families, model scales, and architectures (dense and MoE): LLaMA2-7B and LLaMA3.1-8B from the LLaMA family (Touvron et al., 2023; Grattafiori et al., 2024), and Qwen3.5-9B, Qwen3.5-27B, and Qwen3.5-35B-A3B from the Qwen3.5 family (Qwen Team, 2026). Among them, LLaMA2-7B and Qwen3.5-9B serve as our primary evaluation models, representing a widely adopted PTQ benchmark and a modern hybrid linear-attention architecture, respectively. 

Quantization Methods. We evaluate four representative learning-based PTQ methods spanning diverse learnable quantization designs: scaling factors and clipping thresholds in OmniQuant (Shao et al., 2024), orthogonal rotations and permutations in DuQuant (Lin et al., 2024a), affine trans-formations in FlatQuant (Sun et al., 2025), and scaling, low-rank adaptation, and sliding-window reconstruction in SliderQuant (Wang et al., 2026). Despite their substantially different designs, all four methods rely on MSE-based feature reconstruction. This diversity allows us to examine whether Optimization Imbalance arises from the shared loss function rather than any particular quantization design, and whether RMSE generalizes across different learning-based PTQ methods. Following their official implementations, each unit corresponds to a transformer block, except for SliderQuant, which reconstructs a sliding window of consecutive blocks. 

Quantization Settings and Implementation Details. We evaluate five quantization settings span-ning weight-only and weight-activation quantization: W4A16, W3A16, W2A16, W2A16G128 (group size 128), and W4A4. Unless otherwise specified, we follow the official implementations and hyperparameter settings of each method and add support for model families not covered by the official code. For fair comparison, all results are obtained from our own reproductions, en-abling a unified analysis of optimization behavior across different methods. We evaluate all meth-ods using a unified LM-Evaluation-Harness (Gao et al., 2024) pipeline, reporting WikiText2 (Merity et al., 2017) perplexity (PPL) and zero-shot accuracy on PIQA (Bisk et al., 2020), ARC-Easy and ARC-Challenge (Clark et al., 2018), Winogrande (Sakaguchi et al., 2021), HellaSwag (Zellers et al., 2019), and BoolQ (Clark et al., 2019). Unless otherwise specified, we use RMSE-Token for LLaMA models and RMSE-Channel for Qwen models based on their overall empirical performance. More implementation details are provided in Appendix A.

We first test this hypothesis across model families, model scales, ar-chitectures, and quantization settings. Perplexity Across Quantization Depth. To further connect optimization behavior with model per-formance, we record the perplexity of partially quantized models throughout sequential quantization. As shown in Fig. 4, RMSE consistently maintains lower perplexity than MSE, with the gap generally widening as quantization progresses. This trend highlights the increasing importance of balanced optimization as quantization errors accumulate. 

Computational Overhead. As shown in Table 5, RMSE introduces negligible runtime and memory overhead as a drop-in replacement for MSE, enabling more balanced optimization while preserving computational efficiency. 95 DISCUSSION 

Existing learning-based PTQ research has primarily focused on developing advanced quantization designs. Our work suggests a complementary perspective: the choice of loss function itself funda-mentally shapes optimization behavior across stages during sequential quantization. The consistent benefits of RMSE, particularly under aggressive quantization, indicate that Optimization Imbal-ance is not a minor artifact but a systematic phenomenon arising from the loss-dependent gradient scaling of MSE during sequential quantization. Its impact becomes increasingly pronounced as reconstruction becomes more difficult. This issue is particularly consequential in sequential quanti-zation, where insufficient optimization at an early stage may influence all subsequent quantization stages. Furthermore, the distinct behavior of different feature grouping strategies indicates that fea-ture grouping constitutes a meaningful design dimension, opening a broader space for balanced optimization beyond the variants studied here. Taken together, these findings suggest that optimiza-tion behavior should be treated as a fundamental design consideration in future learning-based PTQ research, alongside quantization design. 

# 6 CONCLUSION 

We identify Optimization Imbalance as a previously overlooked phenomenon arising from MSE-based reconstruction during sequential quantization in learning-based PTQ, where optimization strength across quantization stages is coupled with the reconstruction loss scale. To address this, we propose a family of RMSE loss functions that naturally decouple these two quantities and enable balanced optimization across quantization stages. These loss functions serve as drop-in replace-ments for MSE. Extensive experiments across learning-based PTQ methods, model families, model scales, architectures, and quantization settings demonstrate consistent improvements in quantiza-tion performance across language modeling, commonsense reasoning, mathematical reasoning, and coding tasks, with the gains becoming increasingly pronounced under more aggressive quantization.

We follow the official implementations and hyperparameter settings of each PTQ method. For a fair comparison, RMSE is used as a drop-in replacement for the original MSE loss function, while all other components, including trainable parameters, optimizer, learning rate, reconstruction steps, and quantization configurations, are kept unchanged. # F RECONSTRUCTION LOSS MAGNITUDE VARIATION WITHIN STAGES 

To examine whether reconstruction loss magnitudes also vary within each unit, we analyze Omni-Quant models trained with MSE and compute the group-wise MSE between the high-precision and quantized unit outputs using the four feature groupings introduced in Sec. 3.2. As shown in Fig. 9, reconstruction loss magnitudes vary substantially across samples, with increasingly dispersed distri-butions under the channel, token, and element groupings.
