## ABSTRACT 

Trellis-coded quantization enables high-dimensional compression of large lan-guage model (LLM) weights at ultra-low bit widths without the exponentially large codebooks required by conventional vector quantization. Practical deployment, however, presents two challenges: reconstructing compressed weights at sufficient parallel throughput to avoid making dequantization an inference bottleneck, and maintaining quantization accuracy without costly incoherence transformations. We address these challenges with two complementary techniques. First, we introduce an ultra-low-complexity trellis dequantizer that uses a structured, hardware-efficient state-to-value mapping while preserving diverse reconstruction choices for trellis search. Second, we reformulate discrete trellis path optimization with a curvature-aware objective that reflects model sensitivity directly in the original coordinate space. Together, these techniques enable high-quality ultra-low-bit trellis quantiza-tion with inexpensive, highly parallel runtime reconstruction and without relying on Hadamard-based incoherence processing. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Large language models (LLMs) impose substantial storage and memory-bandwidth costs during inference, making weight-only post-training quantization (PTQ) particularly important for autore-gressive decoding. At extreme bit widths, however, maintaining accuracy becomes difficult. Vector quantization (VQ) improves representational efficiency by jointly quantizing multiple weights, but conventional VQ codebooks grow exponentially with vector dimension. Trellis-coded quantization (TCQ) avoids this growth by coupling quantization decisions through a finite-state path while storing only low-bit transitions, enabling effective dimensions far beyond conventional VQ (Tseng et al., 2024b). High-dimensional trellis quantization creates an important systems challenge: compressed repre-sentations must be converted back into numerical weights as they are consumed during inference. Although ultra-low-bit quantization substantially reduces weight-memory traffic, realizing this band-width advantage requires dequantization to keep pace with the parallel weight-consumption rate of modern GPUs, NPUs, vector CPUs, and other accelerators; otherwise, per-weight reconstruction arithmetic can become a throughput bottleneck and offset part of the memory-bandwidth benefit of compression. This is particularly challenging for computed trellis dequantization: for example, QTIP’s 3INST dequantizer requires arithmetic, logical, and data-manipulation operations for each reconstructed weight, which can require substantial execution bandwidth on programmable processors or replicated datapath resources in custom accelerators. Our objective is therefore to make trellis dequantization sufficiently simple and parallelizable to scale with accelerator weight-consumption bandwidth, without sacrificing the quantization accuracy enabled by high-dimensional trellis coding. This hardware–accuracy co-design objective motivates our first contribution. A second challenge arises from incoherence processing used in a number of state-of-the-art ultra-low-bit quantization methods. Such methods often apply randomized Hadamard transforms (RHTs),

This issue arises not only in trellis-coded quantization, but more broadly in structured high-dimensional quantization approaches whose effectiveness can depend on the geometry of the input representation. QTIP, for example, uses RHT-based incoherence processing to make weights and important Hessian directions more incoherent and thereby improve trellis quantization quality (Tseng et al., 2024b). Rotation-based methods such as QuaRot and SpinQuant similarly show that rotations can suppress outliers and improve low-bit quantization, while non-absorbable rotations can introduce additional inference-time overhead (Ashkboos et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025). Recent LLVQ results show that structured high-dimensional quantization can reduce dependence on Hadamard preprocessing (van der Ouderaa et al., 2026), motivating the question of whether trellis quantization can similarly achieve accurate quantization in the original coordinate space and eliminate the need for Hadamard transformations in the inference path. We find that sensitivity to RHT need not be intrinsic to the trellis representation, but can instead arise in part from a mismatch between model-aware quantization and discrete reconstruction search. Second-order PTQ weights errors according to their predicted model impact, whereas conventional trellis path selection uses isotropic Euclidean distortion. RHT can partly mask this mismatch by making sensitivities more uniform, so that Euclidean distance becomes a better surrogate for the model-aware objective. Recent work such as YAQA similarly highlights the importance of aligning quantization decisions with appropriate Hessian information (Tseng et al., 2026). This observation motivates our second contribution: curvature-aware trellis path optimization in the original, unrotated coordinate space. Rather than transforming the representation to make an isotropic search objective more appropriate, we make the search itself reflect the nonuniform model sensitivity exposed by second-order quantization. We derive an additive curvature-aware trellis path-search objective from the residual sensitivity structure of the LDL factorization, directing reconstruction choices toward errors that are more consequential to the model. The method operates entirely offline and requires no change to the compressed representation or runtime dequantizer. These observations lead to two complementary contributions: • Ultra-low-complexity trellis dequantization. We introduce an XOR-Trellis FP4 dequantizer whose state-to-value mapping is jointly designed for quantization quality and hardware efficiency. Reconstruction uses shallow parity/XOR operations and a small fixed FP4 palette lookup rather than a large state-to-value table or expensive computed function. The resulting structure scales to wide parallel dequantization so that dequantization throughput can track the weight-consumption bandwidth of the target processor, while structured local palettes preserve the reconstruction diversity required for accurate trellis search. • Curvature-aware rotation-free trellis optimization. We identify a fundamental mismatch be-tween the model-aware objective of second-order quantization and the isotropic distortion used for discrete trellis path selection. We introduce a curvature-aware Viterbi objective that uses the residual sensitivity structure exposed by the LDL factorization, directly accounting for nonuniform sensitivity in the original coordinate space. This enables accurate rotation-free trellis quantization without changing the compressed representation or increasing runtime dequantization complexity. On Llama-3.1-8B, the resulting curvature-aware XOR-Trellis method achieves a WikiText-2 per-plexity of 8.00 directly in the original coordinate space, substantially improving over conventional unrotated trellis quantization ( 8.69 ) and outperforming the RHT-enabled QTIP baseline ( 8.48 ). 

## 2 BACKGROUND AND MOTIVATION 

2.1 FROM VECTOR QUANTIZATION TO TRELLIS QUANTIZATION 

Scalar quantization independently maps each weight to a reconstruction level, whereas VQ maps a vector w ∈ Rd to a codeword c, improving shaping efficiency by balancing errors jointly across dimensions. However, maintaining a fixed bitrate as d increases requires exponentially more code-words. Trellis-coded quantization (TCQ) instead represents a sequence of quantization decisions as a path through a finite-state trellis. At each step, the current state and a low-bit branch determine both a reconstruction value and the subsequent state. Quantization therefore searches candidate paths, 2typically using the Viterbi algorithm, to find a sequence of reconstruction values that minimizes a distortion objective. Coupling consecutive reconstruction decisions through the trellis state provides high-dimensional shaping without explicitly storing a high-dimensional VQ codebook. QTIP applies TCQ to ultra-low-bit LLM weight quantization using a bitshift trellis and state-dependent reconstruction mapping (Tseng et al., 2024b). We denote by K the number of transition bits per trellis step, giving 2K possible branches. For the K = 2 trellis considered here, each step therefore selects one of four two-bit branches. A finite state retains recent branch history, so the reconstruction choices for the current weight depend on earlier decisions. Our example uses a 16-bit state comprising 14 retained history bits and the current two-bit branch. Each selected branch enters the history as the oldest bits are discarded, causing the state to act as a sliding memory of recent quantization decisions. 2.2 LDLQ AND HESSIAN INFORMATION 

Second-order PTQ methods use an approximate Hessian H to describe how sensitive the model is to quantization errors (Frantar et al., 2022; Chee et al., 2024). LDLQ-style quantization factorizes 

H = LDL ⊤, (1) which separates this sensitivity information into two useful components. The triangular factor L

captures how quantization errors in different weight dimensions interact and is already used by LDLQ for sequential error feedback and compensation. The diagonal factor D describes the relative importance of the remaining errors after these interactions have been accounted for. Thus, a large Dii 

indicates that an error in dimension i is more costly to the model than an equal-magnitude error in a dimension with a smaller Dii .2.3 WHY ROTATIONS HELP —AND WHY WE WANT TO REMOVE THEM 

RHT-based incoherence processing mixes coordinates so that weight outliers and sensitivity are spread more uniformly, improving the fit of constrained low-bit quantizers (Tseng et al., 2024b). QuaRot and SpinQuant similarly demonstrate the value of rotations for suppressing outliers in LLM quantization (Ashkboos et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025). Although weight rotations can be prepared offline, rotations that cannot be absorbed into surrounding operations require runtime transformation and introduce additional inference-time overhead. As motivated above, RHT can also make Euclidean trellis path distortion a better surrogate for model-aware quantization by reducing sensitivity nonuniformity. Section 4 develops our alternative: making the path-search objective explicitly sensitivity-aware rather than transforming the representation, thereby avoiding online RHT processing and its associated runtime overhead, which can otherwise limit LLM inference throughput. Further related work is discussed in Appendix A. 

## 3 XOR-T RELLIS FP4 T RELLIS DECODER 

3.1 DESIGN OBJECTIVE : ACCURATE TRELLIS SEARCH WITH INEXPENSIVE RECONSTRUCTION 

Trellis-coded quantization couples reconstruction decisions across a sequence through a finite-state path. Unlike scalar quantization, the reconstruction choices available for the current weight depend on preceding quantization decisions. This path dependence provides some of the high-dimensional shaping benefit of vector quantization without explicitly materializing an exponentially large high-dimensional codebook. The same property creates a runtime reconstruction problem. Each compressed transition must be combined with the current trellis state to determine the corresponding numerical weight. A large state-to-value lookup is unattractive, while computed-code approaches replace storage with arithmetic that is exercised repeatedly over large LLM weight tensors. We therefore seek a dequantizer (generator) whose state-to-value mapping is simultaneously expressive enough to preserve accurate trellis search 

and simple and parallelizable enough for high-throughput runtime reconstruction .33.2 TRELLIS STATE AND LOCAL RECONSTRUCTION PALETTES 

For the K = 2 bitshift trellis considered here, two bits encode one of four branches at each step. The trellis state acts as a finite memory of recent branch decisions: after each weight is quantized, its two-bit transition is incorporated into the sliding state while the oldest transition bits are discarded. In the demonstrated configuration, the state is 16 bits, comprising a retained 14-bit history h and a current two-bit branch b ∈ { 0, 1, 2, 3}. This history couples neighboring quantization decisions and determines which reconstruction choices are locally available. For a fixed history h, the four branches define a local reconstruction palette : the four values Viterbi can choose at that search step. This conditional four-way set is more informative than the generator’s global output histogram alone. For example, a local palette [−2, −2, 2, 2] nominally exposes four branches but only two distinct reconstructions, wasting half of the local representational freedom. We therefore design the generator around the geometry of these conditional palettes. In this sense, the generator behaves like a tiny state-dependent codebook whose contents change with trellis history. 3.3 XOR-T RELLIS MAPPING 

In the FP4 embodiment evaluated in this work, we use four reconstruction palettes: 

P0 = [ −6, −1.5, 0, 2] , P1 = [ −4, −1, 0.5, 3] ,P2 = [ −3, −0.5, 1, 4] , P3 = [ −2, 0, 1.5, 6] .

Every palette contains four numerically distinct values spanning negative, near-zero, and positive regions, with minimum spacing 1.5. Consequently, each of the 214 = 16 ,384 retained histories exposes four distinct, well-separated reconstruction choices. These palettes provide one concrete realization of the proposed state-dependent reconstruction mapping; the dequantizer itself is not restricted to FP4 or to four-bit reconstruction values and can be instantiated with other numerical formats and corresponding reconstruction palettes 1.The palette construction is not merely heuristic. They also achieve the maximum possible minimum within-palette numerical separation of 1.5. Appendix C.2 states these results formally and provides the proofs. We next examine how effectively the XOR-Trellis mapping utilizes the information retained in the trellis state. In exhaustive analysis of the demonstrated XOR-Trellis construction, flipping any evaluated state bit changes the decoded numerical value, indicating that all state bits participate meaningfully in the demonstrated mapping. In addition, signed and absolute-value linear correlations between a reconstruction and the reconstructions reachable by successor transitions are essentially zero. These empirical diagnostics complement the algebraic analysis in Appendix C.2, where rank conditions characterize marginal, adjacent-transition, and multi-step balance of the XOR mapping. Decorrelation alone is not sufficient for good quantization, nor do the rank conditions establish global optimality; together with distinct local palettes, however, they provide complementary evidence that the trellis state is used to create diverse reconstruction trajectories. 3.6 HARDWARE REALIZATION 

A 16-bit state nominally defines 216 = 65 ,536 possible state-dependent reconstruction outcomes, but Equation 2 does not require a correspondingly large state-to-value lookup. Instead, the mapping factorizes into four shallow masked-parity reductions, two XOR operations for branch permutation, and a small fixed 4×4 FP4 palette lookup. In the evaluated gate-level realization, the pre-lookup logic requires approximately 14 XOR2 gates. Thus, high-dimensional trellis coupling can be paired with an extremely small per-weight dequantization datapath. This property is important for deployment. The shallow XOR-dominated structure is amenable to wide parallel implementation, allowing many compressed transitions to be reconstructed concurrently into FP4 weights immediately before or 5adjacent to the arithmetic datapath. This allows dequantization throughput to scale with the parallel weight-consumption rate of modern neural-network accelerators while retaining a compact, area- and energy-efficient reconstruction datapath.

RHT and curvature-aware path search address a common difficulty from different directions: RHT : change the representation so an isotropic path metric is more appropriate ,

Ours : retain the representation and make the path metric reflect its curvature .

RHT mixes the coordinate system before quantization, spreading large weights and sensitive directions and making the transformed problem more homogeneous. In that regime, an unweighted Euclidean metric can be an effective approximation. Without RHT, residual curvature can be strongly anisotropic, and equal weighting can mis-rank candidate trellis paths relative to the curvature-aware objective. This interpretation yields a concrete prediction. If D = αI for some α > 0, then curvature-aware and Euclidean Viterbi have exactly the same minimizers because their objectives differ only by a positive constant. More generally, Appendix D shows that the quality of the Euclidean surrogate under the diagonal-curvature objective is controlled by the diagonal anisotropy κD = Dmax /D min .The approximation is tightest near isotropy and can weaken as the spread of D grows. ## 5 EXPERIMENTS 

We evaluate weight-only trellis quantization using the XOR-Trellis FP4 decoder at approximately 2.25 effective bits per weight, including one E5M3 scale per group of 32 weights 2. Unless otherwise specified, experiments use L/K/V = 16 /2/1, trellis tiles Tx = Ty = 16 , 384 RedPajama calibration sequences, seed 0, and no layer-wise or end-to-end fine-tuning. Context length is model-specific and reported with each result. We compare RHT and no-RHT configurations, including Hessian-aware Viterbi weighting and energy-ordered LDLQ variants where indicated. We evaluated both perplexity and zero-shot accuracy on quantized Llama, Mistral and Qwen models. We compare our method against the state-of-the-art low-bit quantization methods QTIP Tseng et al. (2024b) and YAQA Tseng et al. (2026), as well as the state-of-the-art rotation-free quantization method LLVQ van der Ouderaa et al. (2026). For QTIP and YAQA, we reproduce the results using their publicly available code and Hessians under the same non-finetuning setting as our method. 5.1 COMPARISON WITH QTIP In this section, we compare our rotation-free method with QTIP across multiple model families and scales. We present the Llama-3.1 results in the main text in Table 1, covering Llama-3.1-8B, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, and Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct. Across all three models, directly removing the Hadamard transform degrades XOR-Trellis; for example, on Llama-3.1-8B, WT2 perplexity increases from 8.10 with RHT to 8.69 without RHT, indicating that groupwise scaling alone does not fully compensate for the loss of rotation. Overall, these results show that curvature-aware trellis search can recover much of the performance lost when RHT is removed, with energy ordering providing additional gains in several settings. Ultra-low-bit weight compression provides its full bandwidth benefit only when dequantization can keep pace with the parallel weight-consumption rate of the compute engine. The arithmetic reconstruction used by QTIP-3INST is estimated to require the equivalent of approximately twelve 32-bit add/subtract operations, an additional 32-bit addition, mask/XOR logic, and an FP16 addition per reconstruction, corresponding to roughly 3–5k small logic-gate equivalents depending on the FP16-adder implementation. XOR-Trellis instead reduces the state-to-value mapping to approximately 14 XOR2 (2-bit XOR) gates and a tiny fixed 4 × 4 palette lookup, while achieving better perplexity in our matched evaluation. Moreover, a 512-weight-per-cycle implementation of the XOR-Trellis dequantizer occupies only 1,917 μm2 when synthesized using N3P standard cells, demonstrating that the reconstruction function can be replicated at the wide parallelism required to keep pace with high-throughput weight consumption. 96 CONCLUSION 

We presented XOR-Trellis, a hardware/algorithm co-design for ultra-low-bit LLM weight quantization that enables efficient runtime dequantization without Hadamard-based incoherence processing. The XOR-Trellis dequantizer combines shallow parity/XOR logic with structured local reconstruction palettes to provide highly parallel, ultra-low-complexity reconstruction while preserving effective trellis search choices. On the quantization side, we reformulate discrete trellis path optimization with a curvature-aware objective that reflects model sensitivity in the original coordinate space rather than transforming the representation for effective Euclidean path selection. Together, these techniques enable high-dimensional ultra-low-bit trellis quantization with accurate quantization, inexpensive high-throughput dequantization, and Hadamard-free deployment.

Extreme low-bit PTQ. GPTQ uses approximate second-order information for one-shot weight quantization (Frantar et al., 2022). QuIP combines scalar adaptive rounding with incoherence processing (Chee et al., 2024), while QuIP# introduces randomized Hadamard transforms and E8

lattice vector quantization (Tseng et al., 2024a). AQLM represents weight vectors as sums of learned codewords, achieving strong accuracy at very low bitrates (Egiazarian et al., 2024). AQLM uses additive multi-codebook quantization for extreme LLM compression (Egiazarian et al., 2024), while VPTQ combines vector quantization with second-order optimization for extreme-low-bit PTQ (Liu et al., 2024). PV-Tuning further improves extreme compression through representation-agnostic optimization of compressed parameters (Malinovskii et al., 2024); related second-order model-compression methods include LLM Surgeon (van der Ouderaa et al., 2024b). 

Structured codebooks and trellis quantization. PVQ uses a pyramid lattice to encode vector directions without storing an explicit codebook (van der Ouderaa et al., 2024a). LLVQ uses the 24-dimensional Leech lattice, combining algebraic indexing and angular search with parallel dequan-tization (van der Ouderaa et al., 2026). Classical trellis-coded quantization (TCQ) and its vector extension couple reconstruction decisions through trellis paths and optimize additive distortion using Viterbi search (Marcellin & Fischer, 1990; Fischer et al., 1991). QTIP adapts trellis quantization to LLMs using incoherence processing, a bitshift trellis supporting parallel reconstruction, and computed or hybrid lookup codes (Tseng et al., 2024b). Our work builds on this trellis framework, modifying the reconstruction mapping and the path-selection metric. 

Curvature-aware objectives and adaptive rounding. GPTQ and LDLQ use second-order informa-tion to compensate quantization errors through sequential weight updates (Frantar et al., 2022; Chee et al., 2024). GPTVQ uses curvature-weighted distances for codebook fitting and vector assignment (van Baalen et al., 2024). GuidedQuant incorporates end-loss gradients into layerwise objectives and demonstrates improvements with QTIP (Kim et al., 2025). YAQA develops Kronecker-factored Hessian sketches and adaptive rounding with both input- and output-side feedback to better preserve end-to-end model behavior (Tseng et al., 2026). Our trellis search retains the underlying Hessian approximation and error-feedback framework, and uses the diagonal entries of the block-diagonal D

factor to weight the additive Viterbi distortion. This diagonal approximation incorporates residual curvature into path selection while preserving the trellis state space and inference-time reconstruction. 

Rotations and Hadamard-free quantization. QuaRot uses randomized Hadamard rotations to reduce outliers while preserving the full-precision model function, enabling quantization of weights, activations, and the KV cache (Ashkboos et al., 2024). SpinQuant learns rotations to improve quantized model accuracy, distinguishing rotations absorbed into weights from optional online Hadamard transforms (Liu et al., 2025). For weight-only PTQ, LLVQ reports competitive Hadamard-free results using shape–gain quantization with norm-preserving GPTQ corrections, interpreted as Spherical GPTQ (van der Ouderaa et al., 2026). Our experiments investigate Hadamard-free weight-only trellis quantization through the combination of the XOR-Trellis reconstruction mapping and curvature-aware path selection. 

Efficient reconstruction and low-bit formats. SeedLM reconstructs weight blocks from pseu-dorandom matrices generated from stored seeds and a small set of coefficients, providing another approach to computed reconstruction (Shafipour et al., 2025). The OCP microscaling specification combines low-bit elements with shared block scales; standard MXFP4 uses FP4 (E2M1) elements and an E8M0 scale per block of 32 elements (Open Compute Project, 2023) Our XOR-Trellis generator maps trellis-state bits to E2M1 payloads using XOR logic and a small lookup, targeting inexpensive fixed-function reconstruction. The evaluated representation pairs these payloads with E5M3 block scales, so it shares the FP4 element format with MXFP4 but uses a different scale format. We focus on group-wise FP4 because of its native hardware support and high low-precision matrix-multiplication throughput in current and next-generation accelerators, including GPUs. NVIDIA Blackwell provides hardware-accelerated FP4 Tensor Core support, while the emerging Rubin software stack extends block-scaled FP4 GEMM support with E5M3 scale factors (NVIDIA, 2025; 2026). Motivated by this broader hardware trend toward fine-grained FP4 scaling, our evaluation uses group-wise FP4 reconstruction with E5M3 scale factors. 12 B ADDITIONAL RESULTS

## C THEORY OF THE XOR-T RELLIS GENERATION HASH 

This section gives a more formal view of the finite-state generation function used in Section 3. The central observation is that a trellis generator has three logically distinct design objects: (i) the global reproduction law , which controls how frequently numerical symbols occur over the generated state space; (ii) the local palette geometry , which controls which reconstruction values are simultaneously available from a shared history; and (iii) the state/hash topology , which controls how palettes and branch labels evolve along a path. Matching the first object alone is therefore insufficient: two generators can have the same aggregate reconstruction histogram but expose different local menus to Viterbi or connect those menus with different temporal structure. This distinction is classical in spirit— trellis source coding is a finite-state minimum-distortion path problem (Gray, 1977; Marcellin & Fischer, 1990; Fischer et al., 1991)—but it is particularly useful for understanding why the structured palettes in Section 3 matter. A practical design procedure is therefore: preserve the desired symbol inventory, construct locally diverse palettes, reject XOR masks with short-window rank defects, and choose among the surviving masks using held-out full-path distortion at the actual trellis memory and coding horizon. C.4 IMPLICATIONS FOR THE XOR-T RELLIS DEQUANTIZER DESIGN 

The theory suggests a hierarchy that mirrors the empirical design in Section 3. First choose the source/distortion and a useful global reproduction inventory; next partition that inventory into locally useful palettes; then assign palettes and branch permutations to states with a cheap topology that passes structural rank checks; finally evaluate full Viterbi distortion under the actual block length, boundary rule, scales, and Hessian-aware metric. The key consequence for this paper is that the XOR-Trellis generator is not justified by its aggregate FP4 histogram alone. Its four palettes have an exact one-step covering property under explicit assumptions, while the XOR hash provides a low-cost topology whose obvious degeneracies can be screened algebraically. Full finite-horizon performance remains an operational property and is evaluated empirically.

## E MECHANISTIC ANALYSIS OF HESSIAN ANISOTROPY 

This section studies why diagonal-D-weighted Viterbi decoding is especially effective when random-ized Hadamard transforms (RHTs) are disabled. Two related effects arise in the unrotated domain. First, groupwise scaling remains sensitive to within-group outliers, which can force most weights into a narrow region around zero. Second, the local Hessian metric can be strongly anisotropic, so equal Euclidean branch-cost weighting does not reflect the unequal importance of different coordinates. RHT can mitigate both effects by redistributing weight magnitudes and making the local metric more isotropic, whereas our method retains the original basis and incorporates the diagonal of the block metric directly into the Viterbi branch cost. E.1 WHY GROUPWISE SCALING DOES NOT ELIMINATE THE NEED FOR RHT Groupwise scaling mitigates dynamic-range variation, but it does not fully remove the difficulty caused by outliers in the unrotated domain. A group may contain one or more weights whose magnitudes are substantially larger than those of most other weights in the same group. Because a 18 shared scale must accommodate these large values within the available FP4 reconstruction range, the outliers can dominate the scale selection. As a result, the majority of smaller-magnitude weights are compressed into a relatively narrow region around zero. In our analysis of RHT-sensitive matrices, a substantial fraction of the quantization error is con-centrated in this near-zero region. This suggests that groupwise scaling alone does not eliminate the conditioning problem addressed by RHT: it controls the range within each group, but does not redistribute outlier energy across coordinates. RHT can reduce this imbalance by mixing coordinates before quantization, while the rotation-free method must instead remain robust to the resulting non-uniform geometry in the original basis. This effect is complementary to the Hessian anisotropy studied below. Scale distortion describes how the reconstruction grid is used within a group, whereas diagonal-D anisotropy describes how costly errors are along different coordinates. The two mechanisms therefore act at different parts of the quantization problem. E.2 DEFINITIONS AND PROTOCOL

The matrix-level mechanism is consistent with the matched WikiText-2 result: no-RHT diagonal-D

decoding achieves perplexity 8.06145, compared with 8.10438 for legacy RHT, 8.68979 for legacy no-RHT, and 8.19951 for RHT with diagonal-D weighting. These endpoints show that explicitly 20 representing anisotropy can recover the accuracy lost when RHT is unavailable. They also show that diagonal weighting is not automatically beneficial after RHT: once the basis has changed, dense within-block structure can matter more than its diagonal approximation. This analysis should not be read as evidence that preserved correlation is an advantage of no-RHT quantization. Correlation that remains outside the diagonal model is a residual source of error. Moreover, matrix-level proxy correlations do not establish causality, the post-hoc diagnostics were selected after observing the primary analysis, and a single scalar anisotropy statistic cannot characterize the full block metric. The supported conclusion is narrower: robust diagonal anisotropy is a strong, statistically stable predictor of both legacy RHT benefit and no-RHT diagonal-weighting benefit, but it is not a complete predictor of the best quantization method for every matrix.
