## Abstract 

Matrix multiplications dominate the inference cost of modern transformer-based vision models, yet existing efficiency techniques such as post-training quantization and mixed-precision inference are largely limited to the small set of fixed-width formats—INT4, INT8, BF16, and FP16—supported by conventional accelerators. We revisit stochastic computing (SC) as a way to lift this constraint: viewed as a 

dense adaptive quantizer , SC controls precision by bit-stream length L rather than a fixed datapath, while each multiplication reduces to a single AND/XNOR gate. We build a GPU library that emulates SC matrix multiplication at scale, exposes stream lengths as first-class kernel arguments, and evaluates SC end-to-end on image classification, object detection and instance segmentation, class-conditional image generation, and visual world-model planning. On top of this substrate, we develop a dynamic per-row mixed-precision policy that assigns stream length per token or group at matched average budget, requires no retraining, and uses the same SC hardware across schedules. Across tasks, SC remains competitive with fixed-format INT quantization at matched bit budgets, while per-row mixed precision helps maintain accuracy at lower average stream lengths. These results provide software-level feasibility evidence that SC can serve as a dense-precision substrate for fine-grained mixed-precision inference on modern vision transformers. 

## 1 Introduction 

The computational footprint of modern neural networks has grown faster than the efficiency gains provided by conventional hardware scaling. Across vision, language, generation, and world-modeling workloads [Dosovitskiy et al., 2020, Brown et al., 2020, Touvron et al., 2023, Peebles and Xie, 2023, Ha and Schmidhuber, 2018, Hafner et al., 2023], matrix multiplications dominate inference cost, complicating deployment in latency- and energy-constrained settings, including edge devices, real-time control systems, and power-limited datacenters. The dominant response has been low-bit arithmetic that trades efficiency at the cost of accuracy. Post-training quantization (PTQ) maps weights and activations to INT8, INT4, or lower precision, exploiting the empirically observed robustness of trained networks to numerical perturbations [Nagel et al., 2021, Dettmers et al., 2022, Frantar et al., 2023, Xiao et al., 2023]. Mixed-precision methods extend this idea by assigning different bit-widths to different operators, layers, or tensors, thereby recovering accuracy in cases where a uniform low-precision format is too restrictive [Wang et al., 2019, Dong et al., 2019, Rouhani et al., 2023, Chen et al., 2025b]. Conventional mixed precision, however, is constrained by hardware: each supported precision typically requires a distinct datapath or mode, so deployed accelerators expose only a small set of

formats, such as INT4, INT8, BF16, and FP16. This sparse, hardware-defined precision ladder limits the granularity of precision, thereby constraining the effectiveness of mixed-precision inference. Stochastic computing (SC) provides a fundamentally different precision substrate: precision is determined by execution time rather than by a fixed hardware format. In SC, a real value is represented by the ratio of 1s in a stochastic bit stream [Gaines, 1969, Alaghi and Hayes, 2013]. Multiplication of two such streams reduces to a single bitwise AND gate in the unipolar case or XNOR gate in the bipolar case. SC has three properties well-suited to neural-network inference: (i) Area and energy efficiency. Amulti-bit multiplier is replaced by a single logic gate, enabling substantial reductions in area and energy relative to integer or floating-point MACs [Alaghi and Hayes, 2013, Sim et al., 2017, Wu and San Miguel, 2022a, Guo et al., 2023a, Zhong et al., 2022, Weller et al., 2021] (ii) Dense, hardware-shared precision. SC precision is governed by the bit-stream length L, which can be any positive integer rather than only a conventional word size. The same hardware can compute at different precisions by terminating streams at different lengths, allowing a single accelerator to support a dense set of precision choices and fine-grained mixed-precision schedules. (iii) Inherent error tolerance. 

A bit flip in an SC stream perturbs the decoded value by only 1/L , whereas an error in a high-order bit of an integer or floating-point word can induce a much larger deviation [Alaghi et al., 2018, Qian et al., 2011]. This property is particularly relevant for unreliable computing environments, including space computing. Despite these advantages, prior work on SC for deep learning has remained limited in scope. Most studies evaluate small CNNs or simplified transformer models [Ren et al., 2017, Sim et al., 2017, Wu and San Miguel, 2022a, Guo et al., 2023a]. To our knowledge, no prior work has performed an end-to-end evaluation of SC across modern transformer-based AI workloads or quantitatively demonstrated how its dense precision substrate can be used for fine-grained mixed-precision inference. This work addresses that gap by treating SC as a dense adaptive quantizer . We show that this perspective enables competitive accuracy with substantially reduced compute across four families of modern models. Our contributions are: (i) A scalable GPU library for SC simulation. We present, to our knowledge, the first GPU implementation that can effectively emulate SC matrix multiplication at scale. The library implements bit-stream generation, AND/XNOR-based multiplication, and counter-based decoding as fused Triton kernels, and exposes per-row and per-group bit-stream lengths for algorithmic exploration. (ii) An end-to-end evaluation across vision-centric transformer workloads. We evaluate SC inference on standard vision tasks—classification, detection, and instance segmentation—as well as diffusion-based image generation and learned world-model rollouts. To our knowledge, this is the first end-to-end study showing that an SC-based compute pipeline can preserve task-level performance across various transformer-based vision workloads. (iii) A fine-grained mixed-precision algorithm. We introduce a runtime mixed-precision scheme that assigns a separate bit-stream length to each row (token or group) in every matrix multiplication, guided by lightweight operator-specific saliency metrics. The GPU kernel is available at https://github.com/CrucibleComputingGroup/scmp_ kernels .

## 2 Related Works 

2.1 Stochastic Computing 

Stochastic computing (SC) [Gaines, 1969] encodes real values as the density of 1s in probabilistic bit streams, reducing multi-bit multiplication to a single AND/XNOR gate and realizing addition, division, and common activations with small finite-state machines; classical applications include LDPC decoding and image processing [Alaghi and Hayes, 2013]. For neural networks [Liu et al., 2021], SC-DCNN [Ren et al., 2017] and the SC multiplier of Sim and Lee [Sim et al., 2017] scale SC to medium-sized CNNs while preserving accuracy, and recent hardware substrates such as uSystolic [Wu and San Miguel, 2022a] and Cambricon-U [Guo et al., 2023a] push SC closer to modern accelerator scale. 22.2 Post-training Quantization and Mixed Precision 

Post-training quantization (PTQ) compresses pretrained networks by mapping weights and activations to low-bit formats without retraining [Nagel et al., 2021]. For LLMs, OPTQ [Frantar et al., 2023], LLM.int8() [Dettmers et al., 2022], SmoothQuant [Xiao et al., 2023], and AWQ [Lin et al., 2024] enable low-bit transformer inference by addressing weight quantization and activation outliers. For vision and generative transformers, PTQ4ViT [Yuan et al., 2022], Q-Diffusion [Li et al., 2023a], and Q-DiT [Chen et al., 2025a] adapt PTQ to ViTs, diffusion models, and Diffusion Transformers. Mixed-precision methods further assign different precisions to different layers, operators, or tensors. HAQ [Wang et al., 2019], HAWQ-V3 [Yao et al., 2021], and BRECQ [Li et al., 2021] search for sensitivity-aware bit-width assignments, while MX formats [Rouhani et al., 2023], MPQ-DM [Feng et al., 2025], and MixDiT [Kim et al., 2025a] specialize mixed precision for modern accelerators and generative workloads. 

## 3 Preliminaries and Problem Formulations 

3.1 Stochastic Computing Basics Encoding. Stochastic computing (SC) represents a real value by the probability that each bit of a single-bit binary sequence is 1 [Gaines, 1969, Alaghi and Hayes, 2013]. A unipolar stream encodes 

v ∈ [0 , 1] by a length-L sequence Z = ( z1, . . . , z L) ∈ { 0, 1}L in which each bit is 1 with probability 

v. A bipolar stream encodes v ∈ [−1, 1] with Pr[ zℓ = 1] = ( v + 1) /2. Since neural-network activations and weights are signed, we use bipolar encoding throughout. 

Bitstream generation. Stochastic number generation (SNG) is implemented in hardware as an integer comparator. At each cycle ℓ, a pseudo-random number generator (RNG) draws a uniform integer rℓ from the same b-bit range as the input, and the comparator emits zℓ = 1 if rℓ is below the input value, else zℓ = 0 (Figure 1(a)). 4.1 Hardware Model 

SC replaces a multi-bit multiplier with a one-bit gate (AND for unipolar, XNOR for bipolar) and pays precision temporally: a length-L stream runs the same datapath for L cycles. 

Gate-level advantage. 4.2 GPU Kernel for SC Matrix Multiplication GPU execution path. Existing CPU SC simulators are too slow to insert into every transformer matmul, while the C-BSG datapath is a regular tiled AND-and-popcount computation that fits GPU execution. We implement the uSystolic-style C-BSG temporal-unary multiplier from Section 3.1 as fused Triton kernels Tillet et al. [2019]; in our benchmark, this path is about 95 ,000 × faster than direct CPU SC simulation. 4.4 Per-Row Mixed-Precision SC Calibration mode. Section 4.2 exposes stream length as a per-row runtime argument, so mixed precision reduces to choosing the stream length assigned to each output row of each SC matmul. We make this choice through an offline calibration mode : for each target average stream length ¯L, the calibrator runs a small set of FP-teacher trajectories, replays each selected matmul with uniform SC at several candidate stream lengths, and records the row-wise deviation. Inference only evaluates the same lightweight row metrics, looks up the calibrated thresholds, and passes the resulting row-length vector to the SC kernel.

## 6 Discussion 

Limitations. Our evaluation covers only a limited set of configurations: for each task we use representative backbones and a small number of precision budgets, rather than an exhaustive sweep over models, seeds, and more aggressive low-bit settings. Thus, our results establish SC’s feasibility in the evaluated regimes, but do not yet show how robust the conclusions are across a broader configuration space. In addition, the hardware benefits are estimated from analytical and gate-level models rather than measured end-to-end silicon. 

Broader impacts. This work supports broader exploration of SC for modern AI inference. Since specialized hardware requires substantial design effort, software-level feasibility evidence is important for deciding which arithmetic paradigms are worth pursuing in silicon. Our results suggest that dense, temporally controlled SC precision is a promising direction for future accelerator design. 97 Conclusion 

This paper argues that stochastic computing is best viewed as a dense adaptive quantizer for modern transformer inference: precision is controlled by stream length rather than constrained to a small set of fixed word sizes. Empirically, we show that SC retains near-lossless accuracy on transformer-based vision tasks—image classification, object detection and instance segmentation, class-conditional image generation, and visual world-model planning. To make this concrete at scale, we contribute a fused GPU library for SC matrix multiplication on modern vision transformers and a per-row mixed-precision algorithm that turns row-level sensitivity into a calibrated stream-length budget without retraining. Together, these results position SC as a viable substrate for future adaptive-precision accelerators, where a simple shared datapath can expose a much denser precision space than conventional fixed-format arithmetic.

Pr[ zout = 1] = Pr[ zA = 1 , z B = 1] + Pr[ zA = 0 , z B = 0] = Pr[ zA = 1] Pr[ zB = 1 | zA = 1] + Pr[ zA = 0] Pr[ zB = 0 | zA = 0] , (5) which matches (4) only when both conditional probabilities equal the corresponding marginal: 

Pr[ zB = 1 | zA = 1] = Pr[ zB = 1] , Pr[ zB = 0 | zA = 0] = Pr[ zB = 0] . (6) 

Conditional bitstream generation (C-BSG) [Wu et al., 2020, Wu and San Miguel, 2022a] enforces (6) by construction (Figure 1(c)): rather than generating ZA and ZB from two independent RNGs, the second stream is produced by an RNG whose update is gated by the first stream, so that the conditional distribution of zB given the value of zA is forced to equal its marginal. Substituting (6) into the bit-pair counts above yields ad − bc = 0 and hence SCC = 0 , so the XNOR-based bipolar multiplier becomes bias-free at finite L rather than stochastic.

This is the property that lets the discussion of Section 5.1 treat compensation as a pure precision/accuracy lever rather than as a hybrid INT–FP correction. 

Deterministic SC noise lets Wb absorb a fixed realization. The C-BSG kernel of Section 4.2 is stochastic in name only: its randomness is a length-Lmax Sobol low-discrepancy sequence [Sobol’, 1967, Joe and Kuo, 2008] indexed by a configuration (dim, seed, polarity) that is fixed at model load and reused on every forward pass. Consequently the per-element SC error \⟨w, x ⟩ − ⟨ w, x ⟩ is a deterministic function of (w, x ) for a given configuration—it is “shot noise” only across hypothetical Sobol seeds, not across forward passes. We exploit this by using the same Sobol configuration during compensation calibration and during inference, so the residual Yb − Y Zb that Wb is fit against is exactly the residual that Wb encounters at test time. In effect, Wb does not need to “average out” SC noise; it absorbs a fixed deterministic perturbation, and any structure in that perturbation that is approximately linear in x is fully captured by the closed-form fit. This mirrors the interpretation Fu et al. [2025] give for INT-side QwT: where the INT residual is a deterministic, scale-induced rounding map, the SC residual is a deterministic, Sobol-induced kernel-evaluation map, and OLS is well-posed against either. 

Scope: feasibility, not formal analysis. We frame this section strictly as a feasibility demonstration: a closed-form fit, a cross-seed admission gate, and an SC-realized compensator together form a plausible recipe, and the empirical results of Section 5.1 (within 1 pt of FP at ¯L≥192 , within 3.2 pt at ¯L=128 ) suggest the recipe is sound at the budgets we evaluate. We do not establish here (i) that the deterministic-Sobol fixed-point characterization of Wb is unique or stable across changes of 15 Sobol configuration, (ii) conditions under which the cross-seed cosine gate is the optimal admission criterion versus, e.g., a residual-norm threshold, or (iii) the regime in which an SC-realized Wb at 

Lcomp <256 remains accurate enough to keep the cumulative compensation profitable.
